1(Q2 in the original paper) Money & coinsAnswer D

A public telephone has been vandalised and will take only 50 pence coins and 10 pence coins1. A woman puts £8.502 into the phone to make a long-distance call with twenty-five coins2. Five 10 pence coins are returned3 when she finishes her call.

What combination of coins is retained4 by the machine?

  1. ATwice as many 10s as 50s.
  2. BOne-and-a-half times as many 50s as 10s.
  3. CThe same number of 50s and 10s.
  4. DThree times as many 50s as 10s.
  5. EOne-and-a-half times as many 10s as 50s.

Reading it properly

  • 1Only two kinds of coin, so it is a “how many of each?” puzzle.
  • 2Two facts to use together: 25 coins and £8.50 in total.
  • 3The five returned coins are all 10p. Take them off after you find the mix.
  • 4“Retained” = kept by the machine, so after the 5 coins come back.

Working it out

  1. Pretend all 25 coins were 10p, then swap some for 50p coins:

    25 × 10p = £2.50 short by £8.50 − £2.50 = £6.00
    each swap (10p 50p) adds 40p
    £6.00 40p = 600 40 = 15 swaps

    So she put in 15 × 50p and 10 × 10p.

    check: 15 × 50p + 10 × 10p = £7.50 + £1.00 = £8.50 ✓

  2. Now the machine gives back five 10p coins:

    50s kept: 15
    10s kept: 10 − 5 = 5
    15 5 = 3 three times as many 50s as 10s
DThree times as many 50s as 10s: 15 fifties and 5 tens are kept.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A has it upside down. There are far more 50s than 10s (15 against 5).
  • B✗ 15 fifties to 10 tens is 1.5 times as many: that is what went in. It forgets the five 10s that came back.
  • C✗ Equal piles would need, say, 5 and 5 (£3.00). The machine kept 15 fifties but only 5 tens.
  • E✗ E is the “before” ratio (15 : 10) turned upside down, so it mixes up the coins and forgets the returned 10s.
Tip All 10p = £2.50. Extra £6 40p = 15 fifties, so 10 tens. 5 tens come back: 15 : 5 = 3 : 1.

2(Q6 in the original paper) Timings & recipesAnswer D

William wants to cook roast beef and potatoes for himself and four friends1. From his cookbook he gets the following information:

BEEFMedium ovenHot oven
Rare---10 minutes per 500 g
Medium rare20 minutes per 500 g plus 20 mins15 minutes per 500 g plus 15 minutes4
Well done30 minutes per 500 g plus 30 minutes---

Allow 300 g per person.2

After removing from the oven, the meat should be allowed to stand for 10 minutes3.

ROAST POTATOESMedium ovenHot oven
40 minutes30 minutes

William buys a joint of the correct weight and decides to cook it medium rare in a hot oven4.

How long after putting the beef in the oven should he put in the potatoes, so that both will be ready to serve at the same time5?

  1. A25 minutes
  2. B30 minutes
  3. C31 minutes
  4. D40 minutes
  5. E50 minutes

Reading it properly

  • 1Himself and four friends = 5 people, not 4.
  • 25 × 300 g gives the weight of the joint.
  • 3The beef is not ready to serve until 10 minutes after it leaves the oven.
  • 4Only one cell of the beef table matters: medium rare, hot oven. The potatoes go in the same hot oven.
  • 5Work backwards from serving time.

Working it out

  1. Weight of the joint:

    5 people × 300 g = 1500 g = 3 × 500 g

  2. Beef, medium rare, hot oven:

    3 × 15 min + 15 min = 45 + 15 = 60 min in the oven
    + 10 min standing ready to serve 70 min after it goes in

  3. Potatoes take 30 minutes in the hot oven, so they must finish at 70 minutes too:

    70 − 30 = 40 minutes
D40 minutes: the beef is ready to serve at 70 minutes and the potatoes need the last 30.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 25 minutes forgets the “plus 15 minutes”: 45 + 10 − 30 = 25.
  • B✗ 30 minutes forgets the 10 minutes of standing: 60 − 30 = 30.
  • C✗ 31 minutes forgets to count William himself: 4 × 300 = 1200 g, 2.4 × 15 + 15 = 51, then 51 + 10 − 30 = 31.
  • E✗ 50 minutes uses the medium oven: 3 × 20 + 20 = 80, 80 + 10 − 40 = 50. He chose the hot oven.
Tip 1500 g: 3 × 15 + 15 = 60, + 10 standing = 70, − 30 for the potatoes = 40.

3(Q7 in the original paper) Tables & rulesAnswer E

A teacher is going to award a prize to her most deserving pupil in the current school year. She has narrowed it down to five students and must make her final judgements. She won't give the prize to anyone who has been late to her lessons more than twice1. She won't give it to anyone who has failed to complete more than two pieces of homework by the deadline set2. After these criteria have been met, the prize will be awarded to the student with the fewest non-A-grade pieces of work3.

PupilYear groupNo. of lessons missed4No. of times late for lessonNo. of homeworks setHomeworks completed by deadlineHomeworks with grade A
Andrew841605658
Carole1122565653
Edward962595658
Grace1063585756
Ian11102545352

Which student will be awarded the prize?

  1. AAndrew
  2. BCarole
  3. CEdward
  4. DGrace
  5. EIan

Reading it properly

  • 1“More than twice” = 3 or more lates. Exactly 2 is still fine.
  • 2Not in the table directly! Missed = homeworks set − completed by deadline. 3 or more is out.
  • 3Non-A = homeworks set − grade A. Only compare pupils who survive the two rules.
  • 4Red herring: lessons missed (and year group) are not in any rule.

Working it out

  1. Rule 1: late 3 or more times? Only Grace (3). She is out.

  2. Rule 2: work out missed deadlines = set − completed:

    Andrew 60 − 56 = 4 ✗
    Carole 56 − 56 = 0 ✓
    Edward 59 − 56 = 3 ✗
    Ian 54 − 53 = 1 ✓

  3. Only Carole and Ian are left. Count non-A pieces = set − grade A:

    Carole 3, Ian 2 fewest = Ian
    Trap! Ian missed 10 lessons, the most of anyone. It does not matter: no rule mentions missed lessons.
EIan: he passes both rules and has only 2 non-A pieces (Carole has 3).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Andrew has only 2 non-A pieces, but he missed 4 deadlines, so he is out.
  • B✗ Carole passes both rules but has 3 non-A pieces, one more than Ian.
  • C✗ Edward is the trap: only 1 non-A piece, the best of all, but 59 − 56 = 3 missed deadlines rules him out.
  • D✗ Grace was late 3 times, which is “more than twice”.
Tip Cross out Grace (late 3), Andrew (4 missed) and Edward (3 missed). Non-A: Carole 3, Ian 2. Ian.

4(Q8 in the original paper) Graphs & seasonsAnswer D

Plankton are small plants and animals present in the sea. The animal plankton feed on the plant plankton. The rate at which the plant plankton are produced depends on sea temperature and the amount of sunlight.

In tropical regions there is little variation of plankton production through the year. Variations are much bigger at temperate and polar latitudes1. Plant plankton production always rises before that of animal plankton2.

The following graphs show the production rates of plankton in the northern hemisphere3:

In the northern hemisphere winter is in December, January and February. In the southern hemisphere it is in June, July and August4.

Which one of the following graphs provides a good representation of plankton production in the southern polar region5?

  1. Agraph A
  2. Bgraph B
  3. Cgraph C
  4. Dgraph D
  5. Egraph E

Reading it properly

  • 1Polar = big swings. A nearly flat graph is tropical.
  • 2The plant peak comes first, then the animal peak follows (the animals eat the plants).
  • 3The given graphs are for the north. You must convert to the south.
  • 4Southern seasons are 6 months out of step with the north.
  • 5Polar shape, southern timing.

Working it out

  1. Start from the northern polar graph: plant peak in June (about 60), animal peak in October (about 40).

    The south has its seasons 6 months later, so slide both peaks along by 6 months:

    plant: June + 6 December (high at both ends of the graph)
    animal: October + 6 April

  2. Look for: big swings, plant high around December/January, animal peak around April.

    A: almost flat = tropical ✗
    B: small peaks, plant peak in August = southern winter ✗
    C: exactly the northern graph, not shifted ✗
    E: plant peak in August, animal peak first ✗
    D: plant high in Dec and Jan, animal peak in April ✓ D
DGraph D: the northern polar pattern moved on by 6 months.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is almost flat, like the tropical graph. Polar regions have big swings.
  • B✗ B’s plant peak is in August, the middle of the southern winter, and its peaks are only temperate-sized.
  • C✗ C is the northern polar graph copied out. It hasn’t been shifted by 6 months.
  • E✗ E puts the plant peak in August (southern winter), and the animal peak comes before it, but plant production always rises first.
Tip South = north + 6 months. Northern polar peaks June (plant) and October (animal) become December and April: only D.

5(Q12 in the original paper) Tables & percentagesAnswer B

A market research company conducted a survey into the relative popularity of two soft drinks with the following results (all figures are percentages1):

Age 5-15Age 15-25Age 25-35Age over 35
Prefer Drink A28383224
Prefer Drink B20313319
No preference33122234
Don't know19191323

The same number of people were surveyed in each age group2.

Which one of the following conclusions can be justifiably drawn3 from the results?

  1. ADrink A is consistently more popular than Drink B.
  2. BThere were more people with a preference for Drink B than with no preference.
  3. CMore people in the age group 5-15 expressed a preference than did not.
  4. DFewer people had a preference than those who did not.
  5. EMore than 20% of people did not know which they preferred.

Reading it properly

  • 1Each column is out of 100.
  • 2Equal group sizes, so you can simply add the percentages across a row (total out of 400).
  • 3Must be definitely true from the numbers. Test each option.

Working it out

  1. Add each row across the four groups (each group counts equally):

    Prefer A: 28 + 38 + 32 + 24 = 122
    Prefer B: 20 + 31 + 33 + 19 = 103
    No preference: 33 + 12 + 22 + 34 = 101
    Don't know: 19 + 19 + 13 + 23 = 74
    check: 122 + 103 + 101 + 74 = 400 ✓
  2. A: age 25-35 has B 33 > A 32, so not consistent ✗
    B: Drink B 103 > no preference 101 ✓
    C: 5-15: preference 28 + 20 = 48, not 33 + 19 = 52 ✗
    D: preference 225, no preference or unsure 175 ✗
    E: don't know 74 out of 400 = 18.5% ✗
    answer = B
BB: 103 points for Drink B against 101 for no preference (out of 400).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Not consistent: in the 25-35 group Drink B (33) beats Drink A (32).
  • C✗ In the 5-15 group, 48% had a preference but 52% did not (33 + 19).
  • D✗ The other way round: 225 out of 400 had a preference, only 175 did not.
  • E✗ 74 out of 400 is 18.5%, under 20%. Two columns show 19 and one 23, which tempts you, but the average is below 20.
Tip Same group sizes, so add rows: B 103, no preference 101. B is true. Quick-check E: 74 4 = 18.5%, under 20.

6(Q13 in the original paper) Bills & tariffsAnswer D

My monthly electricity bill is calculated as a standing charge plus an amount per unit used1. At the beginning of one month my electricity company decided to change the tariff by reducing the standing charge and increasing the cost per unit2. This was intended to help customers who use less electricity.

The table below shows my consumption and monthly charges for the whole year.

MonthUnits usedCharge (£)MonthUnits usedCharge (£)
Jan12542.50Jul6036.00
Feb15045.00Aug7037.00
Mar13043.00Sep8036.00
Apr11041.00Oct10040.00
May9539.50Nov11042.00
June7537.50Dec13046.00

At the beginning of which month3 was the tariff changed?

  1. AFebruary
  2. BApril
  3. CAugust
  4. DSeptember
  5. EDecember

Reading it properly

  • 1Bill = fixed amount + (price per unit × units). Two months on the same tariff give both numbers.
  • 2After the change: smaller fixed amount, bigger price per unit.
  • 3Find the first month that does not fit the old tariff.

Working it out

  1. Find the old tariff from January and February:

    25 more units cost £2.50 more 10p a unit
    standing charge = £42.50 − 125 × 10p = £42.50 − £12.50 = £30

  2. Test each month with £30 + 10p a unit:

    Mar 30 + 13 = 43 ✓ · Apr 30 + 11 = 41 ✓ · May 30 + 9.50 = 39.50 ✓
    Jun 30 + 7.50 = 37.50 ✓ · Jul 30 + 6 = 36 ✓ · Aug 30 + 7 = 37 ✓
    Sep 30 + 8 = 38, but the bill is £36 ✗ changed in September

  3. Check with the new tariff from October and November: 10 more units cost £2 more, so 20p a unit and a standing charge of £40 − £20 = £20.

    Sep 20 + 16 = 36 ✓ · Dec 20 + 26 = 46 ✓
    answer = September
    Trap! October (100 units, £40) fits both tariffs, because the two lines cross at 100 units. That is why you must test month by month.
DSeptember: it is the first month that the old tariff (£30 + 10p a unit) does not fit.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ February fits the old tariff: 30 + 15 = £45.
  • B✗ April fits the old tariff: 30 + 11 = £41.
  • C✗ August fits the old tariff: 30 + 7 = £37.
  • E✗ December is on the new tariff, but so are September to November. The change came earlier.
Tip Jan and Feb give £30 + 10p a unit. Run down the months: the first that fails is September (should be £38, is £36).

7(Q14 in the original paper) Interpreting dataAnswer E

A survey has shown the following relationships between parental occupation, type of school and educational success at GCSE level:

Parental occupation1Average no. of GCSEs at Grade C or above
Professional7.3
Managerial7.2
Routine office work6.1
Skilled manual3.3
Semi-skilled manual2.4
Unskilled manual1.62
Type of school1Average no. of GCSEs at Grade C or above
Independent (i.e. private)7.2
Specialist maintained schools7.0
Local authority comprehensive4.3
Local authority girls4.4
Local authority boys3.8

Which one of the following can be inferred3 from these tables?

  1. AAll children of managerial-class parents attend independent schools.
  2. BThe majority of children whose parents are office workers attend independent schools.
  3. CSchool type is more important than parental occupation in determining educational success.
  4. DThe parental background of children in independent schools is more restricted than that of children in comprehensive schools.
  5. EFactors other than type of school attended may be important in determining educational success.

Reading it properly

  • 1Two separate tables. Neither says which children go to which school.
  • 2Results swing hugely with parents’ jobs: from 7.3 down to 1.6.
  • 3Must follow from the tables alone. Cautious words like “may” are easier to support.

Working it out

  1. The tables give averages for groups of children, but the two groupings are never linked. You cannot tell whose parents are managers and at independent schools.

  2. Parental occupation alone goes with very different results (7.3 against 1.6). So something other than school type may matter. That is all E claims ✓

  3. Every other option needs a link between the two tables, or a comparison of the two factors:

    A, B, D: need to know who goes to which school ✗
    C: says which factor matters more, which the tables cannot separate ✗
    answer = E
EE: parental occupation also lines up with results, so other factors may matter.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ “All” is far too strong, and the tables never say where managers’ children go to school. Two averages of about 7.2 being similar proves nothing.
  • B✗ Nothing links office workers to any school type. (Their 6.1 average is not the independent 7.2 anyway.)
  • C✗ The tables can’t separate the two factors, because the same children appear in both. If anything, parental occupation shows the bigger spread.
  • D✗ There is no information about the backgrounds of children in each type of school.
Tip Two unlinked tables: rule out anything about who attends which school (A, B, D) or which factor wins (C). E only says “may”.

8(Q18 in the original paper) Choosing a methodAnswer C

There are 420 candidates1 sitting a public exam in a college. The sports hall can accommodate 210 students1; once this has been filled there are other rooms available that are much smaller. The following is a list of the number of single desks that have been set up in each room:

26, 20, 24, 28, 24, 20, 24, 16, 20, 18, 262.

The rooms have sufficient space for all the candidates who are not in the sports hall. The exams officer wants to leave the same number of empty desks in each of these rooms3.

What procedure4 should the exams officer adopt to work out how many students to have in each room?

  1. ADivide the total number of candidates by the total number of workspaces in each room and take that number away from the capacity of each of the rooms.
  2. BAdd up the total capacity of the rooms, subtract that number from the total number of candidates, divide the result by the total number of rooms and then subtract that number from the capacity of each room.
  3. CAdd up the total capacity of the rooms, subtract the total number of candidates from that figure, divide the result by the total number of rooms and subtract that number from the capacity of each room.
  4. DDivide the total capacity by the number of students and then take that number away from the capacity of each room.
  5. EAdd up the total capacity of the rooms and divide by the total number of candidates. Multiply the result by the total number of rooms, which gives the number of students to place in each room.

Reading it properly

  • 1The sports hall takes 210, so 420 − 210 = 210 go into the small rooms.
  • 211 rooms. Total capacity 246.
  • 3Equal empty desks, not equal students. Students in a room = its capacity − empty desks.
  • 4They want the method, so do it with the numbers, then match the steps.

Working it out

  1. Do it with the real numbers first:

    total desks: 26 + 20 + 24 + 28 + 24 + 20 + 24 + 16 + 20 + 18 + 26 = 246
    candidates for these rooms: 420 − 210 = 210
    empty desks overall: 246 − 210 = 36 capacity minus candidates
    empty desks per room: 36 11 divide by the number of rooms
    students in a room = its capacity − that number

  2. That is exactly option C, step for step: add capacities, subtract candidates, divide by rooms, subtract from each room’s capacity.

    answer = C
    Check 36 11 isn’t a whole number (about 3.3), so in practice you’d leave about 3 empty desks per room. The question only asks for the method. “Candidates” here means the 210 not in the sports hall.
CC: (total capacity − candidates) number of rooms gives the empty desks per room; subtract from each capacity.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Candidates desks (210 246 ≈ 0.85) is a fraction, not a number of desks. Taking 0.85 away from a room size is meaningless.
  • B✗ B subtracts the wrong way round: 210 − 246 = −36. Taking away a negative would add desks.
  • D✗ Capacity students (246 210 ≈ 1.2) is a ratio, not a count of empty desks.
  • E✗ E gives the same number of students in every room (246 210 × 11 ≈ 13), which can’t leave equal empty desks in rooms of different sizes.
Tip Empty desks per room = (capacity − candidates) rooms; students = capacity − that. Only C says it in that order.

  3. Check the landmarks on each chart:

    Jan–Mar: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6
    Apr–Jun: 3 + 2 + 2 = 7
    Jul–Sep: 1 + 1 + 3 = 5
    Oct–Dec: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6
    year: 24 needed, 2 already in stock, so 22 to deliver

  2. The catch: April–June needs 7,000 but a delivery can only be 6,000. So 1,000 must be left over from January.

    January: need 6 + 1 spare = 7, have 2 deliver 5,000
    April: 1 left + 6 = 7 deliver 6,000
    July: deliver 5,000 · October: deliver 6,000
    total 22,000, nothing left at the end ✓
    answer = A
AA: 5,000, 6,000, 5,000, 6,000, with 1,000 carried from the first quarter into the second.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ B needs a 7,000 delivery in April, over the 6,000 maximum, and over-orders by 2,000.
  • C✗ C delivers exactly what each quarter needs (4, 7, 5, 6), but 7,000 in April breaks the 6,000 limit.
  • D✗ D never runs out but delivers 24,000 on top of the 2,000 in stock: 2,000 over-ordered.
  • E✗ E runs out in June: January leaves nothing spare, and 6,000 can’t cover April–June’s 7,000.
Tip Quarters need 6, 7, 5, 6 (thousand). Max 6 per delivery, so carry 1 from Q1: deliver 5, 6, 5, 6. Only A.

25(Q50 in the original paper) Spatial reasoningAnswer E

Four trees stand at the corners of square field X1. They are photographed from field Y2, from every possible direction, close up and from a distance3, but never so that one tree stands in front of another4.

In how many different orders, left to right5, can the trees appear in the photographs?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C3
  4. D4
  5. E5

Reading it properly

  • 1Trees 1 and 2 are nearest field Y; 4 and 3 are behind them.
  • 2The photographer is always somewhere in field Y, below the square.
  • 3Moving sideways and moving nearer or further can both change the order.
  • 4The order only changes at the exact moment two trees line up. Those lines are the borders between different orders.
  • 5Count the regions of field Y with different orders.

Working it out

  1. Key idea: as you walk around, two trees swap places in the photo only when you cross the straight line through them. So draw every line through two trees and see which ones run into field Y.

    • 1–2 (front row) and 4–3 (back row): run sideways, never enter Y.
    • 1–4 and 2–3 (the sides): run straight down into Y.
    • 3–1 and 4–2 (the diagonals): carry on down into Y, out to the left and right.

  2. Those four lines cut field Y into 5 regions. Each region gives its own order:

    straight in front: 1 4 3 2
    left, close: 4 3 1 2 · left, further back: 4 1 3 2
    right, close: 1 2 4 3 · right, further back: 1 4 2 3
    number of orders = 5
    Check Check the middle: stand in front, between the two side lines. Tree 1 is on your far left, 2 on your far right, and the back trees 4 and 3 appear in between.
E5 orders: one straight in front, two on the left and two on the right.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1 assumes you always see 1 4 3 2, as from straight in front. Walking to the side changes it.
  • B✗ 2 would be just “left side” and “right side”, missing the view from the front and the diagonal swaps.
  • C✗ 3 only uses the side lines (left, middle, right) and forgets the diagonals, which matter “from a distance”.
  • D✗ 4 misses one region, usually the middle view or one of the diagonal swaps.
Tip Order changes only when you cross a line through two trees. Side lines (2) and diagonals (2) enter field Y, cutting it into 5 regions: 5 orders.
