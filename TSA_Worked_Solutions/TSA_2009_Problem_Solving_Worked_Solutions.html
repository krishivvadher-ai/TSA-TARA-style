1(Q2 in the original paper) Nets & volumeAnswer C

A manufacturer wishes to make an open-topped box1 out of the piece of cardboard shown below by folding up its sides2.

What is the volume3 of this box in cubic centimetres?

  1. A1 600
  2. B2 400
  3. C8 000
  4. D10 125
  5. E12 500

Reading it properly

  • 1No lid: the card is just a base plus four walls.
  • 2The 5 cm flaps between the cut corners fold up to make the walls. So the box is 5 cm deep.
  • 3Volume = length × width × height.

Working it out

  1. The card is 50 cm across, but a 5 cm flap folds up on each side:

    50 − 5 − 5 = 40 cm base is 40 cm × 40 cm
    height = the flap = 5 cm
  3. V = 40 × 40 × 5
    = 1600 × 5 = 8000 cm³
    1600 × 5 · short multiplication
    1600
    ×5
    8000
    3
    Tip × 5 is the same as × 10 then halve: 1600 × 10 = 16 000, half is 8000.
C8000 cm³: a 40 cm × 40 cm base, 5 cm deep.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1600 is only the base area (40 × 40). It forgets to multiply by the 5 cm height.
  • B✗ 2400 is the area of the card: 2500 − 4 corners of 25. That is an area, not a volume.
  • D✗ 10 125 = 45 × 45 × 5: takes 5 cm off only one side. There is a flap on both sides.
  • E✗ 12 500 = 50 × 50 × 5 uses the whole card as the base. The flaps fold up, so they are not part of the base.
Tip Base 50 − 2 × 5 = 40 each way, height 5. 40 × 40 × 5 = 8000.

2(Q6 in the original paper) Fuel & ratesAnswer C

The petrol tank of Jenny's car holds 50 litres1 of petrol. A warning light comes on when only 5 litres are left2. She always fills it as soon as she can after the warning light comes on. The car travels 100km on 8 litres of petrol.3 Last time she put petrol in, which she was able to do immediately after the light came on4, she only had $6 with her so she spent it all on fuel at 60c per litre5. She then drove 50km6 and saw a new garage offering petrol at 50c per litre - an offer too good to miss. She filled the tank completely7 and paid with her credit card.

How much did it cost?

  1. A$ 15.50
  2. B$ 17.50
  3. C$ 19.50
  4. D$ 22.50
  5. E$ 24.50

Reading it properly

  • 1Full tank = 50 litres.
  • 2Light on = 5 litres still in the tank.
  • 38 litres per 100 km, so 4 litres per 50 km.
  • 4So the tank still had exactly 5 litres when she bought the $6 of fuel.
  • 5$6 = 600c. At 60c a litre that buys 10 litres.
  • 6The drive burns some fuel: take it off before filling up.
  • 7She pays 50c a litre for the empty space in the tank.

Working it out

  1. Follow the litres in the tank:

    light on: 5 L
    $6 60c = 600 60 = 10 L 5 + 10 = 15 L
    50 km uses half of 8 L = 4 L 15 − 4 = 11 L
  3. space to fill: 50 − 11 = 39 L
    39 × 50c = 1950c = $19.50
    50 − 11 · column subtraction
    54010
    −11
    39
    39 × 50 · short multiplication
    39
    ×5
    195
    14
    Leave out the point and zeros: 39 × 5 = 195. Then put the size back: 39 × 50 = 1950
    Tip × 50c is just halving: 39 2 = 19.5, so $19.50.
C$19.50: 11 litres left in the tank, so 39 litres at 50c.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ $15.50 buys 31 litres. That happens if you add the 4 litres used (5 + 10 + 4 = 19 in the tank).
  • B✗ $17.50 buys 35 litres: forgets the 4 litres burnt on the 50 km drive (tank at 15).
  • D✗ $22.50 buys 45 litres: fills up from the 5-litre warning level, ignoring both the $6 top-up and the drive.
  • E✗ $24.50 buys 49 litres: forgets the 10 litres bought with $6 (5 − 4 = 1 litre left).

4(Q8 in the original paper) Shapes & hingesAnswer D

The diagram below shows a rather unusual conference table which may take up a range of configurations. The three pieces are hinged together at the points shown by the circles1:

Which of the following shapes could NOT be made2 from this table?

  1. Ashape A
  2. Bshape B
  3. Cshape C
  4. Dshape D
  5. Eshape E

Reading it properly

  • 1Two hinges, three pieces. The pieces can swing round but can’t come apart or slide along.
  • 2Odd one out: four of the shapes are possible.

Working it out

  1. Look closely at the pieces. All three are the same trapezium: long edge 4, short edge 2, sloping ends. The hinges sit at the two ends of the middle piece’s long edge, and each side piece is hinged at an end of its long edge.

  2. So in every possible shape, one piece (the middle) has another piece attached at each end of its long edge. Split each option into its three pieces and look for the two hinge points:

  3. A: the long edges make a triangle; hinges at the top corners ✓
    B: one side piece stands up at the right hinge; the other lies against the long edge ✓
    C: both side pieces swung right round to rest on the sloping ends ✓
    E: one piece folds against the long edge, the other swings off at 45° ✓
    D: the top piece only touches the others along its short edge ✗

    In D, nothing meets the ends of the top piece’s long edge, so it has nowhere to be hinged D

DShape D: its top piece touches the rest only along its short edge, so it cannot be hinged on.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Possible: both side pieces swing inwards until their long edges and the middle’s long edge make a triangle.
  • B✗ Possible: the right-hand piece points straight up from its hinge, and the left-hand piece swings round under the middle piece’s long edge.
  • C✗ Possible: it is the original table turned upside down, with both side pieces swung round onto the middle piece’s sloping ends.
  • E✗ Possible: one side piece folds flat against the middle’s long edge; the other hangs from the opposite hinge at 45°.
Tip In every real shape, two pieces meet the two ends of one piece’s long edge. D’s top piece only touches along its short edge, so it can’t be attached.

5(Q12 in the original paper) Speed, distance & timeAnswer B

Ionnais lives at the bottom of a very steep hill. Worse than that, the post office is at the top. Every Thursday he must go there to collect his pension. He can only walk at 2 km per hour uphill1 but manages 4 km per hour downhill2. The round trip3, excluding the wait at the post office, takes him 4½ hours4.

How far is it from his house to the post office5?

  1. A3.00 km
  2. B6.00 km
  3. C6.75 km
  4. D12.00 km
  5. E13.50 km

Reading it properly

  • 1Slow going up: each km takes half an hour.
  • 2Twice as fast coming down: each km takes a quarter of an hour.
  • 3Up AND back down: the same distance twice, at two different speeds.
  • 4Total walking time for both legs.
  • 5One way only, not the round trip.

Working it out

  1. Work out the time for each km of the trip:

    up: 1 km 2 km/h = ½ hour
    down: 1 km 4 km/h = ¼ hour
    1 km there and back = ½ + ¼ = ¾ hour
  2. 4½ hours ¾ hour = 6 distance = 6 km
  3. Trap! Don’t average the speeds! (2 + 4) 2 = 3 km/h is wrong, because he spends twice as long on the slow leg. That trap gives 3 × 4½ = 13.5 km for the round trip, or 6.75 km each way.
B6 km: 3 hours up plus 1½ hours down = 4½ hours.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 3 km would take only 1½ + ¾ = 2¼ hours, half the time given.
  • C✗ 6.75 km comes from averaging the speeds to 3 km/h. He spends longer going slowly, so his real average is lower.
  • D✗ 12 km is the whole round trip (6 + 6), not the distance to the post office.
  • E✗ 13.5 km = 3 km/h × 4½ h: the averaged-speed trap, and it is a round trip too.
Tip Each km costs ½ h up + ¼ h down = ¾ h. 4½ ¾ = 6 km. (Check: 3 h + 1½ h = 4½ h.)

8(Q18 in the original paper) Money & rangesAnswer B

A boy is given $1.00 by his grandparents to buy sweets. He decides to spend at least half1 his money on gobstoppers at 5c each, at least a quarter2 of his money on fruit chews at 3c each and at least one tenth3 of his money on pieces of bubble gum at 2c each. He will decide how to spend the rest of the money when he gets to the shop.

What is the possible range of number of pieces of bubble gum4 he can buy?

  1. AFrom 5 to 9
  2. BFrom 5 to 11
  3. CFrom 5 to 13
  4. DFrom 6 to 12
  5. EFrom 10 to 22

Reading it properly

  • 1At least 50c on gobstoppers: 10 of them at 5c is exactly 50c.
  • 2At least 25c on chews, but chews cost 3c. 8 chews = 24c is not enough: he needs 9 (27c).
  • 3At least 10c on gum: at least 5 pieces.
  • 4Smallest and largest number of gum pieces (pieces, not cents!).

Working it out

  1. Fewest gum: one tenth of 100c is 10c.

    10c 2c = 5 pieces

  2. Most gum: spend as little as allowed on the other two sweets.

    gobstoppers: 50c (10 × 5c, exactly half)
    chews: need 25c, but 8 × 3c = 24c is too little 9 chews = 27c
    100 − 50 − 27 = 23c left
    23c 2c = 11 pieces (22c), with 1c spare
    50 − 27 · column subtraction
    54010
    −27
    23
  3. range from 5 to 11
    Trap! The quarter is the trap: 25 isn’t a multiple of 3, so the least he can spend on chews is 27c. Using 25c gives 25c for gum and 12 pieces.
BFrom 5 to 11 pieces of bubble gum.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 9 comes from spending more than half on gobstoppers (55c), leaving only 18c. Exactly half (50c) is allowed.
  • C✗ 13 pieces cost 26c, which leaves only 24c for chews: less than a quarter.
  • D✗ 12 comes from spending exactly 25c on chews, which is impossible at 3c each. And 5 pieces already make one tenth, so the minimum is 5, not 6.
  • E✗ 10 to 22 are amounts of money in cents (10c to 22c), not numbers of pieces.
Tip Min: 10c 2 = 5. Max: 100 − 50 − 27 (nine 3c chews) = 23c, so 11 pieces.

10(Q20 in the original paper) Pulleys & ratioAnswer A

The diagram shows three pulleys driven by belts. Pulley A and the small, inner pulley at B are 1 m across1, the outer pulley at B is 2 m across2 and pulley C is 4 m across.

Pulley A rotates at a rate of 8 complete turns every second3. If the belts do not slip4, then how many complete turns every second will pulley C make?

  1. A1
  2. B4
  3. C8
  4. D16
  5. E64

Reading it properly

  • 1A and B’s inner wheel are the same size: 1 m.
  • 2Look at the picture: A’s belt goes round B’s outer (2 m) wheel, and C’s belt round B’s inner (1 m) wheel.
  • 3The starting speed.
  • 4No slipping: each belt moves the same length of rim on both pulleys it touches.

Working it out

  1. First check which part of B each belt uses. A’s belt reaches the top and bottom of the big grey ring; C’s belt starts from the small dark wheel in the middle. Both parts of B turn together.

  2. Belt rule: a pulley twice as wide turns half as often. So turns × diameter is the same at both ends of a belt.

    A → B outer: 8 × 1 m = B × 2 m B turns 4 times
    B inner → C: 4 × 1 m = C × 4 m C turns 1 time
  3. Tip Rim length is π × diameter, but π is on both sides, so it cancels. Just use the diameters.
A1 turn a second: A to B halves the speed (8 → 4), B’s small wheel to C quarters it (4 → 1).

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 4 is B’s speed. You stopped one belt too early.
  • C✗ 8 assumes every pulley turns at the same rate, ignoring their sizes.
  • D✗ 16 multiplies by 2 instead of dividing: a bigger pulley turns more slowly, not faster.
  • E✗ 64 = 8 × 2 × 4: multiplies by both size ratios, upside down twice.
Tip Bigger wheel, fewer turns. 8 2 = 4 (A onto B’s 2 m ring), then 4 4 = 1 (B’s 1 m wheel onto C).

12(Q25 in the original paper) Time zonesAnswer C

Julie lives in London. She has three daughters; April, who lives in New York (where the local time is 5 hours behind London1), May, who lives in Vancouver (8 hours behind London2), and June, who lives in Tokyo ( 9 hours ahead of London3). They all communicate with each other regularly.

Last week April received a text message from May that said "call me on Wednesday at 23.30 your time4." Unfortunately, the message had been sent to April by mistake and was intended for June5.

How much later than expected did May's phone6 eventually ring?

  1. A12 hours
  2. B13 hours
  3. C14 hours
  4. D17 hours
  5. E22 hours

Reading it properly

  • 1New York = London − 5 h.
  • 2Vancouver = London − 8 h. That’s May’s home, but you don’t actually need it.
  • 3Tokyo = London + 9 h.
  • 4“Your time” means the reader’s time. June would call at 23.30 Tokyo time; April calls at 23.30 New York time.
  • 5Expected = June’s 23.30. What happened = April’s 23.30.
  • 6Compare the two moments on one clock (London is easiest).

Working it out

  1. Put both moments into London time:

    expected: Wed 23.30 in Tokyo − 9 h Wed 14.30 London
    actual: Wed 23.30 in New York + 5 h Thu 04.30 London
  3. Wed 14.30 → Thu 04.30 = 9½ + 4½ = 14 hours
    Tip Shortcut: the same clock time in two places is as far apart as the zones are. Tokyo is 9 ahead and New York 5 behind, so 9 + 5 = 14 hours. Tokyo reaches 23.30 first, so the call is late.
C14 hours: 23.30 in New York happens 14 hours after 23.30 in Tokyo.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 12 hours doesn’t match any pair of zones. You get it by mixing up gaps, e.g. 9 + 3 (using the 3-hour New York to Vancouver gap).
  • B✗ 13 hours = 5 + 8, the gaps for New York and Vancouver. May lives in Vancouver, but the call times are set in Tokyo and New York.
  • D✗ 17 hours = 8 + 9, the gap between Vancouver and Tokyo. That’s how far apart May and June are, not how late the call was.
  • E✗ 22 hours = 5 + 8 + 9: adds up every gap.

Four triangles of card coloured on one side1 are joined as shown.

The joined triangles are then folded and joined to make a pyramid2.

Which of the following could not be a view from above3?

  1. Aview A
  2. Bview B
  3. Cview C
  4. Dview D
  5. Eview E

Reading it properly

  • 1The coloured side ends up outside, so you see the colours the right way round, never a mirror image.
  • 2Four triangles make a triangular pyramid: every corner has three faces meeting at it.
  • 3From above you see one corner and the three faces around it. Four options are real corners; one is impossible.

Working it out

  1. The pyramid has 4 corners. Three are the points in the middle of each side of the net, where three triangles already meet. The fourth is where the three outer tips come together.

  2. Folding doesn’t flip anything, so going clockwise round a corner you meet the faces in the same order as on the net:

  3. Read each option clockwise (start anywhere):

    A: G → Y → R = Y → R → G ✓ right middle
    B: R → Y → B ✓ bottom middle
    C: B → G → R = G → R → B ✓ left middle
    D: G → B → Y ✓ the tips
    E: R → B → Y ✗ bottom middle backwards
    E is a mirror image, so it can’t be seen E
    Check Why are the tips G → B → Y? Stand the pyramid on R and look down. You are now looking at the net from behind, so left and right swap: G at the top, B on the right, Y on the left. Going clockwise: G, B, Y = view D.
    Tip Faster: B and E use the same three colours in opposite orders, so one of them must be wrong. Check that corner on the net.
EView E: it shows R, B, Y clockwise, the mirror image of the real corner (R, Y, B).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Possible: the corner at the right-hand middle point of the net (G, Y, R clockwise).
  • B✗ Possible: the corner at the bottom middle point of the net (R, Y, B clockwise).
  • C✗ Possible: the corner at the left-hand middle point of the net (G, R, B clockwise).
  • D✗ Possible: the corner where the three tips meet (G, B, Y clockwise), the top when it stands on R.
Tip B and E are mirror images of each other. Round the net’s bottom middle point: R, then Y, then B clockwise. That’s B, so E is impossible.
