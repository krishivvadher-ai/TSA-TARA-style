1(TSA 2014, Q44) Displays & logicAnswer A

Why it's herejudged the 20th-hardest of the 25. You have to count how many bars each floor number uses in this particular display style, which differs from the usual one for some digits. Then you must be careful about which floors are actually possible. You have met this one before: it was in the 2014 paper you sat.

Allan wishes to visit a client on floor 8 of a building with 10 floors (including the ground floor). He enters the lift on the ground floor. His client has explained by email that the floors are indicated by an illuminated matrix but there is a notice stating that two of the lights are not working1.

Allan enters the lift and pushes the button. Just then he starts to sneeze, distracting his attention from the display. The lift leaves the ground floor2, travels, then stops. He glances at the display and concludes he could be on floor 83 so he gets out. In fact he is on the wrong floor.

On how many of the floors could Allan have got out by mistake?

  1. A5
  2. B6
  3. C7
  4. D8
  5. E9

Reading it properly

  • 1Two bars are dead (always off), the same two whatever floor it shows.
  • 2So he is not on the ground floor.
  • 3An 8 needs all 7 bars, so with 2 dead an 8 shows only 5 bars. He saw a 5-bar shape.

Working it out

  1. An 8 lights all 7 bars. With 2 bars dead, floor 8 would show just 5 bars. Another floor looks the same only if it lights exactly those 5 bars, and its 2 unused bars are the dead ones.

  2. Count the bars each floor uses on this display:

    5 bars: floors 2, 3, 5, 6, 9 (and G, but he left the ground floor)
    1 (2 bars), 4 (4 bars), 7 (3 bars) have too few lights to look like that
    so 5 floors
A5 floors: 2, 3, 5, 6 and 9.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 6 wrongly counts the ground floor, but the lift had left it.
  • C✗ 7 includes floors with too few bars (they can’t show 5 lit bars).
  • D✗ 8 counts nearly every floor, ignoring the number of bars.
  • E✗ 9 counts every floor except 8.
Tip 8 with 2 dead bars = 5 lit bars. Floors that use exactly 5 bars: 2, 3, 5, 6, 9 (G excluded): 5.

2(TSA 2008, Q26) Patterns & tilesAnswer B

Why it's herejudged the 21st-hardest of the 25. The big shapes in the patterns are distracting: the unusual-looking ones are not necessarily the impossible ones. You need to check what each individual tile would have to look like.

The design of a tile for a bathroom wall is shown below:

Which of the following patterns may NOT1 be made using only a number of identical tiles2 as shown?

  1. Apattern A
  2. Bpattern B
  3. Cpattern C
  4. Dpattern D
  5. Epattern E

Reading it properly

  • 1Four patterns can be made; find the impossible one.
  • 2Every tile is the same, so you can only turn it round. However you turn it, it has one square bend and one round bend, in opposite corners.

Working it out

  1. Each pattern is a 3 × 3 block of tiles. The key fact about one tile:

  2. Check each tile in each pattern: does it have one square and one round bend in opposite corners?

    A: every tile the same way round ✓
    C, D, E: each tile has one of each ✓
    B: the middle-left tile has a round bend (bottom of the “D”) and a round bend (top of the bracket) ✗
  3. Tip Quick check without splitting: 9 tiles always give 9 square bends and 9 round bends. Pattern B only has 5 square bends (2 in each “D” and 1 at the top right).
    answer = B
BPattern B: several of its tiles would need two round bends, but every tile has one square and one round bend.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is every tile turned the same way.
  • C✗ C works: each tile is turned so it still has one square and one round bend.
  • D✗ D works: the circle uses four round bends and the square uses four square bends, from eight different tiles.

3(TSA 2021, Q30) PercentagesAnswer C

Why it's herejudged the 25th-hardest of the 25. Working out the full price per person is quick, but it is easy to forget to check whether the second shop qualifies for the discount, and the options are set up to catch exactly that.

I am planning a party next weekend. I have invited 100 people1. Last week I took advantage of an offer at my local supermarket where I was able to get 10% off the total price of party food if I spent more than $752. I paid $1083 and I estimate that the food that I bought will be enough for 60 people3. I’ve now had replies to the invitations and I know that there will be 80 people4 at the party. This afternoon I will buy the remaining food that I need.

What is the total price of the extra food5 that I need?

  1. A$32.40
  2. B$36.00
  3. C$40.00
  4. D$72.00
  5. E$80.00

Reading it properly

  • 1Red herring: only 80 are coming.
  • 2The discount only applies to a shop of more than $75. Check this afternoon’s shop too!
  • 3$108 was after 10% off. Undo it to get the real price of food for 60.
  • 480 − 60 = 20 more people to feed.
  • 5What this afternoon’s shop will cost.

Working it out

  1. $108 is what was left after 10% off, so $108 is 90% of the full price:

    90% = $108
    10% = $108 9 = $12
    100% = $120 for 60 people
    Trap! Don’t add 10% of $108 back on (108 + 10.80 = $118.80). The 10% was taken off the full price, not off $108.

  2. Full price per person, then the extra people:

    $120 60 = $2 a person
    80 − 60 = 20 more people
    20 × $2 = $40

  3. Does this afternoon’s shop get 10% off?

    $40 is under $75 no discount $40.00
C$40: 20 more people at the full price of $2 each, with no discount because $40 is under $75.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ $32.40 takes 10% off a $36 shop. But $40 is under $75, so there’s no discount this afternoon.
  • B✗ $36 uses the discounted price per person (108 60 = $1.80) for the 20 extra people. Today’s shop is too small for the discount.
  • D✗ $72 buys for 40 more people (all 100 invited) at the discounted $1.80. Only 80 are coming.
  • E✗ $80 buys for 40 more people (100 − 60). Only 80 are coming.
Tip $108 0.9 = $120 for 60, so $2 a head. 20 more people = $40, under $75 so no discount.

4(TSA 2011, Q42) Nets & foldingAnswer E

Why it's herejudged the 4th-hardest of the 25. Four of the five views are genuine, and the impossible one differs from a real view only by being a mirror image, so you have to track which way round the end triangles and long faces sit once the card is folded.

The piece of card shown below is folded to make a box with a triangular cross-section1.

Which one of the views below can not2 be of this box?

  1. Aview A
  2. Bview B
  3. Cview C
  4. Dview D
  5. Eview E

Reading it properly

  • 1The three long strips become the three long faces; the two triangles become the ends.
  • 2Four views are real. Find the fake.

Working it out

  1. Name the parts of the net. Each end triangle has a thin grey sliver along the edge where it is attached, so the sliver tells you which long face it touches:

  2. B and E show the same two faces: an end whose sliver sits on the striped face R (so it’s the right-hand end) and R itself. They are mirror images, so at most one of them is right.

  3. Folding never flips a face over: you always see the printed side. So two faces that share an edge look just like they do in the net, only turned round. Turn the right-hand flap and R upside down, so the end is on the left as in the views. The grey stripe is now on top:

  4. Quick check of the rest:

    • A: right-hand end (sliver on R, here the bottom) with P. P’s grey triangle comes to a point at the right-hand end and is widest at the far end. ✓
    • C: right-hand end (sliver on the hidden face R) with Q. Q’s grey half is its right-hand half, next to that end. ✓
    • D: left-hand end (sliver on Q, here the bottom) with P. P’s grey is widest at the left end and runs along the edge it shares with Q. ✓
    cannot be this box = E
EView E: its striped face has the grey band on the wrong side (it is B mirrored).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Real: the right-hand end with the diagonal face P, whose grey triangle is a point at that end and widest at the far end.
  • B✗ Real: the right-hand end with the striped face R, grey band along the top edge, exactly as the turned net shows.
  • C✗ Real: the right-hand end (its sliver on the hidden striped face) with face Q, whose grey half is next to that end.
  • D✗ Real: the left-hand end (sliver on Q, the bottom) with the diagonal face P, grey widest at that end.
Tip B and E are mirror images (end + striped face), so one is fake. Turn the net upside down so the right-hand flap is on the left: R’s grey stripe is on top. That’s B, so E is impossible.

5(TSA 2023, Q8) Digital displaysAnswer C

Why it's herejudged the 15th-hardest of the 25. The obvious reading of the first change leads to a contradiction, and you only escape it by noticing what the other digits tell you about the real times. Then you must sort out which bars are stuck on and which are stuck off.

As I waited for a train yesterday I was puzzled when I noticed the digital clock on the platform change from1

to

but then a minute later it changed to

And then, after another minute, it changed to

I realised that the last digit was not displaying properly2.

What was wrong with the last digit?

  1. AOne element was permanently on and one element was permanently off.
  2. BOne element was permanently on and two elements were permanently off.
  3. CTwo elements were permanently on and one element was permanently off.
  4. DTwo elements were permanently on and two elements were permanently off.
  5. EThree elements were permanently on and one element was permanently off.

Reading it properly

  • 1Each change is one minute. The tens digit goes 2 → 3, so the first change must be 15:29 → 15:30.
  • 2Only the last digit is broken. The real last digits are 9, 0, 1, 2.

Working it out

  1. Find the real times first. The minutes go from 2_ to 3_ in one minute, so it must be:

    15:29 15:30 15:31 15:32
    real last digits: 9, 0, 1, 2

  2. Draw each real digit and compare it with what the clock showed. Red dashes = should be lit but dark. Green = lit but shouldn’t be.

  3. Spot what never changes:

    top-right: dark every time, even for 9, 0, 1, 2 which all need it 1 off
    middle: lit on 0 and 1, which don’t use it on
    top-left: lit on 1 and 2, which don’t use it on
    2 on, 1 off C
    Tip Quick test: which bars are lit in all four pictures? Only top-left and middle. Which bar is never lit? Only top-right.
CTwo elements stuck on (top-left and middle) and one stuck off (top-right).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A spots the dead top-right bar but only one of the stuck bars (for example just the middle, from 0 showing as 6). The top-left is stuck on too: look at the 1.
  • B✗ Only one bar is never lit (top-right). The bottom-left lights up in the 6 and the E, so it works.
  • D✗ Same problem as B: you need two bars that never light, but only the top-right never lights.
  • E✗ Only two bars are lit in all four displays. The top and bottom bars are dark in the third one, so they aren’t stuck on.
Tip Real digits 9, 0, 1, 2. Lit in all four displays: top-left and middle (2 on). Never lit: top-right (1 off).

6(TSA 2018, Q44) Cubes & netsAnswer D

Why it's herejudged the 2nd-hardest of the 25. All five nets share one layout and differ only in which small triangles are shaded, so you must work out exactly which edge each shaded triangle touches after folding, and which way round the corner reads. One misplaced triangle leads you to a near-identical wrong net.

Here is a diagram of a cube.

Which one of the following nets could1 be folded to make this cube?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C3
  4. D4
  5. E5

Reading it properly

  • 1Only one net works. The three faces you see each have one black triangle; check where it sits.

Working it out

  1. On the cube, each visible face has a single black triangle, and each triangle sits against one edge:

  2. In every net the single-triangle squares are the same three (shaded): S1 and S2 are the 2nd and 3rd squares of the top row, S3 is the square under S2. They meet at one corner when folded, because S1’s bottom edge zips onto S3’s left edge. Now see what each lone triangle leans on:

    net 1: S1 → S3, S2 → S3 (both on S3) ✗
    net 2: S1 → S3, S2 → outside, S3 → outside ✗
    net 3: S2 → S3 and S3 → S2 (on each other) ✗
    net 4: S1 → S3 → S2 → outside, a chain ✓
    net 5: same single squares as net 2 ✗
  3. Check Fold net 4 with S1 on top: S3 is on its left and S2 on its right, the same clockwise order as the picture, so it really matches.
    answer: net 4
DNet 4.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Net 1: the lone triangles of S1 and S2 both lean on S3. On the cube they lean on two different faces.
  • B✗ Net 2: S2’s and S3’s triangles both lean on outside edges. On the cube only one does.
  • C✗ Net 3: S2’s and S3’s triangles lean on each other, across the same edge. On the cube no two triangles share an edge.
  • E✗ Net 5 has the same three single-triangle squares as net 2 (only a hidden bow-tie differs), so it fails for the same reason.
Tip Look only at the three squares with one black triangle. The cube needs a chain: top leans on left, left on right, right on the outside. Only net 4 does that.

Why it's herejudged the 9th-hardest of the 25. You have to rebuild a 3D stack from an isometric drawing, including cubes you cannot see, and then picture it from an unusual direction; the mirror image of the true view is among the options.

One of the exhibits in the Interactive Art Exhibition at the Hanson Gallery consists of 16 identical free-standing cubes1. Visitors to the exhibition are invited to handle these cubes and rearrange them to create their own work of art.

This is the arrangement that I have made:

What is the side view2 of my arrangement from the direction indicated by the arrow3?

  1. Aview A
  2. Bview B
  3. Cview C
  4. Dview D
  5. Eview E

Reading it properly

  • 116 cubes. “Free-standing” means every cube sits on the floor or on another cube, so some cubes are hidden underneath.
  • 2A flat outline: in each column you only see the tallest stack.
  • 3Stand where the arrow starts (top right) and look along it. Which end is on your left?

Working it out

  1. Look down on the arrangement from above and write the height of each stack on a plan drawn the same way round as the picture:

    2 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 16 cubes ✓

  2. The arrow looks along the coloured rows. Each row shows up as one column, as tall as its tallest stack:

    front row (bottom right of picture): 1
    next row: 2
    middle row with the 3-high stack: 3
    back row (top left): 2

  3. Which way round? Standing at the arrow and looking down-left, your left hand points to the front of the picture (bottom right). So the front row is on the left:

    1, 2, 3, 2 E
EView E: columns 1, 2, 3, 2 from left to right.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A ends with a column of 1, but the back row has a stack of 2 (the cube top at the upper left of the picture, partly hidden behind the tall stack).
  • B✗ B (2, 3, 2, 1) is E back to front: the view from the opposite side, or left and right swapped.
  • C✗ C has a second column of only 1, but that row includes the 2-high stack just below the tall stack in the picture.
  • D✗ D has no column of height 1, but the front row is just one cube on its own.
Tip Plan + heights. Rows along the arrow: tallest 1, 2, 3, 2 from the front. The front is on your left, so 1-2-3-2: E.

8(TSA 2018, Q20) Cubes & netsAnswer B

Why it's herejudged the hardest of the 25. Every view has to be checked both for which letters can sit together and for how each letter is turned, and two views show the same three faces with one letter turned differently. Tracking letter orientation on the top face after folding is where most people slip.

The diagram below shows the net of a cube.

The following diagrams show five suggested views of the cube once it has been assembled1.

Which two2 of the above are possible views of the cube?

  1. A1 and 2
  2. B2 and 3
  3. C3 and 4
  4. D1 and 4
  5. E4 and 5

Reading it properly

  • 1Every view shows three faces that really are neighbours, so you must check which way each letter is turned.
  • 2Exactly two views work. 3 and 4 show the same faces with A turned differently, so at most one of them can be right.

Working it out

  1. First, opposite faces (they can never be seen together): D–E, N–W, A–R. All five views pass, so the letters’ turns decide it.

    A appears in four views, so pin down A’s four sides:

  2. Now check A in each view:

    • View 1: E is on top and A sits below it, so A’s point should aim up at E. It is lying on its side. ✗
    • View 3: A’s right side touches the top face N, and its feet face D across the middle edge. ✓
    • View 4: A’s point aims at D. But A’s feet must touch D. ✗
    • View 5: A’s right side should touch N on top and its point should aim at E, sideways. It stands upright. ✗
    • View 2 has no A. E’s left side touches R ✓ (joined on the net), E’s bottom touches W’s top ✓ (joined on the net), W’s left side meets R’s bottom ✓ (they zip round E’s corner).

  3. The real cube, turned to match each view:

    possible views: 2 and 3 B
    Tip N looks the same upside down, so a sideways N (looking like Z) only tells you which way its uprights run, not which end is the top.
BViews 2 and 3 are possible.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ View 1 is impossible: with E on top, A must stand upright beneath it, not on its side.
  • C✗ Views 3 and 4 can’t both be right: same three faces, but A is turned the opposite way. In 4 A’s point aims at D, yet A’s feet touch D.
  • D✗ Both 1 and 4 are impossible (see A’s turns).
  • E✗ Both are impossible: in 5 A stands upright, but its right side should touch N on top.
Tip Fix A: feet on D, point at E, right side on N. Views 1, 4 and 5 all turn A wrongly, leaving 2 and 3.

9(TSA 2013, Q6) Dates & digitsAnswer D

Why it's herejudged the 23rd-hardest of the 25. You must work out which digits such dates can use, then combine the two age clues. It is a long logical chain, though testing the options can shorten it.

On only 360 occasions during the twentieth century was it possible to write the date in the form DD/MM/YYYY using eight different digits1. One example was 28th July 1956 (28/07/1956).

Eleven of Arthur's birthdays2 have been such dates (though not his date of birth), and in 1974 and 1983 the two digits that did not form part of the date made up his age at the time3.

In which year was Arthur born?

  1. A1909
  2. B1916
  3. C1918
  4. D1936
  5. E1938

Reading it properly

  • 1DD/MM/YYYY has 8 digits. All different means 8 of the ten digits 0–9 are used once, and 2 are left over.
  • 2A birthday is the same day and month every year. That links 1974 and 1983 together.
  • 3The 2 spare digits make his age, e.g. 3 and 8 make 38.

Working it out

  1. First, what must every such date look like? The year is 19__, so 1 and 9 are used.

    • Month: 10, 11, 12 and 01 reuse the 1, and 09 reuses the 9. So the month is 0m, and 0 is used too.
    • Day: it can’t start with 0 or 1 (used), and 30 or 31 reuse 0 or 1. So the day is 2d.
    every date = 2d / 0m / 19YY 1, 9, 0, 2 always used

  2. Now list the spare digits for each year (d, m and the age share them):

    1974: used 1, 9, 7, 4, 0, 2 free 3, 5, 6, 8
    1983: used 1, 9, 8, 3, 0, 2 free 4, 5, 6, 7
    same birthday, so d and m are in both lists 5 and 6

    So his birthday is 25/06 (or 26/05), and the leftover digits make his age:

  3. 1974: 3 and 8 38 (83 is silly: then 1983 would make him 92)
    1983: 4 and 7 47 ✓ exactly 9 years older
    born 1974 − 38 = 1936
    Check Years using two of 3, 4, 7, 8 after 1936 are 1937, 38, 43, 47, 48, 73, 74, 78, 83, 84, 87. That is eleven birthdays ✓. And 25/06/1936 repeats the 6, so his date of birth doesn’t count ✓.
D1936: his birthday is 25/06 (or 26/05), and he was 38 in 1974 and 47 in 1983.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1909 makes him 65 then 74. In 1974 the date would need 3 and 8, in 1983 it would need 5 and 6: two different birthdays.
  • B✗ 1916 makes him 58 then 67. The date would need 3 and 6 in 1974 but 4 and 5 in 1983: not the same birthday.
  • C✗ 1918 makes him 56 then 65. The date would need 3 and 8 in 1974 but 4 and 7 in 1983.
  • E✗ 1938 makes him 36 then 45. The date would need 5 and 8 in 1974 but 6 and 7 in 1983.
Tip Dates are always 2d/0m/19YY. Spare digits: 1974 → 3, 5, 6, 8; 1983 → 4, 5, 6, 7. Same birthday, so d and m are 5 and 6; ages 38 and 47; born 1936.

Why it's herejudged the 22nd-hardest of the 25. Spotting which faces are opposite each other rules out several cubes, but the last two candidates differ only in the direction of a diagonal stripe, which needs careful folding.

The cut out below was folded together to make a cube1.

Which of the following cubes was formed from this cut-out2?

  1. Acube A
  2. Bcube B
  3. Ccube C
  4. Dcube D
  5. Ecube E

Reading it properly

  • 1In a row of faces, two faces with one face between them end up opposite. You can never see opposite faces together.
  • 2Only one picture is possible. Knock the others out one rule at a time.

Working it out

  1. Name the faces and pair up the opposites (one face between them in a straight line):

  2. Test 1: opposite faces can’t both be seen.

    A: stripe on top and a stripe on the front ✗
    B: stripe on top and a stripe on the right ✗
    C: the thin line on top and the cross ✗
    D: stripe, line, band allowed
    E: stripe, cross, band allowed

  3. Test 2: D or E? Use a corner. In both, the top stripe runs straight into the front-left face. The stripe below the cross does that (it carries on the cross’s bar, and the line face is opposite the cross), so the top face is the bottom stripe. Now look at the corner in the middle of each picture:

    • D: the middle corner joins the line, the bottom stripe and a band. Every band runs into that corner (red rings above), but D’s band goes the other way ✗

    answer = E

  • B✗ B also shows both stripe faces at once (top and right), and they are opposite.
  • C✗ C shows the thin line and the cross together, but they are opposite faces.
  • D✗ D’s faces are allowed, but the band is on the wrong diagonal: it should run into the middle corner where it meets the line and the bottom stripe.

11(TSA 2023, Q46) Relative speedAnswer B

Why it's herejudged the 24th-hardest of the 25. A natural shortcut with the 12% answers a slightly different question; you must be careful about whose laps you are counting and about counting completed laps rather than rounding.

The drawing shows a slot car track.

A grey car and a white car start together at the same point and travel in the same direction1. Because of the figure-of-eight arrangement of the track, each car travels exactly the same distance to complete one lap2 of the track. The grey car is 12% faster3 than the white car.

How many laps will the grey car have completed4 before it passes the white car for the first time5?

  1. A8
  2. B9
  3. C10
  4. D11
  5. E12

Reading it properly

  • 1Same start, same direction: a chase.
  • 2So the two lanes don’t matter. A lap is a lap.
  • 3Every time white does 1 lap, grey does 1.12 laps: grey gains 0.12 of a lap.
  • 4Whole laps finished by grey.
  • 5They start side by side, so to pass (overtake) white again, grey must get a whole lap ahead.

Working it out

  1. Grey gains 12% of a lap for every lap white does. It passes white when it is a whole lap ahead:

  2. lead needed: 1 lap = 100%
    gain per white lap: 12%
    100 12 = 8⅓ white laps
    grey: 8⅓ + 1 = 9⅓ laps completed 9
    Check Check with 1.12: after 8 white laps grey has done 8.96 laps (not yet past). After 9 white laps grey has done 10.08 (already past). So the pass happens partway through grey’s 10th lap, with 9 laps completed.
B9 laps: grey passes white after 9⅓ of its own laps.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 8 is how many laps the white car has completed (8⅓) when it is passed.
  • C✗ 10 rounds 9⅓ up. Grey is still in the middle of its 10th lap when it passes.
  • D✗ After 11 grey laps, white has done only about 9.8, so grey is already more than a lap ahead: it passed earlier.
  • E✗ 12 takes the 12 from “12%” as a number of laps.
Tip Gain 0.12 lap per white lap; need 1 lap: 100 12 = 8⅓ white laps = 9⅓ grey laps. 9 completed.

Four triangles of card coloured on one side1 are joined as shown.

The joined triangles are then folded and joined to make a pyramid2.

24(TSA 2009, Q44) Tiling & ratioAnswer C

Why it's herejudged the 13th-hardest of the 25. Counting tiles in the drawing gives a misleading ratio because of the cut-off edges; you need to find the repeating unit around one hexagon and count only the triangles that belong to it.

The pattern below shows part of the tiling of a large floor in a community centre. It is made up of two types of tile: a hexagon and a triangle.

For a sufficiently large expanse of flooring1, approximately what proportion of the two types of tile2 will be required?

  1. A1 hexagon to 6 triangles
  2. B1 hexagon to 9 triangles
  3. C1 hexagon to 12 triangles
  4. D1 hexagon to 15 triangles
  5. E1 hexagon to 18 triangles

Reading it properly

  • 1Ignore the ragged edge of the picture. Find the pattern that repeats across a big floor.
  • 2How many triangle tiles for every hexagon tile?

Working it out

  1. A hexagon fills exactly the space of 6 triangles. Look at the vertical strips of triangles in the picture: they come in a repeating set of three: a strip through the left half of the hexagons, a strip through the right half, then a plain strip.

  2. Go down one strip from one hexagon to the next (6 triangle spaces) and count:

    left-half strip: 3 spaces of hexagon + 3 triangles
    right-half strip: 3 spaces of hexagon + 3 triangles
    plain strip: 6 triangles
    block total: 1 hexagon and 3 + 3 + 6 = 12 triangles
  3. ratio 1 hexagon to 12 triangles
    Check Area check: 1 hexagon + 12 triangles = 6 + 12 = 18 triangle spaces, so hexagons cover a third of the floor. That looks right in the picture.
    Trap! Counting the picture directly gives about 16 triangles per hexagon, because its border is full of extra triangles. On a big floor the border hardly matters.
C1 hexagon to 12 triangles: each repeating block is one hexagon plus 12 triangles.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 6 counts only the triangles touching the hexagon’s sides and misses the ones in between.
  • B✗ 9 shares the plain strip between two hexagon columns (6 + 3). But the strips go hexagon, hexagon, plain, so each hexagon column has a whole plain strip of its own.
  • D✗ 15 is roughly what you get by counting the picture, which has extra triangles round its border (about 16 per hexagon). A large floor has no such border.
  • E✗ 18 counts the whole ring of triangles round one hexagon, but neighbouring hexagons share those rings. (18 is also the block size if you count the hexagon’s own 6 spaces as triangles.)
Tip Strips repeat hexagon, hexagon, plain. Per hexagon: 3 + 3 + 6 = 12 triangles.

25(TSA 2012, Q26) Folding & symmetryAnswer A

Why it's herejudged the 11th-hardest of the 25. The trap is to slide one hole across onto the other; when the sheet is folded one hole is flipped over, and that changes which pair of shapes gives the octagon.

A sheet of paper has two holes1 cut from it. When it is folded in half2 the hole shown in the diagram below is seen.

Which one of the following could not3 be the unfolded sheet of paper? (The dotted line indicates where it was folded.)

  1. Asheet A
  2. Bsheet B
  3. Csheet C
  4. Dsheet D
  5. Esheet E

Reading it properly

  • 1One hole in each half of the sheet.
  • 2Folding flips one half over onto the other: the left hole becomes its mirror image.
  • 3You can only see through where both layers have a hole, so the octagon is the overlap.

Working it out

  1. Folding along the dotted line flips the left half over onto the right. Light only gets through where the flipped left hole and the right hole overlap. That overlap must be the octagon.

  2. Test the overlaps:

    • A: the left hole has its top-left and bottom-right corners cut off. Flipped, those become top-right and bottom-left, exactly like the right hole. The overlap is that same 6-sided shape, not an octagon ✗
    • B: a downward arrow over an upward arrow: the box parts and the points trim each other into an octagon ✓
    • C: square over diamond: the diamond trims the square’s corners into an octagon ✓
    • D: tall hexagon over wide hexagon: each trims the other’s points, an octagon ✓
    • E: flipped, the left arrow points left, so you get a left arrow over a right arrow, an octagon (like B on its side) ✓
    cannot be the sheet: A
AA: when folded, its two holes line up exactly, so the hole you see has only 6 sides.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ B works: the down-arrow and up-arrow overlap in an octagon.
  • C✗ C works: a square and a diamond overlap in an octagon.
  • D✗ D works: a tall hexagon and a wide hexagon overlap in an octagon.
  • E✗ E works: once flipped, the left arrow points the other way, and opposite arrows overlap in an octagon.
Tip The octagon is the overlap of mirror(left) and right. In A, mirror(left) = right, so the overlap keeps only 2 cut corners: 6 sides.
