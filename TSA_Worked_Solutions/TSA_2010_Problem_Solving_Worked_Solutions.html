1(Q2 in the original paper) Money & best deal

Splashford Swimming Pool charges £2 per session for adults and £1 for children1.

Also available is a Family Swimcard. At a cost of £50, the Family Swimcard allows unlimited use of the pool for one year for 2 adults and up to 3 children2. For larger families, every additional child must pay half the children's rate each time3.

Mr and Mrs Teal and their 4 children4 are keen swimmers. They used their Swimcard when the family went swimming 40 times5 last year.

How much did the Swimcard save6 the Teal family last year?

  1. A£50
  2. B£190
  3. C£230
  4. D£250
  5. E£270

Reading it properly

  • 1Normal prices, per person, per visit.
  • 2The card covers 5 people. The Teals are 6!
  • 3Child number 4 still pays, but only half of £1 = 50p a visit.
  • 42 adults + 4 children = 6 swimmers.
  • 5Everything per visit gets × 40.
  • 6Saving = cost without the card − cost with the card.

Working it out

  1. Cost with no card, one family visit:

    2 adults × £2 = £4
    4 children × £1 = £4
    £8 a visit 40 × £8 = £320

  2. Cost with the card: £50 covers 2 adults + 3 children. The 4th child pays half price every time.

    half of £1 = 50p 40 × 50p = £20
    card + extra child = £50 + £20 = £70
  3. saving = £320 − £70 = £250
    Trap! Do not stop at “the card costs £50”. A saving question always compares two full totals.
D£250: £320 without the card against £70 with it.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £50 is just the price of the card, not what it saves.
  • B✗ £190 would mean the card plus extras cost £130. That happens if you charge the 4th child the adult £2 rate (50 + 80).
  • C✗ £230 charges the 4th child the full £1 each visit (50 + 40 = 90). The extra child pays half the children’s rate.
  • E✗ £270 forgets the 4th child completely (320 − 50). The card only covers 3 children.
Tip No card: £8 × 40 = £320. Card: £50 + 40 × 50p = £70. Save £250.
2(Q6 in the original paper) Calendars & cycles

Acme Haulage pays dividends to its shareholders every six months1. Better Ball Bearings pays its shareholders every eight months1 and Koffmore Chemists pays every eighteen months1.

In January 19902 all three companies paid me a dividend.

In what month and year did this next3 happen?

  1. AJuly 1991
  2. BJanuary 1992
  3. CJuly 1995
  4. DJanuary 1996
  5. EJanuary 2002

Reading it properly

  • 1Three repeating cycles: 6, 8 and 18 months.
  • 2Month 0. Count months from here.
  • 3The first month after this that is in all three cycles: the lowest common multiple.

Working it out

  1. We need the first number of months that is a multiple of 6, 8 and 18. Quickest: go up in 18s (the biggest) and test each one.

    18 ÷ 8 ✗
    36 ÷ 8 = 4.5 ✗
    54 ÷ 8 ✗
    72 ÷ 8 = 9 ✓ and 72 ÷ 6 = 12 ✓
  2. 72 months = 6 years January 1990 + 6 years = January 1996
    Tip Every multiple of 18 is already a multiple of 6, so really you only need “a multiple of 18 that 8 divides into”.
DJanuary 1996: 72 months is the first time all three cycles line up.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ July 1991 is 18 months on. Acme and Koffmore pay then, but 18 isn’t a multiple of 8.
  • B✗ January 1992 is 24 months: Acme and Better line up, but 24 isn’t a multiple of 18.
  • C✗ July 1995 is 66 months: 66 isn’t a multiple of 8 or of 18.
  • E✗ January 2002 is 144 months = 8 × 18 multiplied straight out. All three do pay then, but it is the second time; 72 comes first.
Tip Count in 18s: 18, 36, 54, 72. The first one 8 divides into is 72 months = 6 years, so January 1996.
3(Q7 in the original paper) Digital displays

A woman has to get up for work at seven o'clock in the morning1. She wakes up and looks over at her digital clock. Unfortunately, the batteries are low and the figures on the display, showing hours and minutes, aren't showing up properly2. All she can see is the display below:

It is light outside, so she cannot be more than twelve hours late3.

What is the greatest4 amount of time that she could be late waking up?

  1. A1h 39min
  2. B1h 59min
  3. C2h 25min
  4. D2h 35min
  5. E2h 59min

Reading it properly

  • 1She should be up at 07:00, so lateness = time shown − 07:00.
  • 2Some bars are missing. Every bar you can see is really lit; more bars may be lit too.
  • 3So the time is between 07:00 and 19:00.
  • 4Make each digit as big as it is allowed to be.

Working it out

  1. For each digit, list the numbers that contain all the bars you can see:

    DigitBars you seeNumbers with those barsAllowed in a time
    1st (tens of hours)top, top-right0, 2, 3, 7, 8, 90 or 2
    2nd (hours)top, both right0, 3, 7, 8, 9any of these
    3rd (tens of minutes)top, middle, bottom2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9only 2, 3, 5 (it must be 5 or less)
    4th (minutes)bottom-righteverything except 20, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9

  2. Now the time must be from 07:00 to 19:00:

    • 1st digit 2 gives 20:xx or 23:xx: more than 12 hours late ✗
    • 1st digit 1 is impossible: a “1” has no top bar.
    • So it is 0 then 7, 8 or 9. Biggest: 09.
    • Minutes as big as possible: tens 5, units 9.
  3. latest time 09:59 09:59 − 07:00 = 2h 59min
    Trap! The third digit looks like a 3, and that is the trap. A 5 also has a top, middle and bottom bar.
E2h 59min: the display could be showing 09:59.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1h 39min means 08:39. That misses that the 2nd digit can be a 9 and the 3rd a 5.
  • B✗ 1h 59min means 08:59. A 9 also has the top and both right bars, so 09 is possible.
  • C✗ 2h 25min means 09:25: reads the 3rd digit as a 2 only, when 3 and 5 fit too.
  • D✗ 2h 35min means 09:35: reads the 3rd digit as the 3 it looks like. A 5 has the same three bars, and the last digit could be 9.
Tip Biggest digit that contains the visible bars, within 07:00 to 19:00: 0, 9, 5, 9. So 09:59, which is 2h 59min late.
4(Q8 in the original paper) Scales & multipliers

The Richter Scale is used to measure the strength of earthquakes. It has the property that an increase of one unit on the scale represents a tenfold increase in the strength1 of the quake. Three earthquakes have the following values on the Richter Scale:

X : 2.02
Y : 3.02
Z : 4.02

Which one of the following statements about their relative strengths3 is true?

  1. AX is one-tenth the strength of Y and one-twentieth the strength of Z.
  2. BX is one-tenth the strength of Y and one-hundredth the strength of Z.
  3. CX is one-tenth the strength of Y and one-thousandth the strength of Z.
  4. DZ is twice as strong as X and Y is one-and-a-half times as strong as X.
  5. EZ is one-tenth stronger than Y which is one-tenth stronger than X.

Reading it properly

  • 1Each +1 on the scale = × 10 in strength. Steps multiply, they don’t add.
  • 2X to Y is 1 step. X to Z is 2 steps.
  • 3Compare X with Y, and X with Z.

Working it out

  1. Count the steps and multiply by 10 for each one:

    X → Y: 2.0 → 3.0 = 1 step Y = 10 × X
    X → Z: 2.0 → 4.0 = 2 steps Z = 10 × 10 × X = 100 × X
  2. X is 110 of Y and 1100 of Z B
BB: one step = × 10, two steps = × 100.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ One-twentieth adds the two tenfold jumps (10 + 10). Jumps multiply: 10 × 10 = 100.
  • C✗ One-thousandth would be 3 steps (10 × 10 × 10). Z is only 2 steps above X.
  • D✗ Treats the scale like ordinary numbers (4 is twice 2). Here each unit is a × 10 jump.
  • E✗ “One-tenth stronger” means only 10% more. Each step is ten times as strong.
Tip 1 step = × 10, 2 steps = × 100. So X is 1/10 of Y and 1/100 of Z.
5(Q12 in the original paper) Percentages & discounts

Sue and Ben are buying some kitchen goods at a shop which is having a sale. The terms of the sale are as follows:

Total marked price of purchases (before discount)Reduction (%)
Up to £5025
Up to £100133
£100 or more250

The marked prices of the goods they have chosen come to £963.

How much less would they actually spend4, to the nearest £15, if they chose extra goods to the value of £66?

  1. A£2
  2. B£6
  3. C£10
  4. D£13
  5. E£16

Reading it properly

  • 1£96 sits in this band: 33% off.
  • 2Push the total to £100 or more and everything is half price.
  • 3The starting basket.
  • 4Compare what they pay: before vs after adding the extras.
  • 5Round only at the very end.
  • 6£96 + £6 = £102. The extras get the 50% off too, so they only cost £3.

Working it out

  1. Now: £96 is “up to £100”, so 33% off. They pay 67%:

    96 × 0.67 = 67 − 4 × 0.67 = 67 − 2.68 = £64.32
    Tip 96 × 0.67: do 100 × 0.67 = 67, then take off 4 × 0.67 = 2.68.

  2. With the extras: £96 + £6 = £102, which is “£100 or more”, so half price:

    102 ÷ 2 = £51
  3. £64.32 − £51 = £13.32 to the nearest £1 = £13
D£13 less: £64.32 for £96 of goods against £51 for £102 of goods.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £2 is how far £102 goes over the £100 line, not money saved.
  • B✗ £6 is the value of the extra goods, not a saving.
  • C✗ £10 takes the extra discount on the £96 (£16.32) and then knocks off the full £6 for the extras. But the extras are half price too, so they only add £3.
  • E✗ £16 is the extra discount on the original £96, forgetting they now pay £3 for the extra goods.
Tip Before: 96 × 0.67 ≈ £64.32. After: 102 ÷ 2 = £51. Difference ≈ £13.

QUARTERLY ELECTRICITY BILL

At the end of the quarterly charging period to which the above bill relates, the householder changed her electricity consumption pattern. In the next quarter she roughly halved her standard consumption3 and increased her off-peak consumption by about ten times3.

What approximate4 total bill should she expect for the next charging period?

  1. A£90
  2. B£120
  3. C£150
  4. D£170
  5. E£200

Reading it properly

  • 1The two parts that change: standard £168 and off-peak £10.
  • 2Fixed charges don’t depend on use, so they stay the same: 12.30 + 5.45 ≈ £18.
  • 3Halve the standard cost, multiply the off-peak cost by 10.
  • 4Round freely: options are £30 apart.

Working it out

  1. Split the old bill into its parts (rounded):

    standard ≈ £168
    off-peak ≈ £10
    fixed ≈ 12 + 5.5 ≈ £18

  2. Apply the changes. Only the parts that depend on use change:

    standard: 168 ÷ 2 = £84
    off-peak: 10 × 10 = £100
    fixed: still £18
  3. 84 + 100 + 18 = £202 about £200
    Tip Same cost per unit, so the cost of each part changes exactly like its units: half the units = half the cost.
EAbout £200: £84 standard + £100 off-peak + £18 fixed.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £90 halves the standard part (84) and adds the old off-peak £10, forgetting both the ten-fold rise and the fixed charges.
  • B✗ £120 halves the standard part but leaves off-peak at £10 (84 + 10 + 18 = 112). The off-peak must go up ten times.
  • C✗ £150 would need the off-peak part to be only about £50: that is × 5, not × 10.
  • D✗ £170 is nearer to 84 + 100 = 184 than £200 is, so it comes from forgetting the £18 of fixed charges.
Tip 168 ÷ 2 ≈ 84, 10 × 10 = 100, fixed ≈ 18. Total ≈ 202, so £200.
7(Q14 in the original paper) Shapes & tiling

The diagram shows the outline of a window which is intended to be composed of a number of panes of glass, all of the same size and shape1.

Which one of the shapes below could be used to fill this window2?

  1. Ashape A
  2. Bshape B
  3. Cshape C
  4. Dshape D
  5. Eshape E

Reading it properly

  • 1Every pane identical. They may be turned or flipped over.
  • 2Fill the whole star exactly: no gaps, no overlaps.

Working it out

  1. The star has a centre, 4 tips and 4 inner corners. Join the centre to all 8 corners. It splits into 8 identical thin triangles (each point cut in half down its middle):

  2. Each sliver has a long side (centre to tip), a short side (centre to an inner corner) and a slanting side along the outline. The orange one is exactly shape A A

    Check Half of the slivers are mirror images of the others. Glass can be flipped over, so they are all the same pane.

  3. Why the others fail:

    • D is a whole point cut off at the inner corners. Four of them leave a square hole in the middle.
    • C is half of D: same square hole, and thin right-angled triangles can’t fill a square.
    • B is two opposite points joined. One fits across the middle, but the other two points would overlap it.
    • E is a point plus half the centre square. Four of them would cover the middle square twice.
AShape A: 8 copies (some flipped) fill the star, centre to every corner.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ B covers two opposite points and the middle. A second B can’t cross it without overlapping.
  • C✗ C is half a point. The points get filled, but the square in the middle can’t be made from these.
  • D✗ D is a whole point. Four Ds leave the middle square empty.
  • E✗ E is a point plus half the middle square. Four of them use up two middle squares, so they overlap.
Tip Draw lines from the centre to every corner: 8 equal slivers, each like A.
8(Q18 in the original paper) Capacity & loading

I have been asked to move 120 boxes weighing 25kg each and 90 boxes weighing 20kg each1 from the ground floor to the fifth floor of the office block in which I work. Unfortunately, nobody is available to give me any help.2

There is a sign in the lift which reads:

MAXIMUM LOAD
4 persons3 or 400 kg4

I weigh 80kg.5

What is the minimum6 number of journeys I will have to make from the ground floor to the fifth floor?

  1. A10
  2. B12
  3. C13
  4. D15
  5. E16

Reading it properly

  • 1Total box weight: 120 × 25 + 90 × 20.
  • 2No helper, so I must ride up with every load.
  • 3Boxes are not persons. Only the weight limit matters.
  • 4The real limit: 400 kg including me.
  • 5Room left for boxes: 400 − 80 = 320 kg a trip.
  • 6Pack each trip as full as possible.

Working it out

  1. Weight of all the boxes:

    120 × 25 = 3000 kg
    90 × 20 = 1800 kg
    total = 4800 kg

  2. I travel with every load, so each trip carries at most:

    400 − 80 = 320 kg of boxes
    4800 ÷ 320 = 15 at least 15 trips

  3. Can every trip be exactly 320 kg? Share the boxes evenly over 15 trips:

    120 ÷ 15 = 8 big boxes, 90 ÷ 15 = 6 small boxes
    8 × 25 + 6 × 20 = 200 + 120 = 320 kg ✓
    minimum = 15 journeys
D15 journeys: 8 big + 6 small boxes + me = exactly 400 kg each time.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 10 only moves the 25 kg boxes (3000 ÷ 320 → 10 trips) and forgets the 20 kg ones.
  • B✗ 12 is 4800 ÷ 400: forgets that I am in the lift too, using 80 kg of every load.
  • C✗ 13 would need about 370 kg of boxes per trip, but only 320 kg fits once I am in.
  • E✗ 16 moves the two sizes on separate trips (10 + 6). Mixing them fills every trip exactly, so 15 is enough.
Tip Boxes 4800 kg; room per trip 400 − 80 = 320 kg; 4800 ÷ 320 = 15, and 8 big + 6 small = 320 exactly.
9(Q19 in the original paper) Tables & best deal

It is 12 noon1 and I have just arrived in the centre of town for a meeting that is due to last for at least three and a half hours2.

After this meeting, I have an important appointment at 6.00pm which requires me to leave before 5.30pm3.

There are 5 car parks in the town centre that charge as follows:

Grove Street£6.00 per day, flat rate.
Victoria Square£1.00 per hour or part of an hour4.
Central ParkFree for 2 hours, then £2.00 per hour or part of an hour.
BonningtonsFree for 1 hour, then £1.50 per hour or part of an hour.
Grange Road£2.50 for 3 hours, then £1.50 per hour or part of an hour.

In which car park should I leave my car in order to pay as little as possible5 in parking fees?

  1. AGrove Street
  2. BVictoria Square
  3. CCentral Park
  4. DBonningtons
  5. EGrange Road

Reading it properly

  • 1The clock starts at 12:00.
  • 2“At least”: it could run on. We don’t know when it ends.
  • 3So the car is parked from 3½ hours up to nearly 5½ hours.
  • 4Any part of an hour costs a whole hour: always round up.
  • 5Cheapest whatever time the meeting finishes.

Working it out

  1. How long will the car be parked? Somewhere from 12:00 until between 3:30 and 5:30:

  2. Price each car park for the three possible lengths of stay (round every part-hour up):

    Car parkleave 3:30 to 4:00leave 4:00 to 5:00leave 5:00 to 5:30
    Grove Street£6£6£6
    Victoria Square£4£5£6
    Central Park2 h free + 2 → £4+ 3 → £6+ 4 → £8
    Bonningtons1 h free + 3 → £4.50+ 4 → £6.00+ 5 → £7.50
    Grange Road£2.50 + 1 → £4.00+ 2 → £5.50+ 3 → £7.00

  3. Victoria Square is the cheapest (or joint cheapest) in every column, so it is the safe choice Victoria Square

    Trap! If you only cost exactly 3½ hours you get a three-way tie at £4 (Victoria, Central, Grange). The “at least” is what breaks the tie.
BVictoria Square: £4 to £6, never beaten whenever the meeting ends.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Grove Street is £6 whatever happens. Victoria is never more than £6 and usually less.
  • C✗ Central Park ties at £4 if the meeting ends right on 3:30, but jumps to £6 or £8 if it overruns.
  • D✗ Bonningtons is never below £4.50 (3½ h = 1 free + 3 charged part-hours).
  • E✗ Grange Road ties at £4 up to 4 hours, but costs £5.50 or £7 if the meeting runs on.
Tip Cost each one for “just over 3½ h” and “nearly 5½ h”: Victoria £4 to £6 is never beaten.
10(Q20 in the original paper) Graphs & sharing costs

A taxi service advertises its rates for travel to the local airport as shown below:

AIRPORT TAXI SERVICE
£701
(includes return journey)
EXTRA PASSENGERS £10 EACH2
(vehicles licensed to carry 6 passengers)

Which one of the following bar graphs correctly illustrates how the cost per person3 decreases as more people get together to share a taxi to the airport?

  1. Agraph A
  2. Bgraph B
  3. Cgraph C
  4. Dgraph D
  5. Egraph E

Reading it properly

  • 1One passenger pays £70 in total.
  • 2Each extra passenger adds £10 to the total fare. The first passenger isn’t “extra”.
  • 3Per person = total fare ÷ number of people.

Working it out

  1. Total fare for n people = £70 + £10 for each person after the first. Then share it out:

    PeopleTotal fareEach pays
    1£70£70
    2£80£40
    3£90£30
    4£100£25
    5£110£22
    6£120£20

  2. Look for a graph going 70, 40, 30, 25, 22, 20. The quick test is the second bar: it must be exactly 40 D

DGraph D: 70, 40, 30, 25, 22, 20.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A drops by £10 a person each time (70, 60, 50…): it takes £10 off the price each instead of adding £10 to the total.
  • B✗ B is £70 ÷ people (70, 35, 23…): it forgets the £10 for each extra passenger.
  • C✗ C starts at £80: it charges the first passenger the £10 extra too (80, 45, 33…).
  • E✗ E halves every time (70, 35, 17.5…), far cheaper than the real fares.
Tip Two people: (70 + 10) ÷ 2 = £40. Six: 120 ÷ 6 = £20. Only D has 40 and 20.
11(Q24 in the original paper) Counting arrangements

A secretary is in a bad mood with her boss and has to write three letters intended for three different people1. She makes sure that each of the recipients of the letters will receive one written to someone else2.

In how many different ways3 can she send three different letters to achieve this?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C3
  4. D5
  5. E6

Reading it properly

  • 1Call the people A, B, C. Each has a letter meant for them.
  • 2Nobody gets their own letter. Every single person gets a wrong one.
  • 3Count the whole arrangements, not single letters.

Working it out

  1. A must get B’s or C’s letter. Try both:

    • A gets B’s letter. Then B gets A’s or C’s. B gets C’s C gets A’s ✓   B gets A’s C is left with C’s own ✗
    • A gets C’s letter. B can’t have their own, so B gets A’s C gets B’s ✓
  2. only 2 ways
    Check Of the 6 ways to hand out 3 letters: 1 gives everyone their own, 3 swap just two people (so one person still gets their own), and 2 move everyone.
B2 ways: everyone passes “one along” in one direction or the other.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1 finds one pattern but misses its mirror image (passing the other way).
  • C✗ 3 counts the “swap two people” arrangements, but each of those leaves the third person with their own letter.
  • D✗ 5 = 6 − 1 only removes the case where everyone gets their own letter.
  • E✗ 6 is every way to send 3 letters, including ones where someone gets their own.
Tip Nobody gets their own: the letters must all shift round one place, left or right. 2 ways.

13(Q26 in the original paper) Graphs: change

The graph below shows Mr Evans' bank balance at the end of each month1 in a year.

Which one of the following graphs could2 show the actual change in his bank balance each month3?

  1. Agraph A
  2. Bgraph B
  3. Cgraph C
  4. Dgraph D
  5. Egraph E

Reading it properly

  • 1The heights are balances, not changes.
  • 2No exact numbers needed: the change bars just have to go the right way each month.
  • 3Change in a month = this month’s balance − last month’s. Up = positive bar, down = negative bar.

Working it out

  2. Now compare each option’s bars, month by month (✗ = first wrong month):

    D↓↑↓↓↑=↑↓↑↓↑✓

    Tip July is the giveaway: June and July balances are the same height, so July’s change must be zero (no bar). That kills A at once.
DGraph D: falls, rises and the zero in July all match the balance chart.

  3. C8

  1. Use the example as a template: new = round total − points carried in, then carry half of new.

  2. Do the same for the two finalists:

  3. In the final, Indigo scores I and Orange scores O:

    38 + I > 41 + O
    I − O > 3
    scores are even, so I − O is even at least 4
    Check Winning by 3 is impossible: two even numbers always differ by an even number.
