1(Q2 in the original paper) Calendars & rotationsYour attempt: D ✗

Two neighbours work for the same company and share the journey to work, driving alternately in strict rotation1. They work Monday to Friday each week and every other Saturday2. (They always work the same Saturdays as each other.)

What is the maximum3 number of days either of them has to drive in a calendar month4?

  1. A11
  2. B12
  3. C13
  4. D14
  5. E15

Reading it properly

  • 1Every working day is in the rotation, A, B, A, B… Saturdays included.
  • 2Only alternate Saturdays: 1st, 3rd, 5th… never two in a row.
  • 3Pick the month with the most working days, then give the odd day to whoever starts.
  • 4The longest month has 31 days.

Working it out

  1. Most working days fit in a 31-day month = 4 full weeks + 3 extra days.

    4 weeks 20 weekdays + 4 Saturdays
    best 3 extra days = 3 weekdays (Mon, Tue, Wed) 23 weekdays

  2. Saturdays: that month has 4 Saturdays and they work every other one.

    4 Saturdays work 2 of them
    23 + 2 = 25 working days
    Check Starting the month on a Thursday gives 22 weekdays and 5 Saturdays: they work 3 of them, so 22 + 3 = 25 again. You can never beat 25.

  3. Now share the 25 days in strict rotation, A, B, A, B…

    25 days A gets 13, B gets 12
    maximum = 13
C13 days: 25 working days shared alternately.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 11 is too few: 25 days can only split 13 and 12.
  • B✗ 12 is the other neighbour’s share. It is also what you get if you forget the Saturdays (23 weekdays split 12 and 11).
  • D✗ 14 comes from giving both Saturdays to one driver (12 weekdays + 2 Saturdays). Saturdays are part of the strict rotation, so they alternate too.
  • E✗ 15 would need at least 29 working days. A month never has that many.
Tip Most working days in a month = 23 weekdays + 2 Saturdays = 25. Half of 25 rounded up = 13.
2(Q6 in the original paper) Speed, time & timetablesYour attempt: E ✗

Central Avenue is a long straight road, several kilometres long. Both my home and my office are on Central Avenue, and the diagram below shows their positions, together with two sets of traffic lights that lie in between.

Both sets of lights turn to green for Central Avenue traffic simultaneously1, showing green for exactly two minutes, turning green again exactly two minutes later2.

I cycle to work every morning at a steady speed of 5 metres per second3 (except, of course, whilst waiting at traffic lights).

What is the longest4 journey time I can expect the journey to work to take?

  1. A6 minutes
  2. B7 minutes
  3. C8 minutes
  4. D9 minutes
  5. E10 minutes

Reading it properly

  • 1The two sets of lights are linked: they change at the same moment. That limits the waiting.
  • 2One cycle = 2 min green, 2 min red, then repeat (4 minutes).
  • 35 m every second, so divide each distance by 5 to get seconds.
  • 4Worst case, but it must be a case that can really happen.

Working it out

  1. Riding time first (no waiting):

    400 ÷ 5 = 80 s
    900 ÷ 5 = 180 s = 3 min
    500 ÷ 5 = 100 s
    total = 360 s = 6 min of riding

  2. Worst luck at the first lights: arrive just as they turn red. Wait the full 2 minutes. You set off again at the exact moment both lights go green.

  3. The next 900 m takes exactly 3 minutes, so you reach lights 2 three minutes into their cycle:

    green 0 → 2 min, red 2 → 4 min
    you arrive at 3 min red, so wait 1 minute
    Check If you sail through lights 1 on green instead, you still reach lights 2 at most 1 minute before they go green. So the most waiting possible is 2 + 1 = 3 minutes.

  4. Picture the worst case on a distance–time graph (flat bits = waiting):

    6 min riding + 2 min + 1 min waiting = 9 minutes
D9 minutes: 6 riding + 2 waiting at the first lights + 1 waiting at the second.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 6 minutes is the riding time with no waiting at all, which is the shortest journey.
  • B✗ 7 minutes only adds one short wait.
  • C✗ 8 minutes waits at the first lights but forgets the second set.
  • E✗ 10 minutes waits the full 2 minutes at both lights. That can’t happen, because they change together and you arrive at lights 2 halfway through red.
Tip Ride 1800 m at 5 m/s = 6 min. Max wait 2 min at lights 1; you then reach lights 2 three minutes into the cycle, so 1 more. 6 + 2 + 1 = 9.
3(Q7 in the original paper) Tables & rulesYour attempt: E ✗

The United States Postal Service has an online guide to help people using their postal service. This is an extract:

First Class Mail

First class mail must be used for handwritten or personal correspondence.

First Class Mail Rates – single piece rates

First 30 grams Letter size mail$0.44
Each additional 30 grams or part thereof1$0.17 up to 360 grams
First 30 grams Parcel$1.22
Each additional 30 grams or part thereof$0.17
Standard Postcard$0.28

Letters: 30 cm long, 16 cm wide, 1 cm thick, maximum weight 360 grams2. (Any item larger than this must be sent as a parcel3).

Parcels: Dimensions cannot exceed 1250 cm.

I want to send two items through the post using first class mail. One is 50 cm long, 25 cm wide, 10 cm thick and weighs 300 grams4. The other is 20 cm long, 12 cm wide, 0.20 cm thick and weighs 110 grams5.

How much will it cost me to send the items?

  1. A$2.92
  2. B$3.53
  3. C$3.70
  4. D$4.04
  5. E$4.48

Reading it properly

  • 1“Or part thereof” = any bit of a chunk counts as a whole chunk. Round up.
  • 2Check each item against the letter limits before choosing a price.
  • 3Only items too big for a letter are parcels.
  • 4Item 1: way bigger than 30 × 16 × 1, so it’s a parcel.
  • 5Item 2: 20 × 12 × 0.2 fits inside 30 × 16 × 1, so it’s a letter!

Working it out

  1. Sort each item: letter or parcel?

  2. Parcel, 300 g: the first 30 g is one price, the rest goes in 30 g chunks.

    300 − 30 = 270 g left
    270 ÷ 30 = 9 chunks 9 × $0.17 = $1.53
    $1.22 + $1.53 = $2.75
    Tip 9 × 0.17: do 10 × 0.17 = 1.70, then take one 0.17 off, giving 1.53.

  3. Letter, 110 g: same idea, but watch the rounding.

    110 − 30 = 80 g left
    80 ÷ 30 = 2.67 part of a chunk counts 3 chunks
    $0.44 + 3 × $0.17 = $0.44 + $0.51 = $0.95
  4. $2.75 + $0.95 = $3.70
C$3.70: parcel $2.75 + letter $0.95.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ $2.92 is too low. You would have to leave out most of the letter’s cost.
  • B✗ $3.53 rounds the letter’s 80 g down to 2 chunks ($0.78). “Or part thereof” means round up.
  • D✗ $4.04 overcharges the parcel (11 chunks instead of 9).
  • E✗ $4.48 charges the small item as a parcel ($1.22 + 3 × 0.17 = $1.73). It fits the letter size, so it is a letter.
Tip Letter or parcel first! Parcel 1.22 + 9 × 0.17 = 2.75. Letter 0.44 + 3 × 0.17 = 0.95. Total 3.70.
4(Q8 in the original paper) 3D viewsYour attempt: B ✓

Below is Thomas Leslie Fuller's brass paperweight, which shows his initials.

Which one of the following is not1 a side view of Thomas' paperweight when it is placed flat on a table (either side up)2?

  1. Astrip A
  2. Bstrip B
  3. Cstrip C
  4. Dstrip D
  5. Estrip E

Reading it properly

  • 1Find the odd one out: four strips are real views, one is impossible.
  • 2Lying flat, you can look at it from 4 sides. Turning it over flips each view back to front.

Working it out

  1. Lay the paperweight flat and draw the front face on a 10 × 10 grid. Then look at it from each of the four sides. Wherever the nearest edge changes, you see a line in the side view.

  2. Measure the five strips (each is 10 units long):

    A = 1 | 2 | 1 | 5 | 1 ✓ from below
    C = 3 | 1 | 6 ✓ from above, flipped over
    D = 2 | 2 | 2 | 4 ✓ from the right
    E = 1 | 5 | 1 | 2 | 1 ✓ from below, flipped over
    B = 2 | 4 | 1 | 3 ✗ matches nothing!

  3. So the one that is not a side view is B.

    Check “Either side up” means the mirror image of each view counts too, which is why C and E are allowed.
BStrip B (2-4-1-3) does not match any side.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is the view from below: crossbar, T stem, crossbar, the L + F base, the F’s middle arm.
  • C✗ C is the view from above (flipped): the top bar, the slot, then the F’s top.
  • D✗ D is the view from the right: F’s top arm, stem, middle arm, stem.
  • E✗ E is A flipped over (the view from below with the paperweight upside down).
Tip Count sections: below 5, above 3, left 2, right 4. Only one view has 4 sections (2-2-2-4), so a 2-4-1-3 strip is impossible.

David is planning a holiday for himself and his wife1. He has a budget of £2,000 but wishes to retain about £6002 for spending money. Below is a table showing different holiday possibilities.

  2. BTwelve nights in Cyprus.
  3. CTwelve days and nights in Scotland.
  4. DTwelve nights in Rome travelling by plane.
  5. ESeven nights in Paris travelling by plane.

Reading it properly

  • 12 people! Anything “per person” must be doubled.
  • 2£2000 − £600 = aim to spend about £1400.
  • 3Per person and per night: × 2 people × number of nights.
  • 4“For 2” / “per room” prices are already for both of them, so don’t double them.

Working it out

  1. Target: spend about £1,400 (so £600 is left). Price each holiday, doubling every “per person” cost:

    A Moscow: 2 × 250 + 10 × 120 = 500 + 1200 = 1700 left £300
    B Cyprus: 2 × 195 + 12 × (2 × 42.50) = 390 + 12 × 85 = 390 + 1020 = 1410 left £590
    C Scotland: 12 × 40 + 12 × 80 = 480 + 960 = 1440 left £560
    D Rome: 2 × 110 + 12 × 120 = 220 + 1440 = 1660 left £340
    E Paris: 2 × 80 + 7 × 180 = 160 + 1260 = 1420 left £580
    Tip 12 × 85: 10 × 85 = 850, 2 × 85 = 170, total 1020.

  2. Which is closest to £600?

    B is £10 away, E £20 away, C £40 away B
BTwelve nights in Cyprus leaves £590, the closest to £600.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Moscow costs £1,700, leaving only £300.
  • C✗ Scotland leaves £560: close, but £40 off. (If you forget to double the Cyprus hotel, Cyprus looks like it leaves £1,100 and you end up here.)
  • D✗ Rome costs £1,660, leaving £340.
  • E✗ Paris leaves £580, £20 off. Careful: the Paris hotel is £180 a night, not £120.
Tip Rough first: Moscow (1,700) and Rome (1,660) are way over, so they’re out. Exact for B, C, E only: 590, 560, 580. B wins.
6(Q13 in the original paper) ProportionYour attempt: A ✓

The table below shows the energy values for the main foods used by volunteer wardens at a wild bird reserve to prepare a variety of feed mixes.

FoodEnergy per 100 g1 (calories)
mealworms150
peanuts560
apples350
oats370
niger seed480
cheese400
sunflower seeds4500
raisins300
sunflower hearts600
suet800

A trainee volunteer has been asked to prepare a sample of a special mix to provide exactly 5,000 calories2. He has weighed out the first four ingredients but cannot read the required mass of the final ingredient because of an ink blot. This is what has been weighed out so far:

mealworms 150 g, apple 150 g, raisins 250 g, suet 125 g3

What mass of sunflower seeds must be used to successfully complete the task?

  1. A500 g
  2. B520 g
  3. C540 g
  4. D560 g
  5. E680 g

Reading it properly

  • 1All values are per 100 g, so turn grams into “hundreds of grams” (150 g = 1.5).
  • 2Target total: 5000 calories exactly.
  • 3The four amounts already weighed.
  • 4Seeds (500), not hearts (600): don’t grab the wrong row.

Working it out

  1. Calories so far: (grams ÷ 100) × calories per 100 g.

    mealworms: 1.5 × 150 = 225
    apple: 1.5 × 350 = 525
    raisins: 2.5 × 300 = 750
    suet: 1.25 × 800 = 800 + 200 = 1000
    total so far = 2500
  2. 5000 − 2500 = 2500 still needed
    sunflower seeds: 500 per 100 g 2500 ÷ 500 = 5 lots of 100 g
    = 500 g
A500 g of sunflower seeds.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 520 g would give 2600 calories, making 5100 in total.
  • C✗ 540 g would give 2700, making 5200.
  • D✗ 560 g would give 2800, making 5300.
  • E✗ 680 g would give 3400, making 5900.
Tip 225 + 525 + 750 + 1000 = 2500, so seeds give the other 2500. At 500 per 100 g that’s 500 g.

Sue takes her dog Freya to the park. She throws a stick for Freya to fetch. Freya runs to collect the stick and bring it back to Sue1. While Freya is collecting the stick, Sue walks slowly towards Freya2.

Which one of the following graphs could correctly show the distance between Sue and Freya3?

  1. Agraph A
  2. Bgraph B
  3. Cgraph C
  4. Dgraph D
  5. Egraph E

Reading it properly

  • 1Two parts: out (gap grows), then back (gap shrinks to 0).
  • 2Sue walks towards Freya the whole time: she slows the growing and speeds up the shrinking.
  • 3The graph shows the GAP between them, starting at 0 (they start together).

Working it out

  1. They start together, so the gap starts at 0. Freya brings the stick back to Sue, so it ends at 0. That rules out A, C (start at 20 m) and E (never comes back down).

  2. Now compare the two halves:

  3. Up slowly, down quickly: gentle rise, steep fall B

BGraph B: the gap grows slowly and then shrinks quickly back to 0.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A starts 20 m apart and ends 20 m apart, but they start and finish together.
  • C✗ C also starts at 20 m, but they start together.
  • D✗ D is the wrong way round: fast rise, slow fall. On the way back both are closing the gap, so it must fall faster.
  • E✗ E never returns to 0, but Freya brings the stick back to Sue.

8(Q18 in the original paper) Sequences & timingsYour attempt: B ✗

A main road crosses a side road and the junction is controlled by a set of traffic lights. The normal sequence is for the lights to be green for 25 seconds on the main road1 followed by 15 seconds of green for the side road. There is also a pedestrian phase in the lights when all the road lights are red. If a pedestrian presses a button to cross any of the roads then, after the side road lights have been green, the pedestrian lights turn green for 10 seconds2. Following a pedestrian phase, the main road green period is increased to 35 seconds3 to clear the build-up. Whenever any of the lights change there is a two-second period when all lights are red4. The time on amber is included in the green times given above.

What is the longest5 time traffic on the side road will have to wait for their lights to go green?

  1. A19 seconds
  2. B29 seconds
  3. C45 seconds
  4. D51 seconds
  5. E66 seconds

Reading it properly

  • 1Normal main road green = 25 s.
  • 2The pedestrian phase slots in straight after the side road’s green.
  • 3After pedestrians, the main road gets 35 s, not 25.
  • 4+2 s of all-red at every change. Count every change.
  • 5Longest wait means assume the pedestrian button was pressed.

Working it out

  1. The side road starts waiting the moment its own light turns red. Count everything until it is green again.

  2. normal: 2 + 25 + 2 = 29 s
    with pedestrians: 2 + 10 + 2 + 35 + 2 = 51 s
    Check Every light change adds 2 s. With pedestrians there are three changes before the side road is green again: side → people, people → main, main → side.
D51 seconds, when the pedestrian phase is used.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 19 s (2 + 15 + 2) uses the side road’s own 15 s green, but the side road is waiting for the main road.
  • B✗ 29 s is the normal wait without pedestrians. It isn’t the longest.
  • C✗ 45 s (10 + 35) forgets the three 2-second all-red gaps.
  • E✗ 66 s adds on the side road’s own 15 s of green, which isn’t waiting.
Tip Worst case = pedestrian phase. Write the sequence: 2, 10, 2, 35, 2 → 51.
9(Q19 in the original paper) Calendars & days of the weekYour attempt: D ✓

Elsie has six grandchildren. Their birthdays are as follows:

Ryan19th February3
Rebecca3rd May
Richard30th May
Ralph7th September
Ruth17th November
Robert16th December

Remarkably, in non-leap years the boys' birthdays are on the 50th, 150th, 250th and 350th days of the year, whilst the girls' birthdays are on the 123rd and 321st days1, and all six are on different days of the week.

Which other grandchild's birthday is on the same day of the week as Ryan's in a leap year2?

  1. ARalph
  2. BRebecca
  3. CRichard
  4. DRobert
  5. ERuth

Reading it properly

  • 1Day numbers are given: no need to count through the months!
  • 2A leap year adds 29 February, so every date after it moves one day later in the year.
  • 3Ryan is in February, before the 29th, so he doesn’t move.

Working it out

  1. Two dates fall on the same weekday when the gap between them is a whole number of weeks (a multiple of 7).

  2. In a leap year, Ryan stays on day 50 and everyone else moves up by 1:

  3. Gaps from Ryan’s day 50, then divide by 7:

    Rebecca 124 − 50 = 74 = 70 + 4 ✗
    Richard 151 − 50 = 101 = 98 + 3 ✗
    Ralph 251 − 50 = 201 = 196 + 5 ✗
    Ruth 322 − 50 = 272 = 266 + 6 ✗
    Robert 351 − 50 = 301 = 7 × 43 exactly ✓ Robert
    Tip Quick 7× facts: 7 × 40 = 280, 7 × 43 = 301. Knock off big chunks of 70 or 140 to find remainders fast.
DRobert: in a leap year his birthday is exactly 43 weeks after Ryan’s.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Ralph is 201 days after Ryan: 201 ÷ 7 leaves 5.
  • B✗ Rebecca is 74 days after: leaves 4.
  • C✗ Richard is 101 days after: leaves 3.
  • E✗ Ruth is 272 days after: leaves 6.
Tip Leap year: everyone except Ryan gains 1. Boys’ gaps become 101, 201, 301: only 301 = 7 × 43.
10(Q20 in the original paper) Codes & patternsYour attempt: E ✓

Whenever my friend Alistair writes to me he does so entirely in code. He always uses symbols for letters but changes the code each time1, so any particular symbol does not necessarily stand for the same letter of the alphabet every time.

I always start to crack the code by looking at how he has written his name at the end of his letter. For instance, last time he was:

In my last letter to him I asked him when he intends to visit me next. Today I have received a reply consisting of just one word2 as follows:

When can I assume that he intends to appear?

  1. ATOMORROW
  2. BTHURSDAY
  3. CSATURDAY
  4. DNOVEMBER
  5. ESOMETIME

Reading it properly

  • 1The code changes every time, so last letter’s key is useless now.
  • 2No name this time, so the only clue is the pattern of repeated symbols.

Working it out

  1. Ignore the old key. Number each symbol by when it first appears:

    > ↓ ◊ Σ ∞ ∇ ◊ Σ
    1 2 3 4 5 6 3 4

    So: 8 letters, the 3rd = 7th, the 4th = 8th, all the rest different.

  2. TOMORROW: O appears 3 times ✗
    THURSDAY: no repeats ✗
    SATURDAY: A at 2nd and 7th ✗
    NOVEMBER: E at 4th and 7th ✗
    SOMETIME: M at 3rd and 7th, E at 4th and 8th ✓ E
  3. Trap! The trap: decoding with last time’s key gives S, A, T, ?, R, ?, T, ?, which tempts you towards SATURDAY. But the key has changed, and SATURDAY doesn’t even fit the pattern.
ESOMETIME: it is the only word with the same repeat pattern.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ TOMORROW has O three times (2nd, 4th, 7th) and RR together: wrong pattern.
  • B✗ THURSDAY has no repeated letters, but the reply has two repeats.
  • C✗ SATURDAY repeats A in the 2nd and 7th places, not 3rd/7th and 4th/8th. (It only looks right if you use last time’s key.)
  • D✗ NOVEMBER repeats E in the 4th and 7th places: wrong pattern.
Tip Code changes → use the repeat pattern: positions 3 = 7 and 4 = 8. Only SOMETIME (M…M, E…E).

Mondays – 5 miles
Wednesdays – 5 miles
Saturdays – 8 miles

The table below shows calories burned per mile by walking at various speeds.

  2. B3.0 mph
  3. C4.0 mph
  4. D4.5 mph

Reading it properly

  • 17.5 lb of fat to burn.
  • 2The same walking every week, for 10 weeks.
  • 3Turns pounds into calories: × 3500.
  • 4Only the 180 lb column matters.
  • 5The slowest speed that still burns enough. Careful: the table isn’t in order!

Working it out

  1. Total calories to burn and total miles walked:

    7.5 × 3500 = 24 500 + 1750 = 26 250 calories
    5 + 5 + 8 + 8 = 26 miles a week
    26 × 10 = 260 miles

  2. Calories needed per mile:

    26 250 ÷ 260 260 × 100 = 26 000, with 250 left over
    so just over 100 need about 101 per mile

  3. Now read down the 180 lb column:

    slowest speed with 101 or more = 4.0 mph (102 per mile)
    Check Check: 102 × 260 = 26 520, which is at least 26 250. At 2.5 mph: 98 × 260 = 25 480, which is too few.
C4.0 mph (102 calories per mile at 180 lb).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 2.5 mph burns 98 per mile: 98 × 260 = 25 480, not enough.
