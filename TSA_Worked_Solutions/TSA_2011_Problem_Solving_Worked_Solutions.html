1(Q2 in the original paper) Making totalsAnswer C

To play a football pools game, participants must select four matches1 from the fifty on the coupon. Points are scored depending on the result of each match as follows:

If the home team wins, the participant scores 1.0 point
If the away team wins, the participant scores 1.5 points
If the match is a draw, the participant scores 3.0 points2

Which of the following total scores for the four matches is not3 possible?

  1. A8.5 points
  2. B9.0 points
  3. C9.5 points
  4. D10.0 points
  5. E10.5 points

Reading it properly

  • 1Exactly 4 results get added up. No more, no fewer.
  • 2Only three scores exist: 1, 1.5 and 3.
  • 3Four of the totals can be made. Hunt for the one that can’t.

Working it out

  1. The options are all big (8.5 to 10.5), and the most you can get is 4 × 3 = 12. Big totals need lots of draws, so sort by how many draws. Each group gives a short, complete list:

    4 draws 12
    3 draws 9 + one more match (1 or 1.5) 10 or 10.5
    2 draws 6 + two more (1 + 1, 1 + 1.5 or 1.5 + 1.5) 8, 8.5 or 9
    1 draw 3 + three more (3 to 4.5) at most 7.5
    0 draws at most 4 × 1.5 = 6

  2. Put every possible total on a number line:

    9.5 is missing from every list C
    Check Why not 9.5? With 3 draws you are already at 9 and the last match adds at least 1. With 2 draws you are at 6 and the other two matches add at most 3. So you jump from 9 straight to 10.
C9.5 points: two draws reach at most 9, three draws give at least 10.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 8.5 = 3 + 3 + 1 + 1.5 (two draws, a home win and an away win).
  • B✗ 9.0 = 3 + 3 + 1.5 + 1.5 (two draws and two away wins).
  • D✗ 10.0 = 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 (three draws and a home win).
  • E✗ 10.5 = 3 + 3 + 3 + 1.5 (three draws and an away win).
Tip Three draws give 10 or 10.5; two draws give 8, 8.5 or 9. Nothing lands on 9.5.

2(Q6 in the original paper) Reading tablesAnswer E

To encourage themselves to lose weight, Alma, Brigit, Cara, Danni and Evelyn each put £10 into a pot. They weighed themselves when they began and again at the end of each week. The one who had lost most weight after 4 weeks1 won the £50. The following chart shows their progress:

AlmaBrigitCaraDanniEvelyn
Starting weight271kg67kg63½kg66½kg69kg
Weight after week 167kg68kg62kg67kg66kg
Weight after week 263½kg66½kg59kg62kg63½kg
Weight after week 361kg366kg58kg61½kg62kg
Weight after week 464kg62kg57½kg60kg61kg

Who won the £50?

  1. AAlma
  2. BBrigit
  3. CCara
  4. DDanni
  5. EEvelyn

Reading it properly

  • 1Compare the start with week 4 only. The weeks in between don’t count.
  • 2Just two rows matter: the first and the last.
  • 3Alma was 10 kg down after week 3, but then put weight back on. Tempting trap!

Working it out

  1. Weight lost = starting weight − weight after week 4:

    Alma: 71 − 64 = 7 kg
    Brigit: 67 − 62 = 5 kg
    Cara: 63½ − 57½ = 6 kg
    Danni: 66½ − 60 = 6½ kg
    Evelyn: 69 − 61 = 8 kg
  2. most weight lost after 4 weeks = 8 kg Evelyn
    Trap! Alma had lost the most by week 3 (10 kg), which is why A looks good. But the prize is for weight lost after 4 weeks, and she put 3 kg back on.
EEvelyn: 69 kg down to 61 kg, a loss of 8 kg.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Alma lost 10 kg by week 3 but put 3 kg back on, so only 7 kg after 4 weeks.
  • B✗ Brigit lost 5 kg, the least of all five.
  • C✗ Cara is the lightest at the end, but the prize is for weight lost: she lost 6 kg.
  • D✗ Danni lost 6½ kg, less than Evelyn’s 8 kg.
Tip First row minus last row: 7, 5, 6, 6½, 8. Evelyn wins.

4(Q8 in the original paper) Distance–time graphsAnswer D

A man drives off from his house and travels through the village at a constant speed1. As he leaves the village behind, his speed increases to a new constant value2 which he maintains until he reaches a steep hill which slows him down3. At the top of the hill the driver stops for a few minutes4 to admire the view. He realises that he has left his picnic basket at home and so drives rapidly back along his route5 to collect it.

Which one of the following graphs best represents the driver's distance from home6 throughout his journey?

  1. Agraph A
  2. Bgraph B
  3. Cgraph C
  4. Dgraph D
  5. Egraph E

Reading it properly

  • 1Steady speed = straight sloping line.
  • 2Faster = steeper.
  • 3Slower = less steep.
  • 4Not moving = flat line.
  • 5Heading home: the distance falls, quickly, all the way back to 0.
  • 6He starts at home, so the graph starts at 0, and it ends at 0 when he gets back.

Working it out

  1. Sketch what the graph has to look like, one stage at a time:

  2. Now knock out the graphs that break a rule:

    • A keeps going up at the end: he never comes home. ✗
    • C starts above 0 with a flat bit: he would be standing still away from home. ✗
    • E has no flat part: no stop at the top of the hill. ✗
    • B goes gentle, then even gentler, then steep: he slows down after the village and speeds up on the hill. Backwards! ✗
    • D: gentle, steep, gentle, flat, then down to 0. ✓
    answer = D
DGraph D: gentle, steeper, gentler, flat, then a quick return to 0.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A never comes back down: he would still be driving away from home at the end.
  • B✗ B gets less steep after the village and steeper on the hill: the wrong way round.
  • C✗ C starts above 0, but he sets off from his house.
  • E✗ E has no flat section, so there is no stop to admire the view.
Tip Starts at 0, has a flat bit, ends at 0: only B and D. Speed goes up after the village, so the 2nd slope is steeper: D.

5(Q12 in the original paper) Best buyAnswer A

Jars of Bestcaff coffee weighing 200g are normally £2.501 at Kostless and Savemore, but both supermarkets have a special offer this week:

KOSTLESS
BESTCAFF
80p OFF2		SAVEMORE
BESTCAFF
25% EXTRA FREE3

By how much per kilogram4 is Kostless's offer better value for money than Savemore's?

  1. A£1.50 per kg
  2. B£4.00 per kg
  3. C£7.00 per kg
  4. D£8.50 per kg
  5. E£16.00 per kg

Reading it properly

  • 1Normal jar: 200 g for £2.50.
  • 2Same 200 g, but 80p cheaper: £1.70.
  • 3Same £2.50, but 25% more coffee in the jar.
  • 4Compare the price of 1 kg (1000 g) at each shop.

Working it out

  1. What do you actually get at each shop?

    Kostless: 200 g for £2.50 − £0.80 = £1.70
    Savemore: 25% of 200 g = 50 g extra 250 g for £2.50

  2. Scale each jar up to 1 kg:

    Tip Choose the multiplier that turns each jar into 1000 g: 200 × 5 and 250 × 4. Much easier than finding a price per gram.
  3. £10.00 − £8.50 = £1.50 per kg
A£1.50 per kg: £8.50 a kilo at Kostless against £10.00 a kilo at Savemore.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ £4.00 is just the 80p discount scaled up to a kilogram (0.80 × 5). It ignores Savemore’s offer.
  • C✗ £7.00 isn’t the gap between the two kilo prices (£10.00 and £8.50). It would need Savemore to charge £15.50 a kilo.
  • D✗ £8.50 is Kostless’s price for a kilogram, not the difference between the shops.
  • E✗ £16.00 is far too big: it is more than either shop charges for a whole kilogram.
Tip Kostless £1.70 × 5 = £8.50 per kg. Savemore 250 g for £2.50, × 4 = £10 per kg. Difference £1.50.

7(Q14 in the original paper) 3D viewsAnswer C

There are seven books on my shelf, which are of various sizes1. When I look at them straight on I see the following picture:

I now look at them from the left-hand end (in the direction of the arrow)2.

Which one of the following diagrams is not3 a possible view when seen from the left-hand end?

  1. Aview A
  2. Bview B
  3. Cview C
  4. Dview D
  5. Eview E

Reading it properly

  • 1The picture only shows heights and thicknesses. How deep each book is (front to back) is unknown, so lots of views could happen.
  • 2From the end, a book hides everything behind it, except bits that stick up higher or out further.
  • 3Four views can happen. Find the one that can’t.

Working it out

  1. Number the books from the end you are looking from, and note their heights:

  2. The rule for an end view: book 1 is nearest, so its whole end shows. Every other book only shows where it sticks out above or beyond all the books in front of it.

    Book 2 (height 4) has only book 1 (height 3) in front of it. So book 2 always sticks up above book 1, and its top edge must appear as a line at height 4 starting right at the left edge of the view.

  3. Look for that line in each option. A, B, D and E all have it. C does not:

    In C the left block goes straight from book 1’s top (3) to a top at 5. The only height-4 shape is a thin strip standing further back, which would mean book 2 is hidden at the front. Impossible.

    not possible = C
CView C: book 2’s top edge (height 4) must show from the left edge, and C has no such line.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Possible: books 1 and 2 are equally deep, book 3 is deep enough to hide books 4, 5 and 7, and tall book 6 pokes up above.
  • B✗ Possible: books 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 each reach further back than the books in front of them, so you see a staircase of edges; books 5 and 7 are hidden.
  • D✗ Possible: like B, but books 2 and 3 are equally deep (so book 2’s top is a line inside book 3) and book 4 peeks out behind book 3.
  • E✗ Possible: the books get deeper and deeper towards the far end, so all seven show, and book 7 peeks out behind book 6.
Tip Book 2 is taller than book 1 and nothing else is in front of it, so its top must show as a line from the left edge at height 4. Only C misses it.

8(Q18 in the original paper) Digits & datesAnswer D

Dates may be written in an eight digit form. For instance, 19 January 2005 may be written 19-01-2005.

In what year will the next date1 occur for which all eight digits are different2?

  1. A2013
  2. B2134
  3. C2145
  4. D2345
  5. E2456

Reading it properly

  • 1This paper is from 2011, so look forward from then. Every year until 2999 starts with a 2.
  • 28 different digits from 0–9: only two digits are left out altogether.

Working it out

  1. The year starts with 2, so 2 can’t appear in the day or month.

  2. Month (01 to 12): 02 and 12 contain a 2, 11 repeats. 01 and 10 use both 0 and 1, and then the day could only start with 3: 30 or 31, which need a 0 or 1 again. ✗

    So the month is 0? (03 to 09). It uses the 0.

  3. Day must start with 0, 1, 2 or 3. The 0 and 2 are taken, so the day is 1? (13 to 19) or 31. Either way it uses the 1.

  4. So the day, month and the year’s first digit always use 0, 1 and 2. The other three year digits must come from 3–9. The smallest is 3, 4, 5:

    e.g. 17-06-2345 1, 7, 0, 6, 2, 3, 4, 5 all different ✓
    earliest year = 2345
D2345 (for example 17-06-2345).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 2013 uses 0 and 1, but the month needs a 0 or a 1. No month fits.
  • B✗ 2134 uses 1, 2 and 3. The month must then be 0?, and the day has to start with 0, 1, 2 or 3: all taken.
  • C✗ 2145 uses 1. The month takes the 0, and the day can’t start with 0, 1 or 2; starting with 3 means 30 or 31, which repeat 0 or 1.
  • E✗ 2456 does work (e.g. 17-08-2456), but 2345 comes first.
Tip Day and month together always need a 0 and a 1, and the year starts with 2. So the rest of the year uses 3 upwards: 2345.

11(Q24 in the original paper) Speed, time & routesAnswer C

As part of a journey, I must travel from Bowbridge to Downland. I can choose either the motorway (known locally as 'the road to hell') or the old road known as Buttercup Way. On the motorway I average 60 mph1, on the old road I average 40 mph2. I can change from one to the other at Clatterton3 where they cross.

The distances by the two roads are as follows:

MotorwayButtercup Way
Bowbridge / Clatterton35 miles30 miles
Clatterton / Downland35 miles20 miles

If I take the fastest route4, what will be the time saving over the slowest route5?

  1. A5 min
  2. B10 min
  3. C15 min
  4. D20 min
  5. E30 min

Reading it properly

  • 160 mph = 1 mile a minute. Miles = minutes!
  • 240 mph = 2 miles every 3 minutes, so 1.5 minutes per mile.
  • 3You can swap roads half way, so there are 4 routes, not 2.
  • 4Best choice on each leg.
  • 5Worst choice on each leg.

Working it out

  1. Time for each leg on each road:

    motorway: 35 miles at 60 mph = 35 min (both legs)
    Buttercup, leg 1: 30 miles × 1.5 = 45 min
    Buttercup, leg 2: 20 miles × 1.5 = 30 min

  3. All four routes:

    motorway + motorway = 35 + 35 = 70 min
    motorway + Buttercup = 35 + 30 = 65 min fastest
    Buttercup + motorway = 45 + 35 = 80 min slowest
    Buttercup + Buttercup = 45 + 30 = 75 min
    saving = 80 − 65 = 15 min
C15 minutes: 65 minutes (motorway then Buttercup Way) against 80 minutes (the other way round).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 5 min compares only all-motorway (70) with all-Buttercup (75), forgetting you can switch at Clatterton.
  • B✗ 10 min compares the fastest route with all-Buttercup (75), or all-motorway with the slowest (80). It misses one of the mixed routes.
  • D✗ 20 min is the difference in miles (70 miles all-motorway against 50 miles all-Buttercup), not in time.
  • E✗ 30 min is just the time for the 20-mile stretch of Buttercup Way, not a saving.
Tip Leg 1: 35 v 45, leg 2: 35 v 30. Best minus worst = (45 − 35) + (35 − 30) = 10 + 5 = 15.

12(Q25 in the original paper) Shapes & overlapsAnswer B

Various shapes may be made by overlapping two transparent squares1, for example the dark shaded hexagon2 shown below:

Which one of the following shapes cannot3 be made as the overlap of two transparent squares?

  1. Ashape A
  2. Bshape B
  3. Cshape C
  4. Dshape D
  5. Eshape E

Reading it properly

  • 1Every edge of the overlap is part of an edge of one of the two squares.
  • 2Count the sides: 6, though one is very short (on the left edge). Overlaps can have lots of sides.
  • 3Four shapes can be made. Find the impossible one.

Working it out

  1. Two facts about the overlap of two squares:

    • Its sides lie along the squares’ sides. Each square’s sides point in only 2 directions, at right angles.
    • Each corner is either a corner of a square (a right angle) or a point where the two squares’ edges cross.

    A, C, D and E are all easy to build:

    E is the same idea as C, with one square’s corner poking a bit further in, giving 5 sides.

  2. Now B. It has no right angles and two pairs of parallel sides. Parallel sides from the two different squares would make the squares line up, giving a rectangle. So the two slanting sides must be opposite sides of one square, and the two flat sides opposite sides of the other.

  3. But a square is as long as it is wide. B’s slanting sides are much longer than the gap between them, so they can’t both fit on one square:

    cannot be made = B
    Check This works for any slanted parallelogram: at least one pair of sides is always longer than the gap between them. Only rectangles (and squares) can be made.
BShape B: a slanted parallelogram can’t be the overlap of two squares.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Possible: put one square on top of the other with the same middle and turn it. The overlap has 8 sides, like A.
  • C✗ Possible: two squares overlapping corner to corner give a 4-sided shape with two right angles, like C.
  • D✗ Possible: one straight edge of a square cutting off the corner of the other gives a right-angled triangle, like D.
  • E✗ Possible: like C, but with one corner reaching further in, so you get 5 sides with two right angles.
Tip B’s two slanting sides are parallel, so they would have to be opposite sides of one square. But they are far longer than the gap between them, and a square is as long as it is wide.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A rises by £1.50 a book, but every book saves £2.
  • B✗ B rises by £3 a book.
  • D✗ D goes down and then up again. Each extra book always adds £2 to the saving.
  • E✗ E is exactly £2 per book with nothing taken off. The fixed postage charge has to come off every bar.
