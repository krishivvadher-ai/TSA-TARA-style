1(Q2 in the original paper) Money & profit

A café owner makes 50 mini pizzas and 50 flapjacks1 for sale during the day. The pizzas are sold for $2.00 each and the flapjacks are sold for $1.00 each. However, by the end of lunchtime there are 10 mini pizzas still unsold and 15 flapjacks unsold2, so the price of each is halved3 for the afternoon. At the end of the day there are still 2 mini pizzas and 1 flapjack left which are given away free4 to the last customer.

If the original 50 mini pizzas and 50 flapjacks had a combined production cost of $30.00, how much profit5 is made on the sale of the mini pizzas and flapjacks for the day?

  1. A$100.00
  2. B$102.50
  3. C$115.00
  4. D$120.00
  5. E$130.00

Reading it properly

  • 1Start with 50 of each.
  • 2These are the ones left over. So 50 − 10 = 40 pizzas and 50 − 15 = 35 flapjacks sold at lunchtime.
  • 3Afternoon prices: pizza $1, flapjack 50c.
  • 4Trap! These 3 are part of the 10 and 15 above, and they earn nothing.
  • 5Profit = money taken − $30 cost. Don’t stop at the takings.

Working it out

  1. Split each food into three groups: sold at full price, sold at half price, given away.

    • Pizzas: 40 full price, 10 − 2 = 8 half price, 2 free.
    • Flapjacks: 35 full price, 15 − 1 = 14 half price, 1 free.

  2. Add up the money taken:

    pizzas: 40 × $2 + 8 × $1 = $80 + $8 = $88
    flapjacks: 35 × $1 + 14 × 50c = $35 + $7 = $42
    takings = $88 + $42 = $130
    Tip 14 × 50c: half of 14 = 7, so $7.

  3. Now take off the cost:

    $130 − $30 = $100
A$100: takings of $130 minus the $30 cost.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ $102.50 charges for the free food: it sells all 10 pizzas and all 15 flapjacks in the afternoon ($10 + $7.50).
  • C✗ $115 forgets to halve the prices: 48 pizzas at $2 + 49 flapjacks at $1 = $145, then − $30.
  • D✗ $120 sells all 100 items at full price ($150 − $30). It ignores the leftovers and the price cut.
  • E✗ $130 is the money taken. It forgets to take off the $30 cost, so it isn’t the profit.
Tip Sold: 40 × 2 + 8 × 1 + 35 × 1 + 14 × 0.5 = 80 + 8 + 35 + 7 = 130. Profit 130 − 30 = $100.
2(Q6 in the original paper) Measures & diagrams

A picture 40 cm high1 by 30 cm wide2 is to be framed. There will be a mount between the edge of the picture and the frame. This mount will be 6 cm wide at the top and sides, and 9 cm wide at the bottom3. The width of the wood used for the frame is 2 cm4.

What is the overall height5 of the framed picture?

  1. A46 cm
  2. B49 cm
  3. C56 cm
  4. D59 cm
  5. E62 cm

Reading it properly

  • 1The picture’s height: this is the number you build on.
  • 2Not needed! Height only. The width is there to tempt you.
  • 3Top and bottom are different: 6 cm above, 9 cm below. The sides don’t affect the height.
  • 4The wood goes all the way round, so it adds 2 cm at the top and 2 cm at the bottom.
  • 5Measure from the very top of the frame to the very bottom.

Working it out

  1. Imagine it hanging on the wall. Go from the top edge down to the bottom edge and list every layer you pass through.

  2. wood + mount + picture + mount + wood
    2 + 6 + 40 + 9 + 2 = 59 cm
    Trap! Each layer of frame and mount appears twice in a height: once above the picture, once below.
D59 cm: 2 + 6 + 40 + 9 + 2.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 46 cm is the overall width: 2 + 6 + 30 + 6 + 2.
  • B✗ 49 cm uses the picture’s width (30) instead of its height (40): 2 + 6 + 30 + 9 + 2.
  • C✗ 56 cm uses a 6 cm mount at the bottom too, but the bottom mount is 9 cm.
  • E✗ 62 cm uses a 9 cm mount at the top too, but the top mount is 6 cm.
Tip Height only: 40 + 6 + 9 + 2 + 2 = 59.

Below is a summary of all the second-hand cars sold by Bargain Cars during 2012 and 2013:

JanFebMarAprMayJunJulAugSepOctNovDec

Which month of 2013 saw the biggest increase3 in sales compared with the previous month4?

  1. AApril
  2. BJune
  3. CJuly
  4. DSeptember
  5. ENovember

Reading it properly

  • 1All five options are 2013 months, so work in the 2013 row. The 2012 row is a distraction.
  • 2Example: April is compared with March of the same year: 87 → 114.
  • 3Biggest jump up. Falls don’t count, and the highest month isn’t automatically the biggest jump.
  • 4Previous month, not the same month the year before.

Working it out

  1. Only check the five option months. For each, take away the month just before it (2013 row):

    April: 114 − 87 = +27
    June: 106 − 90 = +16
    July: 124 − 106 = +18
    September: 131 − 113 = +18
    November: 92 − 77 = +15

  2. Line the jumps up side by side: April’s is clearly the biggest.

    biggest increase = +27 in April
    Check Even as a percentage, April wins: 27 on 87 is about 31%, the next best is November (15 on 77, about 19%).
AApril: up 27 cars on March (87 → 114).

Why not the others?

  • B✗ June only rises by 16 (90 → 106).
  • C✗ July rises by 18 on June. It is the biggest rise on the year before (88 → 124), which is the wrong comparison.
  • D✗ September has the highest sales of 2013 (131), but it only rose by 18 on August.
  • E✗ November rises by just 15 (77 → 92).
Tip Five subtractions, 2013 row only: Apr 27, Jun 16, Jul 18, Sep 18, Nov 15. April.
4(Q8 in the original paper) Area & rates

Rosie needs to mow her lawn. She has just bought a new lawn mower, and believes she can mow at a rate of 2 m² a minute1. With her old mower, she could only mow the lawn at a rate of 1 m² a minute2. Her lawn is 10 m by 25 m3. There is a patio in one corner which is 2 m by 3 m and which she does not need to mow4.

How much time does she save5 with her new lawn mower?

  1. A122 minutes
  2. B125 minutes
  3. C128 minutes
  4. D244 minutes
  5. E250 minutes

Reading it properly

  • 1New: twice as fast.
  • 2Old: 1 m² a minute, so minutes = square metres.
  • 3Whole lawn = 10 × 25 = 250 m².
  • 4Take the patio off: 2 × 3 = 6 m² less to mow.
  • 5The saving is the difference between the two times, not either time on its own.

Working it out

  1. Sketch the garden and find the grass that actually gets mown:

  2. old time: 244 ÷ 1 = 244 minutes
    new time: 244 ÷ 2 = 122 minutes
    saving: 244 − 122 = 122 minutes
    Tip Twice as fast means the new time is half the old time, so the saving is also half: 244 ÷ 2 = 122.
A122 minutes saved.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 125 forgets the patio: 250 ÷ 2 = 125.
  • C✗ 128 adds the patio on instead of taking it off: 256 ÷ 2 = 128.
  • D✗ 244 is the old mowing time, not the time saved.
  • E✗ 250 is the old time with the patio forgotten.
Tip (250 − 6) = 244 m². Twice as fast saves half the old time: 122 minutes.
5(Q12 in the original paper) Combinations & totals

Tasty Crisps are currently running a promotion in which there is one money-off coupon inside every packet. Some coupons are worth 19p, some are worth 12p and others are worth 7p.

My friend tells me that he has at least one of each value1 and his seven coupons2 have a total value of exactly £13.

How many of my friend’s coupons are worth 19p4?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C3
  4. D4
  5. E5

Reading it properly

  • 1Free information! Put one 19p, one 12p and one 7p aside straight away.
  • 2Exactly 7 coupons, no more, no fewer.
  • 3£1 = 100p.
  • 4Only the number of 19p coupons is wanted, but you’ll find all three.

Working it out

  1. Use the free information first: take one of each out.

    19 + 12 + 7 = 38p used
    left: 4 coupons worth 100 − 38 = 62p

  2. Now find 4 coupons that make 62p. Try different numbers of extra 19p coupons:

    no more 19s: 4 × 12 = 48 at most too small ✗
    one more 19: 62 − 19 = 43 from three 12s or 7s, but 3 × 12 = 36 at most ✗
    two more 19s: 62 − 38 = 24 = 12 + 12 ✓
    three more 19s: 62 − 57 = 5 from one coupon ✗

  3. So he has 1 + 2 = three 19p coupons, 1 + 2 = three 12p coupons and one 7p coupon.

    number of 19p coupons = 3
C3 coupons are worth 19p (3 × 19 + 3 × 12 + 1 × 7 = 100).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ With one 19p, the other six coupons (12p and 7p) would have to make 81p. Even six 12s only make 72p.
  • B✗ With two 19s, the other five coupons must make 62p. Five 7s make 35p and swapping a 7 for a 12 adds 5p, so five coupons make 35, 40, 45, 50, 55 or 60p. Never 62p.
  • D✗ With four 19s (76p), three coupons must make 24p and include a 12 and a 7. 12 + 7 + anything is at least 26p.
  • E✗ With five 19s (95p), only 5p is left for two coupons, but you need a 12p and a 7p.
Tip Remove one of each (38p): 4 coupons make 62p. 19 + 19 + 12 + 12 = 62, so 3 × 19p in total.

7(Q14 in the original paper) Shapes & fitting

A tiler has encountered a problem when tiling a kitchen floor. He is one tile short1 of completing the job. This is indicated by the greyed-out area in the diagram below which shows part of the kitchen floor:

There are some odd pieces of tile left over that could be used to complete the job. Three of these, if combined, would fit the area exactly2.

Which three pieces of tile could be pieced together to fit into the remaining corner area3? (The tiles are shown face up and cannot be laid face down4.)

  1. A1, 2 and 5
  2. B1, 3 and 5
  3. C1, 4 and 5
  4. D2, 3 and 4
  5. E2, 4 and 5

Reading it properly

  • 1The gap is one square tile, minus the bit the pillar stands on.
  • 2“Exactly” = the areas must add up to the gap’s area. Great for ruling options out fast.
  • 3An L shape: the tile with a square bitten out of one corner.
  • 4You may turn a piece round, but you may not flip it over.

Working it out

  1. Look down on the grey tile from above. The pillar’s base covers one corner and is half as wide as the tile. On a grid of small squares (the size of the bumps on the pieces) the gap is an L:

  2. Count the small squares in each piece (each one is a 3 × 3 block with squares added or bitten out):

  3. The three pieces must add up to 27:

    A: 7 + 10 + 10 = 27 ✓
    B: 7 + 13 + 10 = 30 ✗
    C: 7 + 7 + 10 = 24 ✗
    D: 10 + 13 + 7 = 30 ✗
    E: 10 + 7 + 10 = 27 ✓

    Only A and E are left. Try A: pieces 2 and 5 are both “3 × 3 plus a bump”, so they make the two arms of the L. Their bumps poke into the corner square, and piece 1 fits the hole that is left (see the sketch in Step 1).

    1, 2 and 5 fit exactly A
    Trap! Honest warning: 2, 4 and 5 also make 27, and they can be fitted too if every piece is turned half a turn (2 and 5 in the arms with different bump positions, 4 in the corner). So this question really has two fits. The official answer is A, the fit the question was built around. In the exam, once the area check leaves two options, try the first one; as soon as it fits, choose it and move on.
APieces 1, 2 and 5 (7 + 10 + 10 = 27 squares) fit the L-shaped gap.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 1, 3 and 5 make 30 squares, 3 too many. Piece 3 is too big.
  • C✗ 1, 4 and 5 make only 24 squares, 3 too few.
  • D✗ 2, 3 and 4 make 30 squares, too many.
  • E✗ 2, 4 and 5 have the right area (27) and the official key does not accept them. Strictly, they can also be fitted with half turns, so this option is a flaw in the question rather than a mistake you could spot. Go with A.
Tip Gap = 6 × 6 − 3 × 3 = 27 squares. Pieces: 7, 10, 13, 7, 10. Only A and E make 27; A fits (2 and 5 in the arms, 1 in the corner).
8(Q18 in the original paper) Area & rounding up

I am planning to repaint the walls and the ceiling1 of my dining room. The room has the shape of a rectangular box and is 8 m long, 4 m wide and 3 m high2. I do not need to paint the door and the three windows, the combined surface area of which is 10 m²3. Paint is sold in 8-litre tins4 and 1 litre of paint is sufficient for a surface area of 12 m²5.

How many tins6 of paint do I need to buy?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C6
  4. D8
  5. E17

Reading it properly

  • 1Four walls + ceiling. Not the floor.
  • 2Walls: two are 8 × 3, two are 4 × 3. Ceiling: 8 × 4.
  • 3Take this off the total.
  • 4One tin = 8 litres. The answer is in tins, not litres!
  • 5So one tin covers 8 × 12 = 96 m².
  • 6Whole tins only, so round up at the end.

Working it out

  1. Unfold the room: the four walls make one long strip, with the ceiling on top.

  2. walls: 24 × 3 = 72 m²
    ceiling: 8 × 4 = 32 m²
    paint: 72 + 32 − 10 = 94 m²
  3. litres: 94 ÷ 12 = 7.8… 8 litres
    one 8-litre tin covers 96 m² ≥ 94 m² 1 tin
    Tip Quicker: find what one tin covers (8 × 12 = 96 m²) and compare it with 94 m². No division needed.
A1 tin: 94 m² needs 7.8 litres, and one tin holds 8 litres.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 2 tins: you only need that if you paint the floor too (126 m² needs 10.5 litres).
  • C✗ 6 is the litres for the walls alone (72 ÷ 12), and it’s litres, not tins.
  • D✗ 8 is the number of litres needed, not tins.
  • E✗ 17 is all six faces (136 m²) ÷ 8: it paints the floor and mixes up the tin size with the coverage.
Tip Walls 72 + ceiling 32 − 10 = 94 m². One tin covers 8 × 12 = 96 m². So 1 tin.

11(Q24 in the original paper) Percentages & mixtures

A laboratory technician has made up a stock mixture of two chemicals, X and Y, ready for use by all of the groups in Year 9 at a High School. He has mixed 6.0 kg which is 14 of X and 34 of Y1. He realises that he has used the wrong mix and, rather than waste the chemicals he has used so far, he is going to add some more of chemical X2 so that the mix consists of 40% of X and 60% of Y3.

What mass of chemical X must he add4?

  1. A1.0 kg
  2. B1.5 kg
  3. C2.0 kg
  4. D2.5 kg
  5. E3.0 kg

Reading it properly

  • 1X is a quarter of 6 kg = 1.5 kg. Y is three quarters = 4.5 kg.
  • 2Only X is added, so the Y stays at 4.5 kg. That’s the key.
  • 3The new mix: Y must be 60% of the new total.
  • 4The extra X, not the final amount of X.

Working it out

  1. X = 14 × 6 = 1.5 kg
    Y = 34 × 6 = 4.5 kg

  2. Y doesn’t change, so use it to find the new total:

    60% of new total = 4.5 kg
    10% = 4.5 ÷ 6 = 0.75 kg
    100% = 7.5 kg
  3. added X = 7.5 − 6.0 = 1.5 kg
    check: X = 1.5 + 1.5 = 3.0 kg, and 3.0 ÷ 7.5 = 40% ✓
    Tip Use the thing that stays the same (Y) to find the new total. Adding X changes X’s amount and the total, so X itself is awkward to work with.
B1.5 kg of X: the mix becomes 3 kg X and 4.5 kg Y (40% and 60% of 7.5 kg).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Adding 1.0 kg gives 2.5 kg X in 7 kg, about 36%, not 40%.
  • C✗ Adding 2.0 kg gives 3.5 kg X in 8 kg, about 44%.
  • D✗ Adding 2.5 kg gives 4 kg X in 8.5 kg, about 47%.
  • E✗ 3.0 kg is how much X the final mix holds, not how much is added. Adding 3 kg would make X 50%.
Tip Y fixed at 4.5 kg = 60%, so total = 7.5 kg. Add 7.5 − 6 = 1.5 kg.
12(Q25 in the original paper) Scoring & rules

Five friends wanted to go on holiday together, but they found it difficult to decide where to go. Jo and Mel both wanted to go to Portugal, with France as their second choice.   2. Barrangement B
  3. Carrangement C
  4. Darrangement D
  5. Earrangement E

Reading it properly

  • 1Two identical tables, both the same “L” (a bar of 3 with a foot).
  • 2Four arrangements can be made; find the impossible one.
  • 3The hidden rule: they’re tables. You can turn a table round, but you can’t turn it upside down. So a mirror-image “J” isn’t allowed.

Working it out

  1. Each table covers 4 squares: a bar of 3 with one square sticking out at the end. Here are all the ways one table can sit:

  2. Split each arrangement (8 squares) into two tables:

    • A, B, C and E each split into two L’s, just turned round. ✓
    • D: the end square on the left can only belong to “bar of 3 with a square on top of its right end”. That leaves “bar of 3 with a square on top of its left end”, which is the mirror image. ✗
    impossible arrangement = D
    Check In D, both tables would need their feet pointing towards each other. That only happens if one table is flipped over.
DArrangement D: it needs one table to be the mirror image of the other, which means turning a table upside down.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is one L plus the other L turned half way round: a 2 × 4 rectangle.
  • B✗ B is two L’s each turned a quarter turn, stepped like stairs.
  • C✗ C is one L as bought, plus one turned half way round.
  • E✗ E is one L as bought (bottom) and one turned a quarter turn (top).
Tip Tables can’t be flipped. Split each shape into two 4-square L’s: only D forces an L and a mirror-image J.
