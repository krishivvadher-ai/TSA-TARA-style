1(Q2 in the original paper) Ranges & overlap

Three thermometers are each accurate to within 2 degrees above or below1 the temperature they actually read. One reads 7°, one reads 9° and one reads 10°.

What is the minimum range2 in which the true temperature lies?

  1. A5°–12°
  2. B7°–9°
  3. C8°–10°
  4. D8°–9°
  5. E7°–10°

Reading it properly

  • 1Every thermometer could be up to 2° out either way, so each reading gives a window: reading − 2 to reading + 2.
  • 2All three are accurate, so the true temperature must sit inside all three windows at once. Find the overlap.

Working it out

  1. Turn each reading into a window:

    reads 7° 5° to 9°
    reads 9° 7° to 11°
    reads 10° 8° to 12°

  2. Line the windows up. The true temperature has to be in the bit they share:

  3. bottom: the highest lower limit = 8° (from the 10° thermometer)
    top: the lowest upper limit = 9° (from the 7° thermometer)
    true temperature between 8° and 9°
    Tip Overlap trick: biggest bottom to smallest top. Here 10 − 2 = 8 and 7 + 2 = 9.
D8°–9°: the only temperatures that are within 2° of all three readings.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 5°–12° joins all three windows together. That is where it could be if only one thermometer had to be right.
  • B✗ 7°–9° starts at the lowest reading, but the 10° thermometer says it can’t be below 8°.
  • C✗ 8°–10° forgets the 7° thermometer: the temperature can’t be above 7 + 2 = 9°.
  • E✗ 7°–10° just runs from the lowest reading to the highest. It never uses the ±2.
Tip Lowest it can be: 10 − 2 = 8°. Highest it can be: 7 + 2 = 9°. So 8°–9°.
2(Q6 in the original paper) Dates & digits

On only 360 occasions during the twentieth century was it possible to write the date in the form DD/MM/YYYY using eight different digits1. One example was 28th July 1956 (28/07/1956).

Eleven of Arthur's birthdays2 have been such dates (though not his date of birth), and in 1974 and 1983 the two digits that did not form part of the date made up his age at the time3.

In which year was Arthur born?

  1. A1909
  2. B1916
  3. C1918
  4. D1936
  5. E1938

Reading it properly

  • 1DD/MM/YYYY has 8 digits. All different means 8 of the ten digits 0–9 are used once, and 2 are left over.
  • 2A birthday is the same day and month every year. That links 1974 and 1983 together.
  • 3The 2 spare digits make his age, e.g. 3 and 8 make 38.

Working it out

  1. First, what must every such date look like? The year is 19__, so 1 and 9 are used.

    • Month: 10, 11, 12 and 01 reuse the 1, and 09 reuses the 9. So the month is 0m, and 0 is used too.
    • Day: it can’t start with 0 or 1 (used), and 30 or 31 reuse 0 or 1. So the day is 2d.
    every date = 2d / 0m / 19YY 1, 9, 0, 2 always used

  2. Now list the spare digits for each year (d, m and the age share them):

    1974: used 1, 9, 7, 4, 0, 2 free 3, 5, 6, 8
    1983: used 1, 9, 8, 3, 0, 2 free 4, 5, 6, 7
    same birthday, so d and m are in both lists 5 and 6

    So his birthday is 25/06 (or 26/05), and the leftover digits make his age:

  3. 1974: 3 and 8 38 (83 is silly: then 1983 would make him 92)
    1983: 4 and 7 47 ✓ exactly 9 years older
    born 1974 − 38 = 1936
    Check Years using two of 3, 4, 7, 8 after 1936 are 1937, 38, 43, 47, 48, 73, 74, 78, 83, 84, 87. That is eleven birthdays ✓. And 25/06/1936 repeats the 6, so his date of birth doesn’t count ✓.
D1936: his birthday is 25/06 (or 26/05), and he was 38 in 1974 and 47 in 1983.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1909 makes him 65 then 74. In 1974 the date would need 3 and 8, in 1983 it would need 5 and 6: two different birthdays.
  • B✗ 1916 makes him 58 then 67. The date would need 3 and 6 in 1974 but 4 and 5 in 1983: not the same birthday.
  • C✗ 1918 makes him 56 then 65. The date would need 3 and 8 in 1974 but 4 and 7 in 1983.
  • E✗ 1938 makes him 36 then 45. The date would need 5 and 8 in 1974 but 6 and 7 in 1983.
Tip Dates are always 2d/0m/19YY. Spare digits: 1974 → 3, 5, 6, 8; 1983 → 4, 5, 6, 7. Same birthday, so d and m are 5 and 6; ages 38 and 47; born 1936.
3(Q7 in the original paper) Tables & rules

In order to qualify for a bonus, employees must fulfil certain criteria:

£1,000 bonusAbsences1 less than2 5%
Production target exceeded by at least 10%
Rejects3 are less than 5% of output
£500 bonusAbsences less than 10%
Production targets met4
Rejects are less than 8% of output

Workers performed as follows:

SmithJonesPatelOwololuMcKay
Attendance (%)95901009697
Over production target (%)+5+6+120-4
Product accepted (%)9896959396

Who qualifies for a bonus5?

  1. ANobody
  2. BSmith
  3. CPatel and Smith
  4. DOwololu, Patel and Smith
  5. EJones, Owololu, Patel and Smith

Reading it properly

  • 1The table gives attendance, the rules talk about absences: absence = 100 − attendance.
  • 2“Less than” is strict. Exactly 5% is not less than 5%.
  • 3Same switch: rejects = 100 − product accepted.
  • 4Met = 0 or more over target. Exactly on target (0) counts!
  • 5Either bonus counts, the £500 one too.

Working it out

  1. Flip the table into the rules’ language first:

    absent % = 100 − attendance: 5, 10, 0, 4, 3
    rejects % = 100 − accepted: 2, 4, 5, 7, 4

  2. Test the easier £500 rules (absent under 10, target met, rejects under 8):

  3. Does anyone get the bigger £1,000 instead? It doesn’t change the answer, but it shows the traps:

    Smith: absent exactly 5 (not less than 5), and only +5 no
    Patel: absent 0, +12, but rejects exactly 5 (not less than 5) no
    so nobody gets £1,000, but three get £500 Owololu, Patel and Smith
    Trap! Owololu’s 0 means he hit the target exactly. “Targets met” is satisfied.
DOwololu, Patel and Smith: all three meet every £500 rule.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Nobody gets £1,000, but three people get the £500. Don’t forget the smaller bonus.
  • B✗ Misses Patel (only his rejects stop the £1,000, he still gets £500) and Owololu.
  • C✗ Misses Owololu: 0% over target means the target was met exactly, which counts.
  • E✗ Adds Jones, but 90% attendance is 10% absence, and 10 is not less than 10.
Tip Absent = 100 − attendance, rejects = 100 − accepted. £500 needs absent under 10, target 0 or more, rejects under 8: Smith, Patel, Owololu.
4(Q8 in the original paper) Directions & maps

I stopped in a town called Akeland to ask the way to Eksberg. The only sense I could get from a local was as follows:

Akeland is east of Benford.1

Eksberg is north of Cranton.

Ducton is south of Akeland.2

Cranton is west of Ducton.

Assuming that 'east' means, as near as it matters, 'due east', which one of the following can be said with certainty3?

  1. AI need to go due west.
  2. BI need to go to the west, but it may be north-west or south-west.
  3. CI need to go north-west.
  4. DI need to go to the north, but it may also be a bit west.
  5. EI need to go south-west.

Reading it properly

  • 1Benford never connects to the other towns. A red herring.
  • 2The useful chain: Akeland → south to Ducton → west to Cranton → north to Eksberg.
  • 3It must be true whatever the (unknown) distances are.

Working it out

  1. Start where you are, Akeland, and follow the chain, drawing as you go:

  2. No distances are given, so compare the two up-and-down legs:

    north leg shorter than south leg Eksberg is south-west
    north leg equal to south leg due west
    north leg longer north-west

    The only sure thing: Cranton is west of Ducton, so Eksberg is to the west. North or south of that, we can’t tell B

BWest, but it could be north-west or south-west: the north and south legs have unknown lengths.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Due west only if the north leg exactly equals the south leg. We aren’t told the distances.
  • C✗ North-west only if the north leg is the longer one. Not certain.
  • D✗ Eksberg is definitely west, and it could just as well be to the south. “To the north” isn’t certain.
  • E✗ South-west only if the north leg is the shorter one. Not certain.
Tip Akeland → south → west → north. The west part is certain; the north part might be more or less than the south part. So west, maybe NW or SW.
5(Q12 in the original paper) Percentages & interest

I have just joined a savings scheme. I will pay in £50 each month1 and at the end of each complete year I will be paid 5% interest on my average balance2 (i.e. the average of my starting and finishing amount3) for the previous 12 months.

How much will I have in total when my interest has been paid at the end of the second year4? (Answer to the nearest £10.)

  1. A£1230
  2. B£1260
  3. C£1290
  4. D£1380
  5. E£1990

Reading it properly

  • 112 × £50 = £600 paid in each year.
  • 2Interest once a year, and only 5% of the average, not of the final amount.
  • 3Average = (start + finish) ÷ 2. Nice and easy.
  • 4Two rounds of interest. Year 1’s interest is already in the pot when year 2 starts.

Working it out

  1. Year 1: start with nothing.

    start £0, finish 12 × 50 = £600
    average = (0 + 600) ÷ 2 = £300
    interest = 5% of 300 = £15 pot = £615

  2. Year 2: start with the £615.

    start £615, finish 615 + 600 = £1,215
    average = (615 + 1215) ÷ 2 = 1830 ÷ 2 = £915
    interest = 5% of 915 = £45.75
    pot = 1215 + 45.75 = 1260.75 £1260
    Tip 5% = half of 10%. 10% of 915 = 91.50, half of that = 45.75.

  3. Picture it: each year the money ramps up steadily, and interest is paid on the middle height of the ramp.

B£1260: £15 interest in year 1, £45.75 in year 2, on top of £1,200 paid in.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £1230 gives year 2 only £15 interest again, as if it started from £0. Year 2 starts with £615 already saved.
  • C✗ £1290 pays 5% on the finishing balance each year (£30, then £61.50) instead of the average.
  • D✗ £1380 needs £180 of interest, which is 15% of £1,200. Far too much.
  • E✗ £1990 needs about £790 of interest on £1,200 saved. Nothing like 5% a year.
Tip Year 1: average 300 → +15 → 615. Year 2: average (615 + 1215) ÷ 2 = 915 → +45.75 → 1260.75, so £1260.
6(Q13 in the original paper) Percentages & costs

Every Thursday, Marilyn spends 10 hours making 60 cakes1 to sell at her local market. The cost of the ingredients to make each cake is £1.60 and Marilyn charges £6 an hour for her time2. She usually sets the sale price for a cake at 75% more than the total costs of making it3. However, when she sells to her friends she gives them a 10% discount on the normal sale price4.

What do her friends have to pay for one5 of Marilyn's cakes?

  1. A£3.42
  2. B£4.10
  3. C£4.29
  4. D£4.55
  5. E£4.88

Reading it properly

  • 160 cakes in 10 hours = 6 cakes an hour.
  • 2Her time is a cost too: £6 an hour shared by 6 cakes = £1 per cake.
  • 3Total cost = ingredients + time. Then add 75% of that.
  • 4The 10% comes off the sale price, as a separate second step.
  • 5Price of one cake, not the whole batch.

Working it out

  1. Cost of one cake:

    time: 10 h × £6 = £60 for 60 cakes £1 a cake
    ingredients £1.60 total cost = £2.60

  2. Normal price, 75% more:

    75% of 2.60 = half (1.30) + quarter (0.65) = 1.95
    2.60 + 1.95 = £4.55

  3. Friends get 10% off that:

    10% of 4.55 = 0.455
    4.55 − 0.455 = 4.095 £4.10
B£4.10: cost £2.60, plus 75% makes £4.55, minus 10% makes £4.095.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £3.42 adds the 75% to the ingredients only (2.80), then adds the £1 for time and takes 10% off.
  • C✗ £4.29 merges the two changes into +65% (2.60 × 1.65). Take 10% off the new price instead.
  • D✗ £4.55 is the normal price: it forgets the friends’ 10% discount.
  • E✗ £4.88 is more than the full price of £4.55, so it can’t be a discounted price.
Tip Cost = 1.60 + 60 ÷ 60 = 2.60. × 1.75 = 4.55. × 0.9 = 4.095 ≈ £4.10.
7(Q14 in the original paper) Percentage change

A business, facing turbulent times, changed the wages of its workers by the same percentage1 in two successive years, but the changes were in opposite directions2.

Which of the following best describes the wages of the workers after the second change3?

  1. AThe same as the original wage.
  2. BNecessarily higher than the original wage.
  3. CNecessarily lower than the original wage.
  4. DHigher than originally if the first change was an increase, otherwise lower.
  5. EHigher than originally if the first change was a decrease, otherwise lower.

Reading it properly

  • 1Same percentage both years, e.g. 10% and 10%.
  • 2One up, one down. It feels like they cancel out. They don’t!
  • 3Compare the final wage with the original one.

Working it out

  1. Try it with numbers: a £100 wage and 10% changes, both orders.

    up first: 100 → 110 10% of 110 is 11 110 − 11 = 99
    down first: 100 → 90 10% of 90 is 9 90 + 9 = 99

    The second change is a percentage of a different amount, so it doesn’t undo the first one.

  2. Exaggerate to see it clearly: use 50%.

    × 1.5 × 0.5 = × 0.75, and × 0.5 × 1.5 = × 0.75 the order doesn’t matter
    always lower C
    Tip Stuck on a percentage question with no numbers? Pick £100 and a big, easy percentage.
CNecessarily lower: × (1 + p) × (1 − p) is always less than 1.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Only true if the change is 0%. Any real change leaves the wage lower.
  • B✗ It is never higher: the cut comes off the bigger amount, or the rise goes on the smaller amount.
  • D✗ The order doesn’t matter: × 1.1 × 0.9 and × 0.9 × 1.1 are both × 0.99.
  • E✗ Same mistake as D the other way round: both orders end lower.
Tip £100 with ±10%: 110 → 99, or 90 → 99. Lower either way.
8(Q18 in the original paper) Speed & time

Sven is a keen cyclist and is riding from Alvros to Berget. At his normal cycling speed this will take him 30 minutes1. His wife, Helga, will go by car to bring him and his bike back. They leave at the same time, but two thirds of the way2 between the two towns the car breaks down and Helga has to walk the rest of the way. The car goes at three times the speed3 Sven cycles. But Helga walks at only one third of the speed4 he cycles.

Who arrives at Berget first, and by how much5 (to the nearest minute if necessary)?

  1. AHelga by 12 minutes.
  2. BThey arrive at the same time.
  3. CSven by 7 minutes.
  4. DSven by 33 minutes.
  5. ESven by 37 minutes.

Reading it properly

  • 1Sven’s whole ride is 30 minutes, so each third of the road takes him 10 minutes.
  • 2Two thirds by car, the last third on foot.
  • 33 times faster means a third of the time.
  • 43 times slower means 3 times the time. This is what sinks Helga.
  • 5Two things to find: who wins, and the gap.

Working it out

  1. Work in thirds of the road. Sven takes 10 minutes per third.

    Helga by car: 2 thirds = 20 min at Sven’s speed ÷ 3 = 6⅔ min
    Helga on foot: 1 third = 10 min at Sven’s speed × 3 = 30 min
    Helga total = 6⅔ + 30 = 36⅔ min

  2. Draw both journeys against the clock:

    36⅔ − 30 = 6⅔ min ≈ 7 min Sven by 7 minutes
    Trap! “Three times faster” and “three times slower” don’t cancel out. Slow stretches cost far more time than fast ones save.
CSven by 7 minutes: Helga takes 6⅔ + 30 = 36⅔ minutes against his 30.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Helga can’t win: the last third alone takes her 30 minutes, as long as Sven’s whole ride.
  • B✗ Assumes 3 times faster and 3 times slower cancel out. They don’t.
  • D✗ Swaps the fractions: driving one third (3⅓ min) and walking two thirds (60 min) gives 63⅓, which is Sven by 33.
  • E✗ 37 minutes is roughly Helga’s whole journey time (36⅔), not the gap between them.
Tip Each third = 10 min for Sven. Helga: 20 ÷ 3 + 10 × 3 = 6⅔ + 30 = 36⅔. Sven wins by 6⅔ ≈ 7.
9(Q19 in the original paper) Shapes & cutting

I am making a patchwork quilt from leftover pieces of cloth of various shapes and sizes. This pentagonal piece of cloth is rather large, so I want to cut it into two pieces.

Which one of the following combinations is it not1 possible to produce by making one straight cut2 across a pentagonal piece of cloth?

  1. AA triangle and a quadrilateral.
  2. BA triangle and a pentagon.
  3. CA triangle and a hexagon.
  4. DA quadrilateral and a pentagon.
  5. EA quadrilateral and a hexagon.

Reading it properly

  • 1Four of these can happen. Find the one that can’t.
  • 2One straight line, starting and ending on the edge (at a corner or partway along a side).

Working it out

  1. Count the total number of sides of the two pieces. The cut becomes a side of both pieces (+2), and every side it crosses partway along gets split into two (+1 each).

    corner to corner: 5 + 2 = 7 3 + 4
    corner to the middle of a side: 5 + 2 + 1 = 8 3 + 5 or 4 + 4
    middle of a side to middle of a side: 5 + 2 + 2 = 9 3 + 6 or 4 + 5

  2. Sketch each one on the house shape:

  3. quadrilateral + hexagon = 4 + 6 = 10 sides
    more than the 9 maximum E
    Tip Can’t remember the rule? Just doodle cuts on the pentagon: the four possible pairs turn up quickly, and 4 + 6 never does.
EA quadrilateral and a hexagon: that needs 10 sides in total, but one cut makes at most 9.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Possible: cut corner to corner across the bottom of the roof.
  • B✗ Possible: cut from the top corner to partway down one wall.
  • C✗ Possible: snip the top corner off with a short cut across the two roof edges.
  • D✗ Possible: cut straight across both walls.
Tip Total sides after one cut is 7, 8 or 9. 4 + 6 = 10, so E is impossible.
10(Q20 in the original paper) Tables & conditions

Thomas needs to book a place to sit a test next week, but is free only in the mornings1. The test centre runs the test every day from Monday to Friday and holds a morning and an afternoon sitting. Each sitting has a total of 100 places2 available and will take place in the main hall unless there are 30 or fewer candidates, in which case a smaller room is used3. He does not like to take tests in crowded places, so he wants to make sure that he is in the main hall4 and will try to be with the smallest number of other candidates5 possible. The number of places that are still available6 are shown in the table below.

DaySpaces available a.m.Spaces available p.m.
Monday7567
Tuesday4847
Wednesday572
Thursday3910
Friday6346

On which day should he choose to sit the test?

  1. AMonday
  2. BTuesday
  3. CWednesday
  4. DThursday
  5. EFriday

Reading it properly

  • 1Mornings only: ignore the whole p.m. column.
  • 2100 places in each sitting.
  • 3Main hall needs 31 or more candidates, counting Thomas himself.
  • 4Rule 1: it must be the main hall for certain.
  • 5Rule 2: then as few other people as possible.
  • 6These are empty places. People already booked = 100 − spaces.

Working it out

  1. Turn morning spaces into people already booked:

    Mon 100 − 75 = 25
    Tue 100 − 48 = 52
    Wed 100 − 57 = 43
    Thu 100 − 39 = 61
    Fri 100 − 63 = 37

  2. Add Thomas and check the main hall rule (more than 30):

  3. Monday: 25 + 1 = 26 small room ✗
    the rest are main hall; fewest others = Friday, 37 Friday
    Trap! Monday looks perfect (fewest people), but that is exactly why it fails: with so few, the test moves to the small room.
EFriday: 37 others already booked, the fewest of any morning that is sure to be in the main hall.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Monday has only 25 booked, so 26 with Thomas: 30 or fewer means the small room.
  • B✗ Tuesday has 52 others booked, more than Friday’s 37.
  • C✗ Wednesday has 43 others. (Its 2 afternoon spaces don’t matter: he can’t do afternoons.)
  • D✗ Thursday has the fewest spaces, so it is the most crowded morning: 61 others.
Tip Booked = 100 − spaces: 25, 52, 43, 61, 37. Monday (26 with him) is the small room. Smallest of the rest: Friday.
11(Q24 in the original paper) Area & perimeter

Mr Daley has bought some used carpeting for his new 24 m x 12 m1 car showroom. The carpet is in 8 m x 4 m rectangles2 which will be joined using a strip of double-sided tape along all seams3. Additionally, double-sided tape will be needed to stick the carpet around the edges of the showroom4.

What is the minimum5 amount of tape he requires?

  1. A72 m
  2. B108 m
  3. C144 m
  4. D216 m
  5. E288 m

Reading it properly

  • 1Floor: 24 × 12 = 288 m².
  • 2Each piece is 32 m², so 288 ÷ 32 = 9 pieces.
  • 3A seam is where two pieces meet. One strip covers it.
  • 4Plus the outside edge of the room: its perimeter.
  • 5Lay the pieces to keep the seams short. (Spoiler: the layout makes no difference!)

Working it out

  1. Edges first: the perimeter of the showroom.

    24 + 12 + 24 + 12 = 72 m

  2. Lay the pieces neatly: 3 along (3 × 8 = 24) and 3 down (3 × 4 = 12).

    seams running along: 2 lines × 24 m = 48 m
    seams running down: 2 lines × 12 m = 24 m
    seams = 72 m
  3. tape = 72 + 72 = 144 m
    Check Would a cleverer layout use less? No. Each piece has a 24 m perimeter, so 9 pieces have 216 m of edge. The outside takes 72 m; the other 144 m is seams, each counted twice (a piece on each side), so seams are always 72 m.
C144 m: 72 m round the edge plus 72 m of seams.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 72 m is only the edge (or only the seams). He needs both.
  • B✗ 108 m halves all 216 m of piece edges. But the outside edges have a piece on one side only, so they aren’t shared.
  • D✗ 216 m tapes all the way round every piece (9 × 24), which tapes each seam twice.
  • E✗ 288 is the floor area in m², not a length of tape.
Tip Perimeter 72 m + seams (2 × 24 + 2 × 12 = 72 m) = 144 m.
12(Q25 in the original paper) Calendars & counting
WINTER SPECTACULAR
AT
THE PLAYTIME THEATRE

From: Saturday 7th December1
To: Saturday 28th December
Performances nightly at 7 pm (Sun–Fri)2
(Saturdays at 5.30 pm and 8.30 pm)3
Matinees Weds, Thurs and Sat at 2 pm4
Tickets from £8
(Theatre closed 25th December)5

How many times6 will the Winter Spectacular be performed at the Playtime Theatre during its run?

  1. A31
  2. B34
  3. C35
  4. D36
  5. E38

Reading it properly

  • 1The run starts and ends on a Saturday: 7th, 14th, 21st, 28th are all in.
  • 2One evening show each Sunday to Friday.
  • 3Saturdays have 2 evening shows instead of the 7 pm one.
  • 4Plus an afternoon show on Wednesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays.
  • 5The 25th is a Wednesday (7 → 14 → 21 → 28 are Saturdays). You lose its evening show and its matinee.
  • 6Count every performance, matinees included. The ticket price is a distraction.

Working it out

  1. Sketch the calendar. 7th is a Saturday, so the Saturdays are 7, 14, 21, 28 and Christmas Day is a Wednesday.

  2. Or count by type:

    Saturdays: 4 × 3 = 12
    Sun–Fri evenings: 18 days (three weeks of 6), minus the 25th = 17
    Wed and Thu matinees: 6 (three of each), minus the 25th = 5
    12 + 17 + 5 = 34
B34 performances: 12 on Saturdays, 17 weekday evenings and 5 midweek matinees.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 31 counts only three Saturdays (missing the first or last day of the run), so it is 3 shows short.
  • C✗ 35 takes off Christmas Day’s evening show but forgets its matinee (the 25th is a Wednesday).
  • D✗ 36 forgets that the theatre is closed on the 25th.
  • E✗ 38 adds a 7 pm show on Saturdays as well. Saturdays have 5.30 and 8.30 instead.
Tip Week by week: Sat 7th = 3, then 11 and 11 for the two full weeks, then 11 − 2 = 9 for Christmas week. 34.
13(Q26 in the original paper) Counting pairs

A popular option at the Pizza Parlour is the 'Plus Two' pizza, which is a basic cheese and tomato pizza plus a choice of any two different1 additional toppings from the following list:

Tuna    Ham    Chicken    Onion    Mushroom    Sweetcorn

This gives 15 variations, which can be calculated as follows:

Each of the six additional toppings can be combined with one of the other five to create 30 pairs, but this counts each pair twice2, e.g. tuna and ham; ham and tuna.

Which of the following does not3 give a result of 15?

  1. AThe total number of matches when six teams play each other once.
  2. BThe total number of handshakes when six people shake hands with each other.
  3. CThe total number of crackers needed for six children to pull a cracker with each of the others.
  4. DThe total number of partnerships possible when two people are chosen from a shortlist of six to represent their society in a debate.
  5. EThe total number of cards sent when six friends send each other Christmas cards.

Reading it properly

  • 1Choosing 2 out of 6, where the order doesn’t matter.
  • 2Tuna + ham is the same pizza as ham + tuna, so 6 × 5 = 30 is halved to 15.
