1(TSA 2013, Q6) Dates & digitsAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Calendars, clocks & digital displays question in the past papers. You must first work out the only pattern such a date can follow, then use the spare digits in two different years to pin down the birthday before reading off his age. It is a long chain with little you can test from the options.

On only 360 occasions during the twentieth century was it possible to write the date in the form DD/MM/YYYY using eight different digits1. One example was 28th July 1956 (28/07/1956).

Eleven of Arthur's birthdays2 have been such dates (though not his date of birth), and in 1974 and 1983 the two digits that did not form part of the date made up his age at the time3.

In which year was Arthur born?

  1. A1909
  2. B1916
  3. C1918
  4. D1936
  5. E1938

Reading it properly

  • 1DD/MM/YYYY has 8 digits. All different means 8 of the ten digits 0–9 are used once, and 2 are left over.
  • 2A birthday is the same day and month every year. That links 1974 and 1983 together.
  • 3The 2 spare digits make his age, e.g. 3 and 8 make 38.

Working it out

  1. First, what must every such date look like? The year is 19__, so 1 and 9 are used.

    • Month: 10, 11, 12 and 01 reuse the 1, and 09 reuses the 9. So the month is 0m, and 0 is used too.
    • Day: it can’t start with 0 or 1 (used), and 30 or 31 reuse 0 or 1. So the day is 2d.
    every date = 2d / 0m / 19YY 1, 9, 0, 2 always used

  2. Now list the spare digits for each year (d, m and the age share them):

    1974: used 1, 9, 7, 4, 0, 2 free 3, 5, 6, 8
    1983: used 1, 9, 8, 3, 0, 2 free 4, 5, 6, 7
    same birthday, so d and m are in both lists 5 and 6

    So his birthday is 25/06 (or 26/05), and the leftover digits make his age:

  3. 1974: 3 and 8 38 (83 is silly: then 1983 would make him 92)
    1983: 4 and 7 47 ✓ exactly 9 years older
    born 1974 − 38 = 1936
    Check Years using two of 3, 4, 7, 8 after 1936 are 1937, 38, 43, 47, 48, 73, 74, 78, 83, 84, 87. That is eleven birthdays ✓. And 25/06/1936 repeats the 6, so his date of birth doesn’t count ✓.
D1936: his birthday is 25/06 (or 26/05), and he was 38 in 1974 and 47 in 1983.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1909 makes him 65 then 74. In 1974 the date would need 3 and 8, in 1983 it would need 5 and 6: two different birthdays.
  • B✗ 1916 makes him 58 then 67. The date would need 3 and 6 in 1974 but 4 and 5 in 1983: not the same birthday.
  • C✗ 1918 makes him 56 then 65. The date would need 3 and 8 in 1974 but 4 and 7 in 1983.
  • E✗ 1938 makes him 36 then 45. The date would need 5 and 8 in 1974 but 6 and 7 in 1983.
Tip Dates are always 2d/0m/19YY. Spare digits: 1974 → 3, 5, 6, 8; 1983 → 4, 5, 6, 7. Same birthday, so d and m are 5 and 6; ages 38 and 47; born 1936.

2(TSA 2010, Q44) Networks & logicAnswer C

Why it's herethe hardest Maps, routes & networks question in the past papers. You must turn 'always pass' into a single bottleneck point and the 'pass twice' clue into where the fountain has to be, then decide which of the remaining stars can genuinely be told apart.

My local park has a network of paths linking various locations, as shown below. The stars on the map represent five of the park's landmarks1: the fountain, lake, bandstand, statue and bowling green.

When I walk round the park, I often vary my routes2 and I do not always take the shortest route from one place to another. I have noticed that when I go from the statue to the fountain, I always pass the bandstand3, but that going from the lake to the bowling green, I never pass the fountain unless I pass the bandstand twice4.

How many of the stars5 on the diagram can be identified from the given information?

  1. A0
  2. B1
  3. C2
  4. D3
  5. E5

Reading it properly

  • 1Five stars, five names.
  • 2Any route at all, so “always” really means you can’t get round it.
  • 3The bandstand blocks every route from statue to fountain: a bottleneck.
  • 4To reach the fountain from lake/bowling green you must go through the bandstand and come back.
  • 5How many stars get a definite name?

Working it out

  1. The map falls into two halves, joined at one point: the middle star. It is the only star you can’t walk round.

    “always pass the bandstand” on every route bandstand = middle star

  2. So the statue and the fountain are in different halves. One of them is the bottom-right star, the other is on the left.

    Lake → bowling green: you only pass the fountain by crossing the bandstand and coming back (twice). So the fountain is alone in the other half from the lake and bowling green fountain = bottom-right star.

    Check If the fountain were on the left, you could walk to it from the lake without touching the bandstand at all.

  3. Statue, lake and bowling green are the three left-hand stars, but nothing tells them apart.

    identified: bandstand + fountain = 2
C2: the bandstand (middle star) and the fountain (bottom-right star).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 0 misses that the middle star is the only bottleneck, so it must be the bandstand.
  • B✗ 1 finds the bandstand but not the fountain. The second clue puts the fountain alone in the right half.
  • D✗ 3 would need one of the left-hand stars pinned down, but statue, lake and bowling green all play the same role there.
  • E✗ 5 would need every star named. The three left-hand stars can be swapped round and both clues still hold.
Tip Bottleneck star = bandstand; the lone star in the other half = fountain; the other three are interchangeable. 2.

3(TSA 2018, Q44) Cubes & netsAnswer D

Why it's herethe second-hardest Nets & folding question in the past papers. Only the three faces with a single black triangle matter, but you must work out which edge each triangle leans on, including edges that only meet once the net is folded, and match their order around the corner.

Here is a diagram of a cube.

Which one of the following nets could1 be folded to make this cube?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C3
  4. D4
  5. E5

Reading it properly

  • 1Only one net works. The three faces you see each have one black triangle; check where it sits.

Working it out

  1. On the cube, each visible face has a single black triangle, and each triangle sits against one edge:

  2. In every net the single-triangle squares are the same three (shaded): S1 and S2 are the 2nd and 3rd squares of the top row, S3 is the square under S2. They meet at one corner when folded, because S1’s bottom edge zips onto S3’s left edge. Now see what each lone triangle leans on:

    net 1: S1 → S3, S2 → S3 (both on S3) ✗
    net 2: S1 → S3, S2 → outside, S3 → outside ✗
    net 3: S2 → S3 and S3 → S2 (on each other) ✗
    net 4: S1 → S3 → S2 → outside, a chain ✓
    net 5: same single squares as net 2 ✗
  3. Check Fold net 4 with S1 on top: S3 is on its left and S2 on its right, the same clockwise order as the picture, so it really matches.
    answer: net 4
DNet 4.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Net 1: the lone triangles of S1 and S2 both lean on S3. On the cube they lean on two different faces.
  • B✗ Net 2: S2’s and S3’s triangles both lean on outside edges. On the cube only one does.
  • C✗ Net 3: S2’s and S3’s triangles lean on each other, across the same edge. On the cube no two triangles share an edge.
  • E✗ Net 5 has the same three single-triangle squares as net 2 (only a hidden bow-tie differs), so it fails for the same reason.
Tip Look only at the three squares with one black triangle. The cube needs a chain: top leans on left, left on right, right on the outside. Only net 4 does that.

4(TSA 2012, Q44) 3D shapes & mirror imagesAnswer C

Why it's herethe second-hardest 3D views & cubes question in the past papers. All five solids are built from the same cubes in the same basic skeleton, so you must mentally rotate each one and compare which way its stubs point. One solid differs from a partner only by being its mirror image, which is very hard to see.

Five pictures of solids made by sticking cubes together1 are shown below.

Which one of the following (A-E) is a different shape from all the others2?

  1. Asolid A
  2. Bsolid B
  3. Csolid C
  4. Dsolid D
  5. Esolid E

Reading it properly

  • 1Each solid can be turned any way, so describe it by its structure, not by how it is drawn.
  • 2“All” is the key word: the shapes come in matching pairs, and one is left without a partner.

Working it out

  1. Count the cubes: every solid has 11, so that doesn’t help. Look for the skeleton instead. Each solid is:

    • a corner cube with two straight arms at right angles, each 4 cubes long (counting the corner);
    • plus two 2-cube stubs, one on the 3rd cube of each arm (counting from the corner).

    So the only difference is which way the two stubs point.

  2. Find the stubs in each picture:

    • A: the arms lie flat on the table. One stub lies flat too (pointing away from the other arm); the other is the tower going up.
    • D: the arms are the tall column and the arm coming forward. One stub hangs down (in the same flat plane, pointing away from the column); the other sticks out sideways to the left.
    • C: the arms are the column and the bottom row. One stub hangs down (flat, pointing away from the column); the other goes backwards from near the top of the column.
    • B: arms = column and bottom row. One stub comes forwards along the floor, the other goes backwards from the column: opposite ways, both out of the flat L.
    • E: arms flat on the table. One stub goes up, the other hangs down: again opposite ways, both out of the L.
    B and E have both stubs sticking out in opposite directions B = E

  3. A, C and D all have one flat stub and one sticking-out stub. Now check which way it sticks out. Turn each one so its flat part looks the same (corner top-left, arms going right and down, flat stub pointing left):

    In A and D the sticking-out stub comes towards you; in C it goes away. So A = D, and C is their mirror image.

    pairs: A–D and B–E the one with no partner is C
  4. Tip In the exam you don’t need to prove the mirror image. Once you spot that A matches D and B matches E, C must be the answer.
CC: A and D are the same solid, B and E are the same solid, and C (a mirror image of A and D) matches none of them.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is the same solid as D (turn D so its column lies flat on the table).
  • B✗ B is the same solid as E: both have their two stubs pointing in opposite directions.
  • D✗ D is the same solid as A: same flat part, and the extra stub sticks out on the same side.
  • E✗ E is the same solid as B.
Tip Same skeleton every time: two 4-cube arms with a 2-cube stub on each. Pair them by stub directions: A–D, B–E. C is left over.

5(TSA 2014, Q6) Speed, time & timetablesAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Speed, distance & time question in the past papers. The natural answer assumes the worst possible wait at both sets of lights, but because the lights change together and the ride between them takes a fixed time, both worst cases cannot happen on the same journey. You have met this one before: it was in the 2014 paper you sat.

Central Avenue is a long straight road, several kilometres long. Both my home and my office are on Central Avenue, and the diagram below shows their positions, together with two sets of traffic lights that lie in between.

Both sets of lights turn to green for Central Avenue traffic simultaneously1, showing green for exactly two minutes, turning green again exactly two minutes later2.

I cycle to work every morning at a steady speed of 5 metres per second3 (except, of course, whilst waiting at traffic lights).

What is the longest4 journey time I can expect the journey to work to take?

  1. A6 minutes
  2. B7 minutes
  3. C8 minutes
  4. D9 minutes
  5. E10 minutes

Reading it properly

  • 1The two sets of lights are linked: they change at the same moment. That limits the waiting.
  • 2One cycle = 2 min green, 2 min red, then repeat (4 minutes).
  • 35 m every second, so divide each distance by 5 to get seconds.
  • 4Worst case, but it must be a case that can really happen.

Working it out

  1. Riding time first (no waiting):

    400 5 = 80 s
    900 5 = 180 s = 3 min
    500 5 = 100 s
    total = 360 s = 6 min of riding

  2. Worst luck at the first lights: arrive just as they turn red. Wait the full 2 minutes. You set off again at the exact moment both lights go green.

  3. The next 900 m takes exactly 3 minutes, so you reach lights 2 three minutes into their cycle:

    green 0 → 2 min, red 2 → 4 min
    you arrive at 3 min red, so wait 1 minute
    Check If you sail through lights 1 on green instead, you still reach lights 2 at most 1 minute before they go green. So the most waiting possible is 2 + 1 = 3 minutes.

  4. Picture the worst case on a distance–time graph (flat bits = waiting):

    6 min riding + 2 min + 1 min waiting = 9 minutes
D9 minutes: 6 riding + 2 waiting at the first lights + 1 waiting at the second.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 6 minutes is the riding time with no waiting at all, which is the shortest journey.
  • B✗ 7 minutes only adds one short wait.
  • C✗ 8 minutes waits at the first lights but forgets the second set.
  • E✗ 10 minutes waits the full 2 minutes at both lights. That can’t happen, because they change together and you arrive at lights 2 halfway through red.
Tip Ride 1800 m at 5 m/s = 6 min. Max wait 2 min at lights 1; you then reach lights 2 three minutes into the cycle, so 1 more. 6 + 2 + 1 = 9.

6(TSA 2021, Q25) Percentages & proportionAnswer C

Why it's herethe hardest Percentages question in the past papers. Several chained steps each hide a trap: the loaf is a percentage of the dough (so adding 20% to the loaf is wrong), dough only comes in whole boxes, and the waste must be measured against what you buy, not what you use.

I run a bakery and sell two sizes of loaf. Small loaves (weighing 240 g)1 sell for 80p, and large loaves (weighing 400 g)1 sell for £1.20.

I buy dough, ready-made, at the start of each day. Small boxes (5 kg of dough)2 cost me £10.00 each, and large boxes (15 kg of dough)2 cost me £20.00 each.

The baking process causes each loaf to lose 20% of the weight of the unbaked dough3.

I expect to sell 40 small loaves and 50 large loaves4 each day. I buy enough dough to be able to make these, and any unused dough at the end of the day has to be thrown away.

Assuming that I do not buy any more dough than necessary5, what proportion of the dough that I buy6 each day do I have to throw away?

  1. A5.2%
  2. B6.3%
  3. C7.5%
  4. D17.8%
  5. E34.8%

Reading it properly

  • 1These are baked weights. The dough must weigh more.
  • 2Dough only comes in 5 kg and 15 kg lumps. The prices don’t matter here.
  • 320% of the dough is lost, so the loaf is 80% of the dough. Divide by 0.8; don’t add 20%.
  • 440 small and 50 large every day.
  • 5Buy the smallest amount of boxes that covers what you need.
  • 6Waste dough bought (not dough used).

Working it out

  1. Work back from loaf to dough. The loaf is 80% of the dough:

    small: 240 0.8 = 300 g of dough
    large: 400 0.8 = 500 g of dough
    Trap! Adding 20% to the loaf (240 + 48 = 288 g) is wrong: the 20% is of the dough, not of the loaf. Check: 20% of 300 = 60, and 300 − 60 = 240.

  2. Dough needed each day, and the smallest buy that covers it:

    12 kg + 25 kg = 37 kg needed
    35 kg is too little; 40 kg is the least you can buy (15 + 15 + 5 + 5)
    40 − 37 = 3 kg thrown away
  3. 340 = 7.5100 = 7.5%
    Tip Buying “no more than necessary” means the waste is always less than one 5 kg box, so under 5 40 = 12.5%. That rules out D and E at once.
C7.5%: 37 kg of dough needed, 40 kg bought, 3 kg thrown away.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 5.2% would mean only about 2 kg thrown away from 40 kg. The waste is exactly 40 − 37 = 3 kg.
  • B✗ 6.3% would mean about 2.5 kg thrown away from 40 kg. Again, the real waste is 3 kg.
  • D✗ 17.8% buys 45 kg (three 15 kg boxes, which happen to cost the same £60): 8 45. But 40 kg is enough.
  • E✗ 34.8% would mean about 14 kg thrown away, more than a whole small box. That can’t happen if you buy no more than necessary.
Tip Dough = loaf 0.8: 300 g and 500 g. 40 × 0.3 + 50 × 0.5 = 37 kg. Buy 40 kg. 3 40 = 7.5%.

7(TSA 2020, Q32) Best buy & cuttingAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest Money & best value question in the past papers. The natural plan of cutting whole decorations from each ribbon wastes too much; you need a lower bound from the total length and price per centimetre, then a cutting plan that actually meets it, and two sensible-looking but wasteful plans are among the options.

Bert has agreed to make 14 decorations1 for his daughter’s birthday. Each decoration will require two gold ribbons, one 12 cm long and the other 7 cm long2.

The table shows the lengths and prices of available gold ribbon.

gold ribbon price list

gold ribbon lengthprice
30 cm£4.00
50 cm£5.00
60 cm£6.00

The ribbon can be easily cut to size3 for the decorations, but short pieces of ribbon cannot be joined to make longer lengths4.

What is the least amount5 that Bert will need to spend on gold ribbon for the 14 decorations?

  1. A£26.00
  2. B£27.00
  3. C£28.00
  4. D£29.00
  5. E£30.00

Reading it properly

  • 1So 14 pieces of 12 cm and 14 pieces of 7 cm.
  • 2Total length: 14 × (12 + 7) = 14 × 19 = 266 cm.
  • 3One long ribbon can be cut into lots of pieces.
  • 4Each piece must come from a single ribbon. Leftover scraps are wasted.
  • 5Cheapest set of ribbons that can be cut into all 28 pieces.

Working it out

  1. Price per cm:

    30 cm for £4 = 13.3p per cm (worst)
    50 cm for £5 = 10p per cm
    60 cm for £6 = 10p per cm

    So only use 50s and 60s, at 10p per cm. 266 cm at 10p is £26.60, so it costs at least £27.

    Check £26 buys at most 260 cm (for example 4 × 50 + 60), which is less than 266 cm. So £26 is impossible.

  2. £27 buys 3 × 50 cm + 2 × 60 cm = 270 cm, just 4 cm spare. Can we cut it with almost no waste? Cut the 12s and 7s separately:

    60 = 5 × 12 exactly
    50 = 7 × 7 + 1 spare
    50 = 4 × 12 + 2 spare
  3. 2 × £6 + 3 × £5 = £27
B£27: two 60 cm ribbons and three 50 cm ribbons, cut as shown with only 4 cm wasted.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £26 buys at most 260 cm of ribbon, but 14 × 19 = 266 cm is needed.
  • C✗ Cutting the 7 cm pieces from 60 cm ribbons (8 per ribbon) needs 60 + 50 = £11 for the sevens instead of 50 + 50 = £10.
  • D✗ Making whole decorations (19 cm sets) from each ribbon: 4 × 60 cm (3 sets each) + 1 × 50 cm (2 sets) = £29. That wastes ribbon.
  • E✗ Five 60 cm ribbons: 300 cm, 34 cm more than needed.
Tip Need 266 cm at best 10p/cm, so at least £27. It works: 12s from 60 + 60 + 50, 7s from 50 + 50.

8(TSA 2012, Q14) Rotations & gridsAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest 2D shapes, tiling & rotations question in the past papers. Each grid has to be tested under quarter and half turns against the visible blanks and symbols, and one option fits every square perfectly after a turn but fails because of which way a visible symbol would then face.

In a game, circular counters are moved around a board which is a 4 x 4 grid. There are symbols on some of the squares on the board. At one point in the game, the board looks like this (all the circles represent counters which completely cover any symbol on the square that they occupy1):

Which one of the following diagrams could2 show the layout of the board (not necessarily viewed in the same direction as above3)?

  1. Agrid A
  2. Bgrid B
  3. Cgrid C
  4. Dgrid D
  5. Egrid E

Reading it properly

  • 1A counter can hide anything. But a square you can see that is empty must be empty, and the two symbols you can see must be there.
  • 2Find the only option that agrees with every visible square.
  • 3You may turn the board round. Watch which way the bar and hexagon then face!

Working it out

  1. Write down what the board really tells us: 8 squares are visibly blank, 6 are hidden (?), the bar lies flat in row 2 on the left, and the flat hexagon is in the bottom-right corner.

  2. Which turns are allowed? In every option the hexagon lies flat, like on the board. A quarter turn would stand it on its point, so the option must be either the right way up or turned upside down (half turn).

    right way up: hexagon must be bottom-right C or E
    upside down: hexagon must be top-left A or B

  3. Test those four:

    • C: its triangle in the top row, 3rd column sits on a visibly blank square ✗
    • E: its bar in the top row, 3rd column sits on a visibly blank square ✗
    • A upside down: its triangle (row 2, column 2) lands on row 3, column 3, which is blank ✗
    • B upside down: everything lands on a hidden square, the bar or the hexagon ✓
    only B fits
  4. Trap! D is the sneaky one: give it a quarter turn and every symbol lands in a hidden square, and its upright bar even becomes the flat bar. But the hexagon would then stand on its point, and the board’s hexagon is flat.
BGrid B, turned upside down: every symbol is under a counter except the bar and the hexagon, which match.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Upside down, A’s triangle in row 2, column 2 lands on a square we can see is blank.
  • C✗ C’s triangle (top row, 3rd column) is on a square that is visibly blank on the board.
  • D✗ A quarter turn puts the symbols in the right squares, but the flat hexagon would end up standing on its point.
  • E✗ E’s upright bar (top row, 3rd column) is on a visibly blank square.
Tip Hexagon is flat, so only half turns. Hexagon top-left (A, B) or bottom-right (C, E). Check the blank squares: only B survives.

9(TSA 2010, Q49) Counting possibilitiesAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Counting & combinations question in the past papers. The scrap can sit in more than one position in the number, and a torn scrap could also be the other way up; each case you overlook gives one of the wrong options.

All the telephone numbers in my village have six digits and begin with 581. I have accidentally torn up2 and thrown away a piece of paper on which I had written the telephone number of a friend who lives nearby. The only piece I can find is the scrap shown below:

How many different phone numbers in my village could be consistent3 with this extract?

  1. A2
  2. B10
  3. C11
  4. D12
  5. E22

Reading it properly

  • 1Every number looks like 5 8 _ _ _ _.
  • 2A torn scrap: it could come from anywhere in the number, and you can’t tell which way up it is!
  • 3Count every number the scrap could have come from.

Working it out

  1. The scrap shows 4 digits in a row. Where can “8011” sit inside 5 8 _ _ _ _?

    • It can’t start on the 5.
    • Start on the 8 of 58: 5 8 0 1 1 ? last digit anything: 10 numbers.
    • Start at the 3rd digit: 5 8 8 0 1 1 1 number.

  2. Now turn the scrap upside down. 8, 0 and 1 all look the same turned round, so it reads 1108. It can’t start on the 8 of 58 (that would need a 1 there), so only: 5 8 1 1 0 8 1 number.

    10 + 1 + 1 = 12
D12: 580110 to 580119, 588011, and 581108 (scrap upside down).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 2 only puts the scrap at the very end (588011 and 581108), missing that it can start on the 8 of 58.
  • B✗ 10 only counts 580110 to 580119.
  • C✗ 11 forgets the scrap might be upside down.
  • E✗ 22 thinks upside down gives another 11, but “1108” can’t start on the 8 of 58, so it adds only 1.
Tip 58011? gives 10, 588011 gives 1, and upside down 581108 gives 1. Total 12.

10(TSA 2023, Q20) Counting & pie chartsAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest Charts & graphs question in the past papers. Counting 35 blocks of three sizes in a dense pattern without missing or double-counting any is error-prone, and two of the pies differ only in how the two smaller slices are split, so a miscount of one or two picks the wrong one.

The block paved area shown below is laid using three different-sized blocks1.

Which one of the following charts shows the relative comparison of the number of each size of block2 used in this paved area?

  1. Achart A
  2. Bchart B
  3. Cchart C
  4. Dchart D
  5. Echart E

Reading it properly

  • 1Small squares, rectangles (2 small squares long) and big squares (2 × 2).
  • 2Count blocks, not area. A big square counts as 1, even though it covers 4 small squares.

Working it out

  1. Count carefully, numbering each block as you go so you never count one twice:

    small squares 14, rectangles 13, big squares 8
    total = 35
    Check Check your count with area: the paving is 9 × 8 = 72 small squares, and 14 + 13 × 2 + 8 × 4 = 72. It fits!

  2. Turn the counts into shares of the circle:

    14 out of 35 = 40% (a bit less than half)
    13 out of 35 ≈ 37% (almost the same as 40%)
    8 out of 35 ≈ 23% (about a quarter)

    So we need two big, nearly equal slices and one slice of about a quarter.

  3. Check the charts:

    A: one slice is more than half ✗
    B: about 40%, 37%, 23% ✓
    C: three equal slices ✗
    D: one tiny slice, one nearly half ✗
    E: 40%, then two similar slices of about 30% ✗
    B
BChart B: 14 small squares, 13 rectangles and 8 big squares (40%, 37%, 23%).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A has one slice bigger than half the circle. No block type is more than half of the 35.
  • C✗ C has three equal slices, which would need about 12 of each type.
  • D✗ D has a tiny slice (about 16%) and one nearly half. That looks more like the share of area (big squares cover the most ground), not the number of blocks.
  • E✗ E gets the biggest slice right (40%) but makes the other two nearly equal (about 32% and 28%). We need 37% and 23%.
Tip Count: 14 small, 13 rectangles, 8 big. Two nearly equal large slices plus one quarter-ish slice: only B.

11(TSA 2017, Q36) Timetables & schedulingAnswer C

Why it's herethe hardest Time zones & timetables question in the past papers.   • D✗ 4 misses one region, usually the middle view or one of the diagonal swaps.

Why it's herethe hardest Rates, ratio & proportion question in the past papers. After the weekly cycle a few tablets are left over, and the word 'maximum' means you must choose which kind of day to spend them on. Spending them the obvious way, or using an average daily dose, lands you on a near-miss option.

My dog’s coat had been looking dull, so I bought some conditioning tablets. The pet shop stocks these in boxes of 300 tablets1, and for a dog the size of mine the recommendation (which I have followed) is 6 tablets per day2.

Now, several months later, his coat is much shinier. Opening a new box of tablets today, I have decided to reduce his dosage. I shall just give him 6 tablets per day, twice a week, and on the remaining days each week he can have 4 tablets each day3.

What is the maximum number of extra days4 that this box of conditioning tablets will now last?

  1. A10 days
  2. B15 days
  3. C16 days
  4. D24 days
  5. E25 days

Reading it properly

  • 1300 tablets in the box.
  • 2Old way: 300 6 = 50 days.
  • 3Two 6-days + five 4-days each week = 32 tablets a week.
  • 4“Maximum”: you choose which days are the 6-days, so put the 4-days where they help. Then compare with 50.

Working it out

  1. old: 300 6 = 50 days
    new week: 2 × 6 + 5 × 4 = 12 + 20 = 32 tablets

  2. How many full weeks fit in 300?

    9 × 32 = 288 (10 × 32 = 320 is too many)
    9 weeks = 63 days, with 300 − 288 = 12 tablets left

    To make the last 12 last as long as possible, use the 4-tablet days: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 tablets, so 3 more days.

  3. 63 + 3 = 66 days
    extra = 66 − 50 = 16 days
    Trap! If the leftover 12 went on 6-tablet days (6 + 6), it would last only 2 days: 65 days, 15 extra. “Maximum” means start the week with the 4s.
C16 extra days: 66 days (9 weeks + three 4-tablet days) instead of 50.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Uses an average of 5 a day ((6 + 4) 2): 300 5 = 60, so 10 extra. But there are five 4-days and only two 6-days a week.
  • B✗ Spends the last 12 tablets on two 6-tablet days, so 65 days. That’s not the maximum.
  • D✗ 24 extra means 74 days, but even 10 weeks would need 320 tablets.
  • E✗ Gives 4 a day every day: 300 4 = 75, so 25 extra. Forgets the two 6-tablet days each week.
Tip 32 a week. 9 weeks = 288 = 63 days. 12 left = three 4-days. 66 − 50 = 16.

Why it's herethe hardest Choosing a method question in the past papers. An abstract sufficiency question: you must see that a fixed-charge price line and a flat-rate price line can cross only once, and that a single comparison at one quantity does not settle it.

John wishes to have 500 copies1 of a brochure printed and is offered a choice of two price structures:

1. An equal cost for each brochure printed, regardless of number.2
2. An initial fixed cost followed by a lower cost than structure 1 for each brochure.3

Which one of the following sets of information would be sufficient4 for him to be able to decide which of the two price structures would be least expensive?

  1. AThe number of brochures for which the total bill would be the same by both structure 1 and structure 2.
  2. BAn example showing the cost of 500 brochures for structure 1 and 200 brochures using structure 2.
  3. CThe cost per brochure using structure 1 and the initial cost using structure 2.
  4. DAn example showing the cost of 200 brochures using structure 1 and 200 brochures using structure 2.
  5. EThe cost of 1 brochure using structure 1 and 2 brochures using structure 2.

Reading it properly

  • 1The number that matters: 500.
  • 2Structure 1: cost = price × number. A line from 0, fairly steep.
  • 3Structure 2: starts higher (the fixed cost) but climbs more slowly.
  • 4It must settle the question whatever the actual prices turn out to be.

Working it out

  1. Sketch both structures as cost lines:

    Structure 2 starts higher but grows more slowly, so the lines cross exactly once, at some number N. Below N structure 1 is cheaper; above N structure 2 is cheaper.

  2. So knowing N is all John needs: compare it with 500. That is option A.

  3. Check the others really aren’t enough:

    • B: structure 2’s cost for 200 doesn’t tell you its cost for 500, because you can’t split the fixed part from the per-brochure part.
    • C: structure 2’s per-brochure price is missing.
    • D: tells you which is cheaper at 200 only. If structure 1 wins at 200, structure 2 could still win at 500.
    • E: 2 brochures by structure 2 mixes up the fixed cost and the price per brochure.
AA: the break-even number tells you which structure is cheaper on each side of it, so you just compare it with 500.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ One cost for structure 2 (at 200) can’t be split into fixed cost and price per brochure, so its cost for 500 is unknown.
  • C✗ Structure 2’s price per brochure is missing, so its total for 500 can’t be found.
  • D✗ This only compares the two at 200 brochures. If structure 1 is cheaper there, structure 2 might still be cheaper at 500.
  • E✗ The cost of 2 brochures by structure 2 is fixed cost + 2 × price: one total, two unknowns. Not enough.
Tip Two straight lines cross once. Know the crossing point and you know which is cheaper on each side of it. A.

The housing estate on which Andrew lives has streets of variable length. In each street the houses are directly opposite each other, with the same number of houses on each side1. However, the numbering of the houses is inconsistent.

Some streets are numbered consecutively on each side, starting at one end of the street, going up to the end and then back down the other side2.

Other streets have the odd numbers on one side, starting from 1, and the evens on the other side, starting with 2 at the same end3.

Andrew's friend Amy lives on the same estate at 25 Acacia Avenue, which is not the end house in the street4.

If Andrew knows the number of the house opposite Amy's5, which one of the following statements is definitely true6?

  1. AHe knows the exact number of houses in the street.
  2. BHe knows that the number of houses in the street is one of two numbers.
  3. CIf the house opposite Amy's is 26, he knows the number of houses in the street.
  4. DIf he knows the number of the house next door to Amy, he can work out the number of houses in the street.
  5. EHe knows the numbers of the houses immediately adjoining Amy's.

Reading it properly

  • 1Same number of houses each side, so a street has an even number of houses.
  • 2System 1 (up and back). With n a side, house k faces house 2n + 1 − k.
  • 3System 2 (odds and evens). House 25 always faces 26.
  • 4This rules out one sneaky case (see step 2).
  • 5He knows the opposite number, but not which system the street uses.
  • 6Must be true whatever the opposite number turns out to be.

Working it out

  1. Sketch both kinds of street with Amy at 25:

  2. What does the opposite number tell Andrew?

    opposite is not 26 must be up-and-back next door are 24 and 26
    opposite is 26 up-and-back would need 2n + 1 − 25 = 26, so n = 25 and Amy is the last house on her side: an end house!
    Amy isn’t at the end so it is odds-and-evens next door are 23 and 27

    Either way he knows her neighbours E

  3. Check A and B fail when the opposite house is 26: an odds-and-evens street can be any length (28, 30, 32… houses). C says the opposite of the truth, and D fails for the same street because 23 and 27 are next door whatever its length.

  • C✗ Backwards: 26 opposite means odds-and-evens numbering, which tells you nothing about the length.
  • D✗ In an odds-and-evens street, next door is 23 and 27 whatever the length, so it doesn’t help.

You have met this one before: it was in the 2014 paper you sat.

Membership typeLengthExtrasNew member (£)Renewal price (£)
Bronze1 yearnone108
Silver6 monthsfree locker2518
Silver1 yearfree locker4528
Gold6 monthsfree locker, free competition entry4032
Gold1 yearfree locker, free competition entry7552

I am currently a Gold member, but I do not want to enter competitions any more as I play only once a month3. I do need a locker when I visit and the normal cost to hire a locker is £2 per visit4. I want to choose a membership that will give me the smallest possible total cost for a full year5.

What will be the difference between the amount that I will pay now and the amount I paid six months ago?

    Bronze: 8 + 12 × 2 = 8 + 24 = £32
    Silver 1 year: £28, locker free
    Silver 6 months twice: 18 + 18 = £36
    Gold: £52 (or 32 + 32 = £64)

25(TSA 2013, Q36) AveragesAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest Averages question in the past papers. You must squeeze a total out of two 'just under' and 'just over' clues, work out how far behind target you are today, then count the maximum-distance days needed to catch up. The shortfall sits close to a boundary, and using the wrong starting point gives a distractor.

Last month, in an attempt to improve my fitness, I began to run daily. The maximum distance1 I have time to fit in on any particular day is 8 miles, but my aim is to maintain an average of at least 5 miles per day2.

After 15 days my average was just under 6 miles per day3, then the following day I increased my average to just over 64. Since then (for the last 9 days, up to and including today5) I have been able to run only 2 miles each day.

What is the minimum number of days from now6 until I can bring my average back up to 5 miles per day?

  1. A2 days
  2. B4 days
  3. C9 days
  4. D18 days
  5. E26 days

Reading it properly

  • 1The best catch-up day is 8 miles.
  • 2Average 5 means the total must be at least 5 × (number of days).
  • 3Total after 15 days: just under 15 × 6 = 90.
  • 4This is the one that matters: after 16 days the total is just over 16 × 6 = 96.
  • 59 more days at 2 miles: + 18 miles. Today is day 16 + 9 = 25.
  • 6So run the maximum 8 miles every day from now on.

Working it out

  1. Where am I today?

    days so far: 16 + 9 = 25
    miles so far: just over 96 + 9 × 2 = just over 114
    needed for an average of 5: 25 × 5 = 125
    behind by just under 11 miles

  2. Each new day the target goes up by 5. Running 8 gains 8 − 5 = 3 miles a day on it.

    3 days: 3 × 3 = 9 still about 2 short ✗
    4 days: 4 × 3 = 12 back above target ✓
    answer = 4 days
  3. Check With totals: after 4 more days, just over 114 + 32 = just over 146, and 29 × 5 = 145 ✓. After 3: just over 138, but 28 × 5 = 140 ✗.
B4 days of 8 miles: each gains 3 miles on the target, and he is about 11 behind.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Two 8-mile days win back only 6 miles; he is about 11 behind.
  • C✗ 9 undoes the nine 2-mile days one for one ((5 − 2) × 9 = 27 behind, 3). That ignores the 16 miles he was ahead on day 16.
  • D✗ 18 aims for an average of 6 again, not 5: 150 − 114 = 36 behind, gaining only 2 a day.
  • E✗ 26 is about how many days have passed. He only needs to win back about 11 miles, at 3 a day.
Tip Day 25, just over 114 miles, need 125: about 11 short. Each 8-mile day gains 3: 4 days.
