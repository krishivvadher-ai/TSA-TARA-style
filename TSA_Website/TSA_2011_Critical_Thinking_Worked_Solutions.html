1(Q1 in the original paper) Main conclusionAnswer A
11% of nurses across the country would like to leave the profession. However, this figure rises to 39% amongst nurses who are working alternating shift systems, which mix day, evening and night shifts. It was found that many nurses preferred one type of shift most of the time, whether that involved working early, late or through the night.Evidence / example Employers should urgently review staffing arrangements to avoid the compulsory, alternating patterns.Main conclusion This would make nurses' working hours more compatible with their social and family lives. A further benefit would be a reduction in the cost of training new nurses to replace those leaving.Reason

What is the best expression of the main conclusion of this argument?

  1. AThe health service should adapt shift patterns to suit nurses in order to retain staff.✓ The “should” sentence: employers should drop alternating shifts. A rewords it, and “retain staff” matches the benefit of fewer nurses leaving.
  2. BAlternating shift systems cause nurses to want leave the health service.✗ A reason drawn from the 39% figure, not the recommendation. “Cause” also overstates a link.
  3. CMany nurses prefer a regular pattern of work.✗ Evidence: “many nurses preferred one type of shift”.
  4. DSome nurses would like to leave the profession no matter what their shift pattern.✗ Hinted at by the 11% figure, but it is a side point, not what the argument argues for.
  5. E61% of nurses prefer alternating shift patterns.✗ Bad maths: 39% wanting to leave does not mean 61% prefer alternating shifts.
Tip Look for “should”. The figures and the benefits are all there to back that one recommendation.

2(Q3 in the original paper) FlawAnswer C
Some recent films have been very expensive to make, but have not been the big box-office hits that would have justified the expense. At the same time, there have been films made very cheaply which have been received with both huge critical and popular acclaim. Indeed, some directors who have made successful low-budget films have gone on to make unsuccessful but expensive films.Evidence / example It is obvious then that if directors want to make popular films, they should stick to low budgets.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in the argument above?

  1. ACritics are often wrong in their predictions about the popularity of films.✗ Critics’ predictions are never part of the argument.
  2. BThe cost of making a film is normally greater than its original budget.✗ Going over budget is irrelevant: cheap films would still be cheaper.
  3. CThe cost of a film need not be the factor that determines its popularity.✓ A few examples of cheap hits and expensive flops do not show that the budget causes success. Story, actors or luck could matter more.
  4. DThe popularity of a film would justify a high level of expense in making it.✗ About whether expense is justified, not about what makes a film popular.
  5. EThe public does not necessarily know whether a film has been expensive or cheap to make.✗ Viewers need not know the cost for cost to affect a film. It misses the real gap.
Tip “Some” examples, then “it is obvious” about all directors: check whether the link is really cause and effect.

3(Q4 in the original paper) Draw a conclusionAnswer E
It is clear that the world is getting warmer.Context The only thing that could slow down this process of global warming, and avert ecological disaster, would be an immediate switch to green technologies. If global warming continues at the present rate, some species will be unable to live in the warmer conditions.Reason They will need to move to cooler places than they now occupy, for example closer to the poles, or higher up mountains. Not all of these species will be able to reach cooler climates, because of the long distances they would have to travel. Some of those that could move would end up in overcrowded habitats, which would make successful breeding impossible.Evidence / example

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. AMost species require cool habitats for successful breeding.✗ “Most species” is too strong. Breeding fails because of overcrowding, not a need for cool habitats.
  2. BMany species will be able to adapt to living in warmer climates.✗ Contradicts the passage: some species “will be unable to live in the warmer conditions”.
  3. CEndangered species will survive if we switch to green technologies.✗ The switch is needed to slow warming, not enough to guarantee survival.
  4. DIt is not possible to avert the ecological disaster caused by global warming.✗ Contradicts the passage: an immediate switch could avert disaster.
  5. EUnless we switch immediately to green technologies, some species will not survive.✓ Only an immediate switch can slow warming. Without it, warming carries on and some species cannot live, move or breed. So no switch means some species die out.
Tip “The only thing that could” sets up a must-have condition. The conclusion will be an “unless” statement.

4(Q5 in the original paper) AssumptionAnswer B
Cannabis smoking is widespread, even though it is illegal.Context It is also more dangerous to health than most people realise.Reason If we want to reduce the use of cannabis, it should be legalised.Main conclusion People do not turn away from dangerous habits unless they are fully aware of the damage these habits can cause.Reason We should have learnt this lesson from the example of tobacco smoking, which has declined dramatically because people have seen that it results in deaths from lung cancer.Comparison

Which one of the following is an assumption underlying the above argument?

  1. APeople who still smoke tobacco are unaware that it can affect their health.✗ Not needed. The argument is about people who gave up once they knew, not those who still smoke.
  2. BUnless cannabis smoking is legalised, its dangers will not be widely known.✓ The reasons are about awareness, the conclusion is about legalising. The missing link: legalising is what makes the dangers known.
  3. CIf tobacco smoking had been illegal, it would have been less widespread.✗ Not needed, and it would back keeping cannabis illegal.
  4. DThere has been insufficient research on the damage caused by cannabis smoking.✗ Not needed. The problem is what the public realises, not how much research exists.
  5. ECannabis users are motivated by the fact that they are engaging in an illegal activity.✗ A different reason to legalise. The argument works through awareness of danger, not thrill.
Tip Spot the jump: “people must know the dangers” → “so legalise”. The assumption joins those two.

5(Q9 in the original paper) StrengthenAnswer E
If children are sitting in rows in a classroom, the teacher can have eye contact with all of them while she is explaining something to them. This is not always possible if they are sitting in groups around tables. Also, when they look up, instead of seeing the child opposite in a group and being tempted to talk, they see the teacher.Reason So, sitting in rows helps children to concentrate better on their workIntermediate conclusion and should therefore be the standard arrangement in every school classroom.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, would most strengthen the above argument?

  1. ARows of desks take up no more classroom space than tables.✗ Only says rows are no worse on space. It adds nothing about learning or concentration.
  2. BSome children are easily distracted whether they sit in rows or in groups.✗ If some are distracted either way, rows make less difference: this slightly weakens.
  3. CSitting in groups of between four and seven makes discussion work easier.✗ A benefit of groups: this weakens.
  4. DTraditionalists argue that teaching the whole class in rows is best.✗ An appeal to opinion. What traditionalists think is not evidence that rows work.
  5. EIf desks are arranged in rows, children can all see visual aids more easily.✓ Another real benefit of rows for learning: everyone faces the front and can see what is being shown. That adds to the case.
Tip To strengthen, add a new reason that points the same way as the passage (rows help children learn).

6(Q10 in the original paper) FlawAnswer C
"There are more police officers than ever before in the history of this country. We have more police officers and more community support officers.Evidence / example That is why, last week, crime was down.Main conclusion Crime is down 30 per cent."Evidence / example

Which of the following states the flaw in the argument above?

  1. ACommunity support officers don't have the same powers as police officers.✗ True or not, it does not touch the jump from “more officers” to “that is why crime fell”.
  2. BCrime levels can be measured in different ways.✗ Too vague. It does not say what is wrong with linking police numbers to the fall.
  3. CCrime could have fallen if there had been no increase in policing.✓ Two things happened together, so the speaker says one caused the other. Crime might have fallen anyway, for some other reason.
  4. DThe fact that crime was down last week does not mean it will stay down.✗ The claim is only about last week, not about the future.
  5. EHaving more police officers may mean more crime is being detected.✗ More detection would push recorded crime up, so it does not explain a fall.
Tip “That is why” after two facts: ask whether the first really caused the second.

7(Q11 in the original paper) Main conclusionAnswer C
The differences between human beings are sometimes more striking than the similarities.Intermediate conclusion There is a minority of people who, driven by a competitive urge to be the first or the best, undertake ambitious expeditions involving severe physical deprivations, high risk of fatality, and extreme isolation. Compare these people to the majority who prefer an easy, risk free life of comfortEvidence / example and we can see that one human being can be as different from another in their behaviour as two different species of animal.Intermediate conclusion Any attempt to form general rules about human behaviour needs to bear this variation in mind.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the conclusion of the passage above?

  1. AMembers of the human race can be as different in their behaviour as two separate species of animal.✗ An intermediate conclusion (“we can see that…”) used to support the last sentence.
  2. BHumans who take risks in the interests of achieving unusual feats are untypical.✗ A detail of the example (a “minority”), not the point it makes.
  3. CVariation in human behaviour makes it difficult to generalise.✓ The last sentence: general rules about human behaviour must allow for the variation. In other words, the variation makes generalising hard.
  4. DDifferences between people can be more striking than similarities.✗ The opening claim, which the example supports on the way to the conclusion.
  5. EHuman beings are superior to all other animals.✗ Never said. Animals only appear in a comparison.
Tip “We can see that…” gives an intermediate step. Ask what it is used for: the final sentence.

8(Q15 in the original paper) Draw a conclusionAnswer B
Similar effects have similar causes. The problem is deciding when one set of effects sufficiently resembles another to infer a similar cause.Reason You say that the universe resembles a man-made object like a house, so its cause must also be similar, an intelligent designer, resembling human beings.Opposing view But is the universe so like a house? It seems to resemble an animal or vegetable just as closely, so why should its origin not lie in reproduction or some vegetative process?Reason The universe contains many classes of effects and objects, all with their different causes.Evidence / example There seems to be no compelling reason for concluding that the universe as a whole resembles one of these classes of effects or objects more closely than another.Intermediate conclusion

Which one of the following is a conclusion that can reliably be drawn from the above reasoning?

  1. AThe universe bears no resemblance to a man-made object like a house.✗ Too strong. It resembles a house, just no more than other things.
  2. BThe inference that the universe and man-made objects have similar causes is not justified.✓ The universe is no more like a house than like an animal or plant. So “it is like a house, so it has a house-like cause” is not justified.
  3. CThe world resembles a vegetable more closely than a house.✗ Contradicts “just as closely”.
  4. DThe universe is not the result of intelligent design.✗ Too strong. The passage attacks the argument for a designer; it does not prove there is none.
  5. EThe most likely origin of the universe is a reproductive process.✗ Too strong. Reproduction is only offered as “why not?”, equally likely, not most likely.
Tip When a passage knocks down an argument, the safe conclusion is “that argument fails”, not “its conclusion is false”.

9(Q16 in the original paper) AssumptionAnswer A
At first glance, there is no phenomenon creepier than crop circles - huge geometric designs that appear in fields overnight seemingly without cause, explanation or reason.Context UFO enthusiasts are quick to supply all three, by attributing the patterns to alien visitors seeking to communicate with humans, using laser beams or other advanced technology to flatten the crops.Opposing view These fanciful claims can fortunately be dismissed thanks to two quite mundane observations.Main conclusion Firstly, ordinary Earth-born individuals have repeatedly demonstrated how to produce the effects with nothing more elaborate than a rope and a plank.Evidence / example Secondly, there is a clear parallel for the phenomenon in the form of urban graffiti. Sneaking into fields to make crop circles is simply a rural variation of a widespread and well-documented human urge.Comparison

Which one of the following is an implicit, underlying assumption that is made by the above argument?

  1. AWhere two explanations compete we should accept the more ordinary one.✓ The evidence only shows humans could make the circles. To dismiss aliens on that basis, you must prefer the ordinary explanation.
  2. BThere is nothing difficult or technical about producing crop circles.✗ More or less stated (a rope and a plank), and it still only shows humans could do it.
  3. CAlien visitors would not try to communicate with humans by flattening crops.✗ Not needed. The aliens are dismissed because a mundane cause exists, not because they wouldn’t try.
  4. DNo visits from intelligent aliens have as yet been documented.✗ Not needed. The argument is about crop circles, not alien visits in general.
  5. EThe appearance overnight of huge geometrical designs is creepy if it can not be explained.✗ About why they seem creepy, which is just the opening. The reasoning does not rely on it.
Tip “Can be done by people” is not “was done by people”. Find the rule that bridges the two.

10(Q17 in the original paper) WeakenAnswer C
When you install a burglar alarm in your home, you do so presumably because the cost of the alarm is less than the expected benefits.Context The cost of a burglary, if one occurs, is likely to be much higher than the cost of fitting an alarm. A clearly visible box on an outside wall of an alarmed house alerts and discourages potential thieves.Reason Because your house is less likely to be burgled, your neighbour's house may be more vulnerable.Intermediate conclusion Therefore, while there may be little impact on overall crime levels, expensive security devices reduce crimes against those with the devices but increase crimes against those without.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, would most weaken the above argument?

  1. AFor similar houses, a higher percentage of those without intruder alarms are burgled than those with alarms.✗ Alarmed houses burgled less: this strengthens.
  2. BAs the percentage of houses with intruder alarms increased from 40% to 90%, house burglary rates fell by 60% and there was a rise in street muggings of 100%.✗ Crime moved elsewhere (muggings) rather than vanishing. That fits “little impact on overall crime”.
  3. CBurglars perceive an alarm as a signal that there are items well worth stealing in a house, while no alarm indicates less valuable contents.✓ If an alarm box says “valuables inside”, it may attract burglars, not put them off. Then alarms need not reduce crime against their owners.
  4. DOnly 60% of houses with alarms set the alarm every day.✗ The argument relies on the visible box putting thieves off, set or not.
  5. EBurglars know that 25% of all visible alarm boxes are fakes, but cannot tell the fake from the genuine.✗ Thieves can’t spot fakes, so every box still deters: this strengthens.
Tip The argument hangs on “a visible box discourages thieves”. Attack that link.

11(Q21 in the original paper) FlawAnswer A
It would be a good idea if the police force was separated into two divisions, one for proper policing i.e. protecting citizens and upholding law and order, and the other for road traffic.Main conclusion This second division could be combined with traffic wardensIntermediate conclusion as basically they are doing a similar job: obtaining money from road users. Perhaps then there would be a proper focus put on catching criminals and protecting the law-abiding public, and we would have a police force doing the job they were actually intended to do.Reason

Which one of the following best describes the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt overlooks the fact that many traffic offences are both criminal and a serious danger to the public.✓ It treats road policing as just “obtaining money”, not “proper policing”. But dangerous and drunk driving are crimes that put the public at risk.
  2. BIt rests on the false assumption that the police do not protect citizens or catch criminals.✗ Too strong. It only says the focus could be better, not that police do none of it.
  3. CIt ignores the fact that traffic wardens and the police have different duties and different powers.✗ A practical detail. The claim is only that the jobs are “basically” similar.
  4. DIt wrongly equates protecting citizens with catching criminals.✗ It lists both as parts of proper policing. It never says they are the same thing.
  5. EIt implies that the police once did the job they were supposed to do.✗ Not implied, and even if it were, it would not damage the reasoning.
Tip Check the split the argument relies on: is traffic policing really separate from protecting the public?

12(Q22 in the original paper) Parallel reasoningAnswer B
If you practise playing a musical instrument, your playing will get better.Reason I have not practised playing the piano, so my playing has not got better.Intermediate conclusion I have an exam in two month’s time.Context So I must practise every day if I want to do well.Main conclusion

Which of the following most closely parallels the reasoning in the argument?

  1. AIf you practise playing the cello your playing will get better. Dawn has not practised much but her playing has got better so she must have done enough. She may even pass her Grade III exam next month.✗ Not A but B happened anyway, so A must have been enough. A different (and odd) pattern.
  2. BIf you work hard at a sport, you will become better. I have not worked hard at my trampolining, so I have not become better. There is a competition next month. So I must work hard if I want to do well.✓ Step for step the same: if A then B; not A, so not B; an event is coming; so I must do A to do well.
  3. CIf you work hard at running you will get faster. I have worked hard to get faster, and I have started winning races. There is a race next week. If I want to win it I must keep practising.✗ Starts from “I have done A”, not “I have not done A”.
  4. DPeople who practise a craft get better at that craft. All master craftsmen and women have put long hours of practice into their art. It is impossible to become an expert without practising.✗ A general claim backed by examples, ending “impossible without practice”. No personal not-A, not-B step.
  5. EPractise makes perfect. I have practised hard on my violin so I should soon be perfect. I have a competition coming up soon which I fully expect to win.✗ “I have done A, so B will follow”: the opposite middle step, and no “must” at the end.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: if A (practise) then B (get better) not A, so not B event coming (exam) so I must do A to do well.

BIf A (work hard) then B (get better) → not A, so not B → competition coming → must work hard to do well. ✓ Same shape, even the same slip.
AIf A then B → not much A, yet B → so she did enough A. Starts from B happening. ✗
CIf A then B → I did A, and B is happening → race coming → keep doing A. Uses “did A”, not “didn’t”. ✗
DA gives B → all experts did A → impossible to get B without A. No “I”, no event. ✗
EA gives B → I did A, so B soon → expect to win. Positive prediction, no “must”. ✗
Tip The original is actually flawed (you could improve without practising). A parallel copies the flaw too, so look for “not A, so not B”.

13(Q23 in the original paper) PrincipleAnswer D
Legislation is needed in order to prevent the abuse of genetic testing by insurance companies.Main conclusion New genetic tests may reveal how likely an individual is to suffer from certain debilitating or fatal conditions in later life.Context Without legislation, insurance companies might make such tests compulsory and make those at risk pay more for insurance.Reason This would be unfair.Intermediate conclusion We do not choose our genes; we are born with them. People should not be penalised for things over which they have no control.Reason

Which one of the following best illustrates the principle underlying the above argument?

  1. AKylie did not deliberately get her accounts wrong, but she should pay the fine anyway. She should have made sure the accounts were right.✗ Kylie is penalised because she could have checked. The opposite case.
  2. BSome people have lost everything in the flood. Their insurance companies are responsible for their losses. Therefore the Government should not help out.✗ About who should pay for losses, not about penalties or control.
  3. CPhilip should not receive any help paying his debts, however sorry one may be for him. He knew in advance that his business venture was very risky.✗ Philip chose the risk, so he is refused help. It never says to spare someone with no control.
  4. DLate payment normally incurs a fine. But Jamuna's payment was held up because postal workers were on strike, so she should not be made to pay the fine.✓ The postal strike was outside Jamuna’s control, so she should not be fined. That is exactly “don’t penalise people for what they can’t control”.
  5. EThree people were not properly informed of possible financial losses when they joined the scheme. Their financial advisors should lose their professional licences.✗ Punishing advisers who were at fault. Nothing about sparing those who had no control.

Working: write the principle first

Genes are not chosen charging people more for them would be unfair we need a law to stop it.
So the principle: people should not be penalised for things over which they have no control.

DPayment late because of a strike (no control) → should not pay the fine. ✓ Same principle, new setting.
ANot deliberate, but she could have checked → fine her. Penalises, and she had control. ✗
BInsurers are responsible → no Government help. About who pays. ✗
CHe knew the risk (had control) → no help. Not about sparing anyone. ✗
EAdvisers at fault → lose licences. Punishing fault, not sparing the blameless. ✗
Tip The principle is often stated in the passage itself. Here it is the last sentence.

14(Q27 in the original paper) Main conclusionAnswer E
Britain's property market is in the midst of a full-scale slump with house prices falling at their fastest rate in 15 years, according to a new study.Context But why is there an assumption that continuing rises in house prices is a good thing?Opposing view Why not see the fall in house prices as a welcome change?Main conclusion House prices as a percentage of income are grossly overpriced. Low interest rates and cheap mortgages have led to the property market, particularly in London, being overvalued.Reason Surely this will make houses more affordable.Intermediate conclusion First time buyers will be happy beneficiaries of a housing slump. Others finding a silver lining in the gloom are those in rural communities for whom a house had seemed simply out of reach.Reason

Which one of the following is the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. ALow interest rates and cheap mortgages have led to an overvaluing of the property market.✗ A reason: prices were too high to begin with.
  2. BIt is always assumed that continually rising house prices are a good thing.✗ The view being challenged, and “always” overstates it.
  3. CRural communities will no longer find buying a house out of reach.✗ A supporting point, made stronger (“no longer”).
  4. DSurely a slump in prices will make buying a house more affordable.✗ An intermediate step: falling prices help buyers, which is why the fall is good.
  5. EWe should view the fall in house prices as a good thing.✓ “Why not see the fall… as a welcome change?” is a question that means “we should welcome it”. Everything after explains why.
Tip Rhetorical questions can carry the conclusion. Turn “Why not…?” into a plain statement.

15(Q28 in the original paper) Draw a conclusionAnswer C
The average life expectancy in working class areas of London was significantly higher than in similar areas in other major cities in the first half of the nineteenth century. In London during this time drinking tea had become a part of daily life amongst the poor.Evidence / example The reason for this was a combination of availability and cost. Boats bringing tea to the country docked in London and a certain amount was sold off directly to local markets.Context At this time in the rest of the country tea was still regarded as a luxury, consumed only by the better off.Evidence / example

Which one of the following conclusions is best supported by the passage above?

  1. AThe drinking of tea increases life expectancy.✗ Too strong. A link between tea and long life does not prove tea causes it.
  2. BEveryone ought to include tea in their daily diet.✗ “Ought” goes far beyond a historical link.
  3. CThere may be properties of tea which are beneficial to health.✓ Poor Londoners drank tea and lived longer than the poor elsewhere. That is only a link, so the careful “may be” is all we can say.
  4. DThe growing popularity of tea led to a decrease in the consumption of more harmful drinks, such as gin.✗ Gin is never mentioned. A possible explanation, not a conclusion from the passage.
  5. EFor the poor tea was a substitute for food.✗ Never suggested.
Tip A correlation supports a cautious “may”, never a firm “does”.

16(Q29 in the original paper) AssumptionAnswer C
A recent study by a drugs company found that the amount of money spent on a specific pill used for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease in the UK showed wide regional variation.Evidence / example Alzheimer's is a condition associated with the ageing process.Context In some areas the expenditure was as low as £1 per head of the population over 65 whereas in others it was as high as £10 per head.Evidence / example These pills are the most effective treatment for this condition that is currently available.Reason Therefore, this is another example of 'post-code lottery' where the effectiveness of treatment for a health condition will depend upon where you live.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is an assumption underlying the above argument?

  1. AThe pills are very successful in halting the progress of Alzheimer's disease.✗ Not needed. They only have to be the best treatment available.
  2. BThe drugs company that did the research doesn't have a vested interest in selling the pills.✗ Not needed. The argument uses the spending figures, not the company’s opinion.
  3. CThere are no regional variations in the incidence of Alzheimer's in the 65+ age group.✓ Spending is per person over 65, not per patient. If some areas have more over-65s with Alzheimer’s, spending should differ, and there is no lottery.
  4. DThere are no regional variations in the number of people in the 65+ age group.✗ Already dealt with: the figures are per head of the over-65s.
  5. EIt is not possible to develop Alzheimer's disease below the age of 65.✗ Not needed. A few younger patients would not explain a £1 to £10 gap.
Tip Ask “per head of what?” A fair comparison needs the right group: people who have the disease.

17(Q33 in the original paper) StrengthenAnswer C
In a high speed police chase, police drivers sometimes lose sight of the car they are chasing.Context But that’s no reason to give up. All they have to do is take the first left, and then the first left again.Intermediate conclusion Pursued drivers are likely to keep taking turns as they come up, as this seems instinctively like the best way to lose someone who is following them. They are not going to turn right as this involves waiting and checking for oncoming traffic.Reason Therefore take a couple of left turns and the police car will soon have their criminal in sight once again!Main conclusion

Which of the following, if true, would strengthen this argument?

  1. ACar chases tend to end with the pursued driver losing control of their vehicle.✗ How chases end says nothing about the left-turn trick.
  2. BDrivers in TV car chases tend to turn left rather than right when pursued.✗ TV chases are fiction, not evidence of real drivers.
  3. CPolice drivers are usually able to sustain faster average speeds over a length of time than other drivers.✓ Taking the same turns puts police on the right road. Being faster means they close the gap and “soon” see the car again.
  4. DPolice drivers usually request overhead assistance from a helicopter when chasing criminals in cars.✗ A different way to track them. It does not support the left-turn method.
  5. ETwo left turns might take the criminal where they want to be going.✗ Only “might”, and reaching their destination could let them stop and vanish.
Tip The plan has two parts: same route, then catch up. An option that helps either part strengthens it.

18(Q34 in the original paper) FlawAnswer C
The museum has decided to spend money in the hope of staying profitable and, therefore, remaining open.Context It has opened an expensive new front hall, which is very big and well planned, and will undoubtedly impress new visitors.Evidence / example For serious students, however, it has nothing to offer that was not there before. All the scholars who want to do so already use the museum, they will continue to come however artistic the displays may or may not be.Reason So, it was a waste to spend so much money on appearances,Intermediate conclusion the money should have been spent on improving conditions for those who come to study.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AThe museum cannot be sure that new visitors will be attracted by its new front hall.✗ A doubt about the hall would, if anything, support calling it a waste.
  2. BThere is no evidence that visitors to museums are interested in the conditions in which the contents are displayed.✗ Display conditions for visitors are not the issue. The conclusion is about study conditions.
  3. CIf scholars will continue to come anyway, the museum will not make more money by improving their conditions.✓ The aim is to stay profitable. Scholars come anyway, so spending on them earns nothing. The argument’s own reason undermines its conclusion.
  4. DThere is no contact or sympathy between serious scholars and members of the general public in museums.✗ Never assumed or relevant.
  5. EIt is regular users, and not potential new visitors, to the museum who deserve to be rewarded.✗ A value judgement that backs the conclusion. It is not a flaw.

25(Q47 in the original paper) Parallel reasoningAnswer A
All trees have leaves. An oak is a tree.Reason Therefore oaks have leaves.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the argument?

  1. AAll leaves grow on trees. That plant has leaves. Therefore that plant is a tree.✓ Same valid shape: all X are Y; this is an X; so it is Y. Here: anything with leaves is on a tree; this plant has leaves; so it is a tree.
  2. BAll oaks have leaves. That tree has leaves. Therefore that tree is an oak.✗ All oaks have leaves; this has leaves; so it is an oak. Runs the rule backwards.
  3. CAll plants have leaves. That leaf is an oak leaf. Therefore all plants are oaks.✗ Jumps from one leaf to “all plants are oaks”. Not even close.
  4. DAll trees have leaves. That plant has leaves. Therefore that plant is a tree.✗ Same words as the original, but backwards: having leaves does not make you a tree.
  5. EAll trees have leaves. That plant is not a tree. Therefore that plant does not have leaves.✗ Denies the first part: not a tree, so no leaves. Invalid.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: all X (trees) are Y (have leaves) this is an X (oak is a tree) so it is Y (has leaves). Valid.

AAll X (leaves) are Y (grow on trees) → that plant has X (leaves) → so Y (it is a tree). ✓ Same shape, and valid.
BAll X (oaks) are Y (leafy) → this is Y → so X. Starts from the result. ✗
CAll plants are leafy → one oak leaf → all plants are oaks. Nonsense jump. ✗
DAll X (trees) are Y (leafy) → this is Y → so X. Starts from the result. ✗
EAll X are Y → this is not X → so not Y. Denies the start. ✗
Trap! Don’t match the words. D shares the original’s first sentence but is the classic backwards trap. Check which part each middle sentence matches.
