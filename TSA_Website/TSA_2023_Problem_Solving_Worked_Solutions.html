1(Q2 in the original paper) Rates & fractionsAnswer D

The table shows the number of cold drinks sold, per hour1, yesterday at a 24-hour service station.

cold drinks
day time (15 hours)218
night time (9 hours)12

The service station also sells hot drinks. One-third fewer hot drinks were sold per hour than cold drinks3, regardless of the time of day.

How many drinks in total4 were sold yesterday?

  1. A252
  2. B378
  3. C450
  4. D630
  5. E720

Reading it properly

  • 1These are drinks per hour, not per day. Multiply by the hours.
  • 2Two different rates: 15 hours at one rate, 9 hours at the other. Do them separately.
  • 3“One-third fewer” means take a third away. Hot = ⅔ of cold, not ⅓ of cold.
  • 4Total = cold + hot, for the whole 24 hours.

Working it out

  1. Cold drinks over the whole day: rate × hours, for each part of the day.

    day: 15 × 18 = 270
    night: 9 × 12 = 108
    cold total = 270 + 108 = 378

  2. Hot drinks: one-third fewer every hour, so the whole day is one-third fewer too. Split the cold total into thirds:

    378 3 = 126 one third
    hot = 378 − 126 = 252 (two thirds)
    Check Same answer the long way: hot per hour is 12 by day and 8 by night, so 15 × 12 + 9 × 8 = 180 + 72 = 252.

  3. Picture it as parts: cold is 3 parts, hot is 2 parts.

    378 + 252 = 630
D630 drinks: 378 cold + 252 hot.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 252 is only the hot drinks. The question wants cold + hot.
  • B✗ 378 is only the cold drinks.
  • C✗ 450 is the daytime total only (270 cold + 180 hot). It leaves out the 9 night hours.
  • E✗ 720 uses 24 hours for both rates (18 × 24 + 12 × 24) instead of 15 hours and 9 hours.
Tip Cold: 15 × 18 + 9 × 12 = 378 = 3 thirds. Hot is 2 thirds. Total = 5 thirds = 5 × 126 = 630.

2(Q6 in the original paper) Logic & digitsAnswer D

The 4-digit PIN of my bank card has been set so that the first two digits and the last two digits both separately add up to 111.

The first digit is odd2 and the last digit in the PIN is the smallest of the four3. All four digits are different4, and no two digits in the PIN are numerically consecutive with each other5. So, for example, if the PIN contains the digit ‘6’ then it cannot contain either the digits ‘5’ or ‘7’.

What is the first digit6 of my PIN?

  1. A1
  2. B3
  3. C5
  4. D7
  5. E9

Reading it properly

  • 1Two pairs, each adding to 11.
  • 2First digit: 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9.
  • 3The last digit is the smallest of all four.
  • 4No repeats.
  • 5The big filter! No two digits next to each other on the number line, anywhere in the PIN, not just side by side.
  • 6Only the first digit is asked for.

Working it out

  1. List the digit pairs that add to 11:

    2 + 9, 3 + 8, 4 + 7, 5 + 6
    5 + 6 ✗ they are consecutive only 29, 38, 47 left

  2. The PIN uses two of these pairs. Check each mix for neighbours:

    29 with 38: 2 and 3 are neighbours ✗
    38 with 47: 3 and 4 are neighbours ✗
    29 with 47: digits 2, 4, 7, 9, no neighbours ✓

  3. Now place them. The smallest digit (2) is last, so the last pair is 9, 2. The first pair is 4 and 7, and the first digit is odd, so it starts with 7.

    PIN = 7 4 9 2 first digit = 7
D7 (the PIN is 7492).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1 would need a 10 to make 11. Impossible.
  • B✗ 3 pairs with 8, but the other pair (29 or 47) always brings a neighbour: 2 or 4 next to 3, 7 or 9 next to 8.
  • C✗ 5 pairs with 6, and 5 and 6 are consecutive.
  • E✗ 9 pairs with 2, but 2 is the smallest digit so it must go last. That makes 9, 2 the last pair, not the first.
Tip Pairs to 11 without neighbours: 29 and 47 only. Smallest (2) last gives __92, then the odd one of 4 and 7 goes first: 7.

3(Q7 in the original paper) Tables & ratesAnswer C

Jarold weighs 60 kg1. He decides to start a daily exercise regime of fast walking for 10 minutes, slow cycling for 20 minutes and slow swimming for 20 minutes2.

The table below shows the rate at which calories are used up, per 30 minutes3 of exercise, by people of different weights.

50 kg55 kg60 kg65 kg
slow cycling160170180190
fast cycling190210230240
slow swimming190200210220
fast swimming220250280295
slow walking50556075
fast walking4100110120150

How many calories will Jarold use during exercise on the first day of this regime?

  1. A170 calories
  2. B190 calories
  3. C300 calories
  4. D340 calories
  5. E510 calories

Reading it properly

  • 1Use the 60 kg column only.
  • 2Three activities, and none of them lasts 30 minutes!
  • 3Every number in the table is for 30 minutes. 10 min = ⅓ of it, 20 min = ⅔ of it.
  • 4Pick the right rows: fast walking, but slow cycling and slow swimming.

Working it out

  1. Read the 60 kg column (all per 30 minutes):

    fast walking 120
    slow cycling 180
    slow swimming 210

  2. Work in 10-minute blocks: divide each value by 3. Walking is 1 block, the others are 2 blocks each.

    walking: 120 3 = 40
    cycling: 180 3 × 2 = 120
    swimming: 210 3 × 2 = 140
    total = 40 + 120 + 140 = 300 calories
C300 calories: 40 walking + 120 cycling + 140 swimming.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 170 gives every activity only 10 minutes: (120 + 180 + 210) 3.
  • B✗ 190 is just one number from the table (for example slow cycling at 65 kg), not the total of the three activities.
  • D✗ 340 gives every activity 20 minutes: (120 + 180 + 210) × ⅔. The walking is only 10 minutes.
  • E✗ 510 adds the table values straight (120 + 180 + 210), as if each activity lasted 30 minutes.
Tip Per 10 minutes at 60 kg: walk 40, cycle 60, swim 70. Total = 40 + 2 × 60 + 2 × 70 = 300.

  • E✗ Only two bars are lit in all four displays. The top and bottom bars are dark in the third one, so they aren’t stuck on.

5(Q12 in the original paper) Speed & meetingAnswer E

David set out at 09:00 on a 10 km path1 up a hill, walking at 3 km/hour. Andrew set out from the top of the hill one hour later2, walking the same path in the opposite direction, at 4 km/hour.

How far apart will David and Andrew be 1 hour after they pass each other3?

  1. A1 km
  2. B3 km
  3. C4 km
  4. D6 km
  5. E7 km

Reading it properly

  • 1The path ends after 10 km. Check nobody reaches the end too soon.
  • 2A distraction for this question! The head start only changes when they meet, not how fast they separate.
  • 3After passing they walk away from each other.

Working it out

  1. Once they have passed, they walk in opposite directions, so the gap grows by both speeds added together:

    3 + 4 = 7 km every hour 1 hour later: 7 km

  2. Check neither walker runs out of path in that hour:

    10:00: David is 3 km up, Andrew at the top (10 km), 7 km apart
    gap closes at 7 km/h they meet at 11:00, 6 km up
    12:00: David at 6 + 3 = 9 km, Andrew at 6 − 4 = 2 km
    9 − 2 = 7 km
E7 km: after passing they separate at 3 + 4 = 7 km/h.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1 km uses 4 − 3, as if they walked the same way. They walk in opposite directions.
  • B✗ 3 km is only how far David walks in the hour.
  • C✗ 4 km is only how far Andrew walks in the hour.
  • D✗ 6 km is where they meet (6 km up the path), not the gap an hour later.
Tip Moving apart in opposite directions: 3 + 4 = 7 km in an hour. Quick check: David at 9 km, Andrew at 2 km, both still on the path.

plastic308245012

Juan wants two different boxes2 with a combined capacity of at least 1000 cm³3. He also wants to make sure that neither box has any side longer than 25 cm4.

What is the least5 that Juan can pay for the boxes?

  1. A$28
  2. B$30
  3. C$31
  4. D$34
  5. E$35

Reading it properly

  • 1Use this column for “capacity”. Don’t multiply the sides: the walls take up room, so capacity is less than length × width × depth.
  • 2Two different boxes: no buying two of the same.
  • 3Add the two capacities: 1000 or more.
  • 4Any box with a 30 cm side is out.
  • 5Cheapest pair that passes every test.

Working it out

  1. First knock out boxes with a side over 25 cm:

    left: plastic 450 ($13), wood 500 ($15), wood 600 ($22)

  2. Now try the pairs (different boxes, at least 1000 cm³):

    450 + 500 = 950 ✗ too small
    450 + 600 = 1050 ✓ $13 + $22 = $35
    500 + 600 = 1100 ✓ $15 + $22 = $37
    least = $35
    Trap! Two of the $15 wood boxes would make exactly 1000 cm³ for $30, but he wants two different boxes.
E$35: the plastic 20 cm box ($13) and the 600 cm³ wood box ($22).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ $28 pairs the $13 and $15 boxes because 20 × 6 × 4 = 480 and 20 × 10 × 3 = 600 make 1080. But the real capacities are 450 + 500 = 950: too small.
  • B✗ $30 is two of the $15 wood box. They have to be two different boxes.
  • C✗ $31 ($13 + $18) uses the 550 cm³ wood box, which is 30 cm long.
  • D✗ $34 ($12 + $22) uses the $12 plastic box, which is 30 cm long.
Tip Cross out the two 30 cm boxes. Of 450, 500 and 600, only pairs with the 600 reach 1000. Cheapest: the 450 and 600 boxes, $13 + $22 = $35.

7(Q14 in the original paper) Percentages & offersAnswer D

Dan wants to buy 12 kg1 of rock salt to help keep his driveway clear of ice during the winter. He has looked online and found four different offers on the recommended retail price of a 1 kg bag2.

1  Store 1 has 30% off3 the price of a 1 kg bag.

2  Store 2 has buy one and get one at half price4.

3  Store 3 has buy two and get one free5.

4  Store 4 has one third extra for same price6 as a 1 kg bag.

Which pair of offers works out to be the same price per kg7?

  1. A1 and 2
  2. B1 and 4
  3. C2 and 3
  4. D2 and 4
  5. E3 and 4

Reading it properly

  • 1Handy: 12 divides by 2, 3 and 4, so price exactly 12 kg at each store.
  • 2No price is given. Make one up: say a 1 kg bag costs £1.
  • 3Each bag 70p.
  • 4Every 2 bags cost £1 + 50p = £1.50.
  • 5Every 3 bags cost £2.
  • 6Not 30% off! You get 1⅓ kg for £1, so a third more salt, not a third off the price.
  • 7Compare like with like: the cost of the same 12 kg at each store.

Working it out

  1. Say a 1 kg bag is £1 and price Dan’s 12 kg at every store:

    Store 1: 12 bags × 70p = £8.40
    Store 2: 6 pairs × £1.50 = £9.00
    Store 3: 4 lots of 3 bags × £2 = £8.00
    Store 4: 12 1⅓ = 9 big bags × £1 = £9.00
    Tip 12 1⅓: each big bag holds 4 thirds of a kg, and 12 kg = 36 thirds, so 36 4 = 9 bags.

  2. See it as bags:

    Stores 2 and 4 both cost £9.00 2 and 4
DStores 2 and 4: both work out at 75p per kg (when a bag is £1).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Store 1 is 70p per kg but Store 2 is 75p per kg.
  • B✗ Treats “one third extra” like “30% off”. A third extra salt for the same money only saves a quarter (75p per kg), not 30%.
  • C✗ Store 3 is cheaper: you pay for 2 bags out of every 3, so about 67p per kg.
  • E✗ Mixes up “one free with every two” (pay ⅔) with “one third extra” (pay ¾). Store 3 costs £8 for 12 kg; Store 4 costs £9.
Tip Bag = £1. Per kg: 1 → 70p, 2 → 75p, 3 → 67p, 4 → £1 1⅓ = 75p. Same: 2 and 4.

8(Q18 in the original paper) Logic & scoresAnswer A

Five friends play a word game called Wordsmith. The objective is to score as many as points as possible, using the letters of a randomly chosen starting word to create new words. Each letter used in a word scores one point1, and the player with the most points after five rounds is the winner.

The word chosen for this particular game was ‘RAINBOW’. In the first four rounds, the words selected by the five players were as follows:

GeorginaRiazBobbyVandySonny
BRAINBROWNROBINBRAWNWORN
WARNBARONBORNIRONBOAR
BRANRAINWARBROWBAN
NOWWONANNOOR

The words given in the final round by the five players were: ‘ROW’, ‘BAR’, ‘AIR’, ‘ON’ and ‘I’2 (not in order)3.

Which one of the following is not a possible difference4 between the final scores of the players with the most and least points?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C3
  4. D4
  5. E5

Reading it properly

  • 1Score = number of letters. BRAIN = 5 points.
  • 2Final round adds 3, 3, 3, 2 and 1 points: one each.
  • 3We don’t know who said which word, so try different matches.
  • 4Find the gap (top score − bottom score) that can never happen.

Working it out

  1. Totals after four rounds (count the letters):

    Georgina 5 + 4 + 4 + 3 = 16
    Riaz 5 + 5 + 4 + 3 = 17
    Bobby 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 14
    Vandy 5 + 4 + 4 + 2 = 15
    Sonny 4 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 13

  2. Riaz gets at least 1 more, so he ends on at least 18. Sonny gets at most 3 more, so he ends on at most 16.

    top ≥ 18 and bottom ≤ 16 the gap is at least 2
    a gap of 1 is impossible 1

  3. Check the others really can happen (final-round points in brackets):

    gap 2: Sonny 13 (+3) = 16, Bobby (+3) 17, Vandy (+3) 18, Georgina (+2) 18, Riaz (+1) 18
    gap 3: Sonny (+3) 16, Bobby (+3) 17, Vandy (+3) 18, Georgina (+1) 17, Riaz (+2) 19
    gap 4: Sonny (+3) 16, Bobby (+2) 16, Vandy (+1) 16, Georgina (+3) 19, Riaz (+3) 20
    gap 5: Sonny (+2) 15, Bobby (+3) 17, Vandy (+1) 16, Georgina (+3) 19, Riaz (+3) 20
A1: Riaz always ends on 18 or more and Sonny on 16 or less, so the gap is at least 2.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 2 can happen: give Sonny, Bobby and Vandy the 3-letter words, Georgina ON and Riaz I. Scores 16, 17, 18, 18, 18.
  • C✗ 3 can happen: Sonny, Bobby, Vandy +3, Georgina +1, Riaz +2. Scores 16 to 19.
  • D✗ 4 can happen: Georgina and Riaz +3, Sonny +3, Bobby +2, Vandy +1. Scores 16 to 20.
  • E✗ 5 can happen: Riaz 20 at the top and Sonny +2 = 15 at the bottom.
Tip Riaz starts on 17, Sonny on 13. Riaz ends ≥ 18, Sonny ends ≤ 16, so the gap can never be 1.

My local shop sells a variety of bags containing either just apples, just oranges or different mixtures of the two fruits. The options available are shown below:

optionapplesorangesprice (p)total number in bag
apple bag60996
orange bag061096
lucky dipat least 13at least 1956
half and half331056

What is the minimum4 that I could spend to be sure5 that I have enough fruit for all three children next week?

  1. A£2.85
  2. B£2.99
  3. C£3.04
  4. D£3.17
  5. E£3.19

Reading it properly

  • 13 children × 5 days = 15 pieces. But the type of fruit matters.
  • 2Two children want oranges: 2 × 5 = 10 oranges. One wants apples: 5 apples.
  • 3A lucky dip only promises 1 apple and 1 orange. The other 4 could be anything.
  • 4Cheapest.
  • 5“Sure” means it must work even if every lucky dip is as unhelpful as possible.

Working it out

  1. What do we actually need? Draw the week:

  2. Lucky dips only guarantee 1 orange each, so they are a poor way to get 10 oranges. Build from the guaranteed bags:

    2 orange bags + 1 apple bag 12 oranges, 6 apples ✓ 109 + 109 + 99 = 317p
    1 orange bag + 2 half and half 12 oranges, 6 apples ✓ 109 + 105 + 105 = 319p
    3 half and half only 9 oranges ✗, a 4th bag pushes it over £4
    minimum = 317p = £3.17
    Check Count only what each bag promises: orange bag 6 oranges, half and half 3 + 3, lucky dip 1 + 1. Just two sets of 3 bags promise 10 oranges and 5 apples (317p and 319p). Any 4 bags cost at least 4 × 95p = £3.80.
D£3.17: two orange bags and one apple bag.

  • E✗ £3.19 (an orange bag + two half-and-half bags) does work, but it costs 2p more than £3.17.
Tip Need 10 oranges + 5 apples. Lucky dips are useless when you must be sure. Two orange bags + an apple bag = 109p + 109p + 99p = £3.17.

10(Q20 in the original paper) Counting & pie chartsAnswer B

The block paved area shown below is laid using three different-sized blocks1.

Which one of the following charts shows the relative comparison of the number of each size of block2 used in this paved area?

  1. Achart A
  2. Bchart B
  3. Cchart C
  4. Dchart D
  5. Echart E

Reading it properly

  • 1Small squares, rectangles (2 small squares long) and big squares (2 × 2).
  • 2Count blocks, not area. A big square counts as 1, even though it covers 4 small squares.

Working it out

  1. Count carefully, numbering each block as you go so you never count one twice:

    small squares 14, rectangles 13, big squares 8
    total = 35
    Check Check your count with area: the paving is 9 × 8 = 72 small squares, and 14 + 13 × 2 + 8 × 4 = 72. It fits!

  2. Turn the counts into shares of the circle:

    14 out of 35 = 40% (a bit less than half)
    13 out of 35 ≈ 37% (almost the same as 40%)
    8 out of 35 ≈ 23% (about a quarter)

    So we need two big, nearly equal slices and one slice of about a quarter.

  3. Check the charts:

    A: one slice is more than half ✗
    B: about 40%, 37%, 23% ✓
    C: three equal slices ✗
    D: one tiny slice, one nearly half ✗
    E: 40%, then two similar slices of about 30% ✗
    B
BChart B: 14 small squares, 13 rectangles and 8 big squares (40%, 37%, 23%).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A has one slice bigger than half the circle. No block type is more than half of the 35.
  • C✗ C has three equal slices, which would need about 12 of each type.
  • D✗ D has a tiny slice (about 16%) and one nearly half. That looks more like the share of area (big squares cover the most ground), not the number of blocks.
  • E✗ E gets the biggest slice right (40%) but makes the other two nearly equal (about 32% and 28%). We need 37% and 23%.
Tip Count: 14 small, 13 rectangles, 8 big. Two nearly equal large slices plus one quarter-ish slice: only B.

11(Q24 in the original paper) Calendars & countingAnswer E

Every Christmas season, an ice-skating rink is laid in the central square in Boland. The rink is open to the public from 12th December to 9th January inclusive1, between 2 pm and 5 pm. It is closed on Sundays and Christmas Day (25th December)2, but there is an extra session on Saturdays3 between 10 am and 1 pm. The charge is £2 an hour4.

When Jimmy was learning to skate, he went to the rink for two hours every session5 that it was open. That year, 12th December was on a Monday6.

What is the total amount that Jimmy had to pay?

  1. A£100
  2. B£104
  3. C£108
  4. D£112
  5. E£116

Reading it properly

  • 1Inclusive: count both end days. 12–31 Dec is 20 days, plus 9 in January = 29 days.
  • 2Check which day Christmas falls on! If it is a Sunday, the rink is shut anyway. Don’t take it off twice.
  • 3Saturdays have two sessions.
  • 42 hours × £2 = £4 per session.
  • 5He pays for 2 hours, not the whole 3-hour session.
  • 6Lets you find the weekday of every date.

Working it out

  1. Find Christmas Day: 12 Dec is a Monday, so 18 Dec and 25 Dec are Sundays. Christmas is already a closed day.

  2. Count the sessions:

    29 days = 4 full weeks + Monday 9 Jan
    Sundays: 18, 25 Dec, 1, 8 Jan = 4 closed 29 − 4 = 25 afternoon sessions
    Saturdays: 17, 24, 31 Dec, 7 Jan 4 extra morning sessions
    25 + 4 = 29 sessions
  3. 29 sessions × £4 = £116
    Tip 29 × 4: do 30 × 4 = 120, then take off 4.
E£116: 29 two-hour sessions at £4 each.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £100 is 25 sessions: it forgets the 4 extra Saturday-morning sessions.
  • B✗ £104 is 26 sessions: three sessions too few, for example by losing 9 January (not counting inclusively), taking Christmas off again and missing a Saturday morning.
  • C✗ £108 is 27 sessions: takes Christmas off as an extra closed day and forgets that 9 January counts.
  • D✗ £112 is 28 sessions: takes Christmas Day off as well, but it is a Sunday, so it was already closed.
Tip 29 days from Mon 12 Dec, 4 Sundays (Christmas is one of them): 25 days + 4 Saturday extras = 29 sessions × £4 = £116.

  • 210 tokens for $40 = $4 a ride, cheaper than $5 singles.
  • 3A 5th person: an adult who needs her own entry.
  • 4Spend all $250: entry first, then as many tokens as possible.
  • 5Four equal shares.
  • 6Pearl must get 2 or more, so the four can’t take every token.
  • 7Amy’s share, as big as possible.

Working it out

  1. Entry: the family ticket covers Mike, Helen, Amy and Ben and gives 8 tokens. Pearl pays adult entry.

    family ticket $120 + Pearl $30 = $150
    $250 − $150 = $100 left for tokens

  2. Spend the $100 on tokens, cheapest first:

    2 super rides = $80 20 tokens
    $20 left 4 single tokens
    total = 8 + 20 + 4 = 32 tokens
    Check Without the family ticket, entry is 30 + 30 + 25 + 25 + 30 = $140, leaving $110 for 26 tokens. The family ticket wins.

  3. Share them: 4 equal shares, with at least 2 left for Pearl.

    8 each = 32 Pearl gets 0 ✗
    7 each = 28 Pearl gets 4 ✓
    Amy = 7
D7 rides: 32 tokens = 4 × 7 + 4 for Pearl.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 4 forgets both the family ticket’s 8 tokens and the spare $20 (20 tokens = 4 each + 4).
  • B✗ 5 forgets the 8 tokens that come with the family ticket (24 tokens = 4 × 5 + 4).
  • C✗ 6 leaves $20 unspent (28 tokens), or pays separate entry fees instead of the family ticket (26 tokens).
  • E✗ 8 each uses all 32 tokens, leaving Pearl with none. She must have at least 2.
Tip Family $120 + Pearl $30 = $150. $100 → 2 super rides + 4 singles = 24 tokens, plus 8 free = 32. 32 − 2 = 30 → 7 each (Pearl 4).

25(Q50 in the original paper) Ratios & percentage changeAnswer A

At a factory, the senior management are monitoring the ratio of production to resources used1 each day. For example, if on one day the production is $200 000 and the resources used is $40 000, then the ratio is 5.

Last week, the production and resources used each day (both in tens of thousands of dollars) were as shown in the graph below.

Between which two days did the ratio of production to resources used increase by the greatest percentage2?

  1. AMonday to Tuesday
  2. BTuesday to Wednesday
  3. CWednesday to Thursday
  4. DThursday to Friday
  5. EFriday to Saturday

Reading it properly

  • 1Ratio = production resources = grey bar black bar. The “tens of thousands” cancel out.
  • 2Only rises count, and compare them as a percentage of the starting ratio.

Working it out

  1. Read the bars and work out each day’s ratio (grey black):

    Mon 10 5 = 2
    Tue 10 4 = 2.5
    Wed 12 4 = 3
    Thu 12 5 = 2.4
    Fri 14 7 = 2
    Sat 10 6 ≈ 1.67

  2. Only two changes go up:

    Mon → Tue: 2 → 2.5, up 0.5 out of 2 = 14 = 25%
    Tue → Wed: 2.5 → 3, up 0.5 out of 2.5 = 15 = 20%
    biggest % rise = Monday to Tuesday A
    Trap! Both rises are +0.5, but the same jump is a bigger percentage when you start from a smaller number.
AMonday to Tuesday: the ratio rises from 2 to 2.5, a 25% increase.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ Tuesday to Wednesday also rises by 0.5, but from 2.5, so only 20%.
  • C✗ Wednesday to Thursday the ratio falls (3 → 2.4).
  • D✗ Thursday to Friday the ratio falls (2.4 → 2), even though production jumps to its highest. Watch the ratio, not the bars.
  • E✗ Friday to Saturday the ratio falls (2 → 1.67).
Tip Ratios: 2, 2.5, 3, 2.4, 2, 1.67. Rises: +25% (Mon–Tue) and +20% (Tue–Wed). A.
