1(Q2 in the original paper) LCM & repeating cyclesAnswer B

The fairy lights on a Christmas tree are in three colours, blue, green and silver. The blue lights flash every 5 seconds1, the green lights flash every 8 seconds1 and the silver lights flash every 12 seconds1. The lights have all just flashed at the same time2.

How many times will they all flash at the same time3 in the next 45 minutes4?

  1. A5
  2. B22
  3. C45
  4. D108
  5. E112

Reading it properly

  • 1Three different gaps: 5, 8 and 12 seconds.
  • 2That flash has already happened, so it is not one of the ones you count.
  • 3All three together = a time that 5, 8 and 12 all divide into: a common multiple.
  • 445 minutes. Work in minutes or seconds, but don’t mix them.

Working it out

  1. All three flash together again after the lowest common multiple (LCM) of 5, 8 and 12.

    go up in 12s: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108, 120
    120 8 = 15 ✓ and 120 5 = 24 ✓
    so they all flash together every 120 s = 2 minutes
    Tip LCM trick: count up in the biggest number (12) and stop at the first one that the others divide. It must end in 0 because of the 5.

  2. Picture the first 2 minutes. Lots of near misses, but all three only line up at 0 and 120 s:

  3. Now count the together-flashes in the next 45 minutes:

    one every 2 minutes at 2, 4, 6, …, 44 minutes
    45 2 = 22.5 only 22 whole ones fit
    answer = 22
    Trap! The flash at 0 minutes has already happened, so don’t count it. The next one after 44 minutes is at 46 minutes, too late.
B22 times: they line up every 2 minutes, at 2, 4, … 44 minutes.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 5 uses 5 × 8 × 12 = 480 s (8 minutes) as the gap: 45 8 ≈ 5.6. That is a common multiple, but not the lowest one.
  • C✗ 45 uses a gap of 60 s (5 × 12). But 60 is not a multiple of 8, so the green lights are not flashing then.
  • D✗ 108 adds the gaps: 5 + 8 + 12 = 25 s, and 2700 25 = 108. Adding gaps has nothing to do with when they line up.
  • E✗ 112 uses 24 s, the LCM of 8 and 12 only, and forgets the blue lights: 2700 24 = 112.5.
Tip LCM of 5, 8, 12 = 120 s = 2 min. 45 2 = 22.5, so 22 times.

2(Q6 in the original paper) Codes & ciphersAnswer D

I have received the following message that is encrypted with a Caesar cipher:

JYMDWEFTQEBAF

This Caesar cipher replaces each letter of the plaintext (the message to be encrypted) with a letter that is a certain fixed number of positions along1 the 26-letter English alphabet. Knowing this fixed number of positions (the shift parameter) is the key to decrypt the message. For example, if the letter in the plaintext is A (the 1st letter of the alphabet) and the shift parameter is 4, then the encrypted letter is E (the 5th letter of the alphabet). Similarly, with a shift parameter of 7, Y (the 25th letter of the alphabet) is encrypted as F, which is the 32nd letter if the alphabet is repeated immediately after Z2.

I would like to decrypt the message that I have received but I do not know the shift parameter. I only know that the first letter of the plaintext is X3.

What is the sixth letter4 of the decrypted message?

  1. AP
  2. BQ
  3. CR
  4. DS
  5. ET

Reading it properly

  • 1Every letter moves forward by the same amount. Find that amount once and use it for every letter.
  • 2Wrap-around! After Z you go back to A. So counting can go past 26.
  • 3The coded message starts with J, so plain X became J. That gives the shift.
  • 4Count carefully: J Y M D W E. The 6th coded letter is E.

Working it out

  1. Find the shift: X turned into J.

    X is letter 24, J is letter 10
    X → Y → Z is 2 steps, then A … J is 10 more shift = 12
    check: 24 + 12 = 36, and 36 − 26 = 10 = J ✓

  2. To decrypt, go back 12. The 6th coded letter is E (letter 5):

    5 − 12 = −7 wrap round: −7 + 26 = 19
    letter 19 = S
    Tip Going back 12 is the same as going forward 14 (because 12 + 14 = 26). Use whichever you find easier.

  3. See it on a strip: top row is the real letter, bottom row is what it turns into.

  4. Check by decoding the whole message:

    12345678910111213
    JYMDWEFTQEBAF
    XMARKSTHESPOT

    X MARKS THE SPOT. It makes sense, so the shift is right.

DS: the shift is 12, and E goes back 12 places to S (“X MARKS THE SPOT”).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ P needs a shift of 15. That only comes from miscounting the X to J gap by 3. (P is also the 11th letter, not the 6th.)
  • B✗ Q moves E 12 places forward, which codes it again instead of decoding it.
  • C✗ R uses a shift of 13: an off-by-one when counting from X round to J. (R is also the 4th letter of the message.)
  • E✗ T uses a shift of 11 (off by one the other way). It is also what you get by decoding the 7th letter, F, by mistake.
Tip X → J is +12. Sixth coded letter E (5): 5 − 12 + 26 = 19 = S.

4(Q8 in the original paper) Codes & patternsAnswer D

A signal transmitter transmits a message in binary form. (In other words, the message is coded in ‘bits’. Each bit is represented either by the digit ‘1’ or by the digit ‘0’.)

To add an element of security, the signal is embedded within other random bits1 (so that there are other 1s and 0s before and after the message), creating a 26-bit transmission.

A particular 12-bit message2 is transmitted as follows:

01100010111001010011000100

The same message is transmitted again3, as follows:

10101001111011100101000101

What is the 12-bit message contained within these two transmissions4?

  1. A001010011000
  2. B001111011100
  3. C010011000100
  4. D101110010100
  5. E111101110010

Reading it properly

  • 1The random padding can be a different length each time, so the message can sit in a different place.
  • 2Look for a block of exactly 12 bits in a row.
  • 3Key fact: the real message appears in both strings.
  • 4Just test each option: is it in string 1? Is it in string 2?

Working it out

  1. The real message must appear, unbroken, in both transmissions. Random padding can make a fake match in one string, but not in both.

  2. Test the options in the 1st transmission (count the bit where each one starts):

    A: 01100010111001010011000100, found at bit 12 ✓
    B: not there ✗
    C: found at bit 15 ✓
    D: found at bit 7 ✓
    E: not there ✗

  3. Only A, C and D survive. Now the 2nd transmission:

    A: not there ✗
    C: not there ✗
    D: found at bit 11 ✓ D
  4. Trap! Fake matches happen! B and E really are in the 2nd string, and A and C really are in the 1st. That’s why you must check both.
D101110010100: the only option inside both transmissions (bits 7–18 of the first, 11–22 of the second).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is in the 1st transmission (bits 12–23) but nowhere in the 2nd.
  • B✗ B is in the 2nd transmission (bits 6–17) but not in the 1st.
  • C✗ C is in the 1st transmission (bits 15–26) but not in the 2nd.
  • E✗ E is in the 2nd transmission (bits 8–19) but not in the 1st.
Tip Check each option in string 1 (A, C, D are there), then string 2: only D is in both.

5(Q12 in the original paper) Money & best dealAnswer D

Help Yourself! is a restaurant in which customers can help themselves to as much food as they wish from a buffet for a fixed price of $10 for adults and $6 for children1.

A Family Meal Ticket is available for $400 that allows unlimited visits to the restaurant for 2 adults and 2 children2 for one year. Any families with more than 2 children are charged half the children's price for every additional child, every time they eat3.

Mr and Mrs Monroe and their 3 children4 made good use of their Family Meal Ticket last year, eating at Help Yourself! 30 times4.

How much did the Family Meal Ticket save5 the Monroe family last year compared to if they had just paid per visit?

  1. A$470
  2. B$560
  3. C$590
  4. D$650
  5. E$740

Reading it properly

  • 1Normal prices for each visit.
  • 2The $400 only covers four people.
  • 3The 3rd child is not free: half of $6 = $3, on every visit.
  • 45 people, 30 visits.
  • 5Saving = (cost paying each time) − (cost with the ticket).

Working it out

  1. Without the ticket, the whole family pays every visit:

    2 × $10 + 3 × $6 = $20 + $18 = $38 a visit
    30 × $38 = $1140
    Tip 30 × 38: do 30 × 40 = 1200, then take off 30 × 2 = 60. That leaves 1140.

  2. With the ticket, they pay $400 plus the 3rd child at half price each time:

    half of $6 = $3 a visit
    30 × $3 = $90
    $400 + $90 = $490
  3. $1140 − $490 = $650
D$650: $1140 paying per visit against $490 with the ticket.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ $470 forgets the 3rd child in the pay-per-visit cost (30 × $32 = $960), then takes off $490.
  • B✗ $560 charges the 3rd child the full $6 each visit (400 + 180 = 580).
  • C✗ $590 charges the 3rd child half the adult price, $5 (400 + 150 = 550).
  • E✗ $740 forgets the 3rd child’s $3 a visit: 1140 − 400.
Tip Pay per visit 30 × 38 = 1140. Ticket 400 + 30 × 3 = 490. Saving 650.

6(Q13 in the original paper) Counting combinationsAnswer C

Jason’s workplace offers a special deal on any four breakfast items1. He can choose from bacon, sausage, eggs, toast, tomatoes and beans.

Jason will not have bacon and sausage together2, nor will he have egg with beans2.

How many different combinations3 of the six items will be acceptable to Jason?

  1. A2
  2. B3
  3. C4
  4. D5
  5. E6

Reading it properly

  • 1Exactly 4 different items out of 6.
  • 2Two clashing pairs: at most one from each pair.
  • 3Combinations: order on the plate doesn’t matter.

Working it out

  1. Group the six items:

    • {bacon, sausage}: at most one
    • {egg, beans}: at most one
    • toast and tomatoes: no rules

  2. Most he can take is 1 + 1 + 2 = 4, and he needs exactly 4. So no slack at all: one from each pair and both toast and tomatoes.

  3. 2 choices × 2 choices = 4
    Check Long way: 15 ways to choose 4 from 6. Bacon + sausage are together in 6 of them, egg + beans in 6, and both clashes in 1. So 15 − 6 − 6 + 1 = 4.
C4 meals: bacon or sausage, egg or beans, plus toast and tomatoes.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 2 only lets one pair change (e.g. always egg), forgetting that egg/beans is a choice too.
  • B✗ 3 is 15 − 6 − 6 = 3: it takes away the meal with both clashes twice.
  • D✗ 5 needs a 5th meal, but any meal missing toast or tomatoes must use both items from a clashing pair.
  • E✗ 6 is the number of meals with bacon and sausage together: banned, not allowed.
Tip One from each clashing pair plus toast and tomatoes: 2 × 2 = 4.

7(Q14 in the original paper) PercentagesAnswer D

Cyclists in a small town were surveyed about their age. A table was produced to show the percentages and numbers of cyclists in five categories of their age. Some of the survey data was added to the table.

age categoriescumulative percentages1 of cyclistsnumbers of cyclists
up to 10 years15%22702
up to 18 years50%
up to 40 years80%
up to 60 years90%
up to 85 years100%

How many cyclists in the survey were above 40 years of age3?

  1. A180
  2. B200
  3. C270
  4. D360
  5. E540

Reading it properly

  • 1Cumulative = running total. “Up to 40 years: 80%” means 80% are aged 40 or under.
  • 215% of everyone is 270 people. That’s your conversion rate.
  • 3Above 40 = everyone not in the “up to 40” 80%.

Working it out

  1. Above 40 is everything after the 80%:

    100% − 80% = 20% are above 40

  2. Use the 270 to turn percentages into people:

    15% = 270
    1% = 270 15 = 18
    20% = 20 × 18 = 360
    Check No need to find the total. But if you want it: 100 × 18 = 1800 cyclists, and 20% of 1800 = 360.
D360 cyclists: 20% of the survey, and 1% is 18 people.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 180 is 10%, just the 41–60 group (90% − 80%). It forgets the over-60s.
  • B✗ 200 isn’t a whole percentage here (1% = 18). It is 20% of a guessed total of 1000.
  • C✗ 270 is the “up to 10 years” number copied from the table.
  • E✗ 540 is 30%, the 19–40 group (80% − 50%). Wrong end of the table.
Tip Above 40 = 100 − 80 = 20%. 15% is 270, so 5% is 90 and 20% is 360.

8(Q18 in the original paper) Time zonesAnswer D

Tim and Jerry are business partners. Tim lives in New York (Eastern Time Zone) and Jerry lives in San Francisco (Pacific Time Zone). They both agree to meet in Oklahoma (Central Time Zone)1.

time zones relative to Pacific Time Zone
MountainCentralEastern
+1 hour+2 hours2+3 hours2

Tim set off at 10 am local time3 but shortly afterwards he realised he had left his ticket at home. As a result he missed his 4 hour flight4 to Oklahoma, so had to get the 12 pm (midday) flight4 instead.

Jerry travelled by train for 2 hours5 to Salt Lake City (Mountain Time Zone), leaving San Francisco at 6 am local time5. He then immediately boarded a bus from Salt Lake City to Oklahoma, where he arrived 6 hours later5.

Which one of the following is true?

  1. AJerry arrived in Oklahoma 3 hours before Tim.
  2. BJerry arrived in Oklahoma 1 hour before Tim.
  3. CJerry and Tim both arrived in Oklahoma at the same time.
  4. DTim arrived in Oklahoma 1 hour before Jerry.
  5. ETim arrived in Oklahoma 3 hours before Jerry.

Reading it properly

  • 1Compare arrivals on one clock: Oklahoma (Central) time.
  • 2Central = Pacific + 2. Eastern = Pacific + 3, so Central = Eastern − 1.
  • 3Red herring: he missed that flight. Only the 12 pm flight matters.
  • 4Leaves 12 pm New York time, takes 4 hours.
  • 5Jerry: 2 + 6 = 8 hours of travel from 6 am Pacific.

Working it out

  1. Work everything out in Central time (the Oklahoma clocks).

  2. Tim (Eastern clocks, 1 hour ahead of Central):

    12 pm + 4 h = 4 pm Eastern
    4 pm Eastern − 1 h = 3 pm Central

  3. Jerry (Pacific clocks, 2 hours behind Central):

    6 am + 2 h train + 6 h bus = 2 pm Pacific
    2 pm Pacific + 2 h = 4 pm Central
    Tip You never need the Mountain zone. Jerry just travels for 8 hours in a row.

  4. Put both on one Oklahoma-time line:

    Tim 3 pm, Jerry 4 pm Tim arrives 1 hour first D
DTim arrived 1 hour before Jerry: Tim at 3 pm Central, Jerry at 4 pm Central.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Needs Tim to land at 7 pm Central. That happens if you add Eastern’s +3 to Tim’s 4 pm instead of taking 1 hour off.
  • B✗ Leaves Jerry’s arrival in Pacific time (2 pm) and compares it with Tim’s 3 pm Central.
  • C✗ Compares Tim’s 4 pm Eastern with Jerry’s 4 pm Central: two different clocks.
  • E✗ Uses Tim’s original 10 am start as his flight time (landing 2 pm Eastern = 1 pm Central). He missed that flight.
Tip Tim: 12 + 4 = 4 pm Eastern = 3 pm Central. Jerry: 6 + 8 = 2 pm Pacific = 4 pm Central. Tim 1 hour first.

They have looked at the price tariff for tickets and have decided that they would want to book online the day before3 the proposed visit and that they could go either on Martha's birthday or on the day before it4.

The table shows the possible ticket prices.

Reading it properly

  • 1Turning 16 makes Martha an adult (16–59). The day before, she is 15: still a child.
  • 2Brothers are children (3–15). Gran is 62: a senior, not an adult.
  • 3Only the “1 day in advance” column counts. Cover the other two columns.
  • 4Two possible days, and Martha’s age is different on each. Price both.
  • 5Family tickets only cover adults and children, never seniors.
  • 6Find the cheapest mix of tickets on the cheaper day.

  1. Sort the 7 people for each day:

    • day before: Mum, Dad = adults; Martha (15), 14, 12, 9 = 4 children; Gran = senior

    Family of 4 (2 adults + 2 children) $49 + 2 children $22 + Gran $13 = $84
    all single tickets: 2 × $15 + 4 × $11 + $13 = $87
    cheapest = $81 (go the day before)
    Tip Being a child for one more day saves $4: a child ticket is $11, an adult ticket $15.
B$81: go the day before the birthday, Family of 5 + one child + Gran.

  • A✗ $76 uses the “7 days in advance” prices (54 + 10 + 12). They are only booking 1 day ahead.
  • C✗ $84 uses a Family of 4 ticket and pays for two more children separately (49 + 22 + 13).
  • D✗ $87 buys every ticket separately on the day before (30 + 44 + 13), with no family ticket.
  • E✗ $91 pays gate prices (63 + 13 + 15), or buys single tickets on the birthday (45 + 33 + 13).
Tip Day before, Martha is still a child: Family of 5 ($57) + child ($11) + senior ($13) = $81.

  1. Aclothes, furniture, stationery, food
  2. Bfood, clothes, stationery, furniture
  3. Cfood, stationery, clothes, furniture

  • 1Biggest spenders first.
  • 2Average spend = total sales number of customers. Compare each department’s two slices.

    spend per customer ∝ sales slicecustomers slice

11(Q24 in the original paper) CountingAnswer B

A cinema uses magnetic letters to write the title of the most popular film that it is screening on its advertisement board. A set of magnetic letters includes the letters of the English alphabet in both upper and lower cases1, i.e. 26 capital letters and 26 small letters. The cinema has just enough sets2 to be able to write the following title:

STAR WARS The Force Awakens

It is now considering putting this title up all in capital letters3:

STAR WARS THE FORCE AWAKENS

What is the minimum number of extra sets4 of magnetic letters that the cinema needs to buy?

  1. A0
  2. B1
  3. C2
  4. D3
  5. E4

Reading it properly

  • 1One set = one of each capital and one of each small letter. So a set gives only one capital A.
  • 2They own exactly the number of sets the first title needs. Work that out first.
  • 3Now every letter is a capital, so the small letters they own are no use.
  • 4Sets needed now − sets owned.

Working it out

  1. How many sets do they own? Count the most-used letter in the first title (capitals and small letters separately):

    capitals: A in STAR, WARS, Awakens = 3
    small letters: e in The, Force, Awakens = 3
    every other letter is used 2 times or fewer they own 3 sets

  2. Now count capitals in STAR WARS THE FORCE AWAKENS:

    A: STAR, WARS, AWAKENS (twice) = 4
    S, R and E: 3 each
    need 4 sets, own 3 buy 1
    Trap! A small e can’t stand in for a capital E, and a set holds only one capital A. So only the count of capitals matters now.
B1 extra set: the all-capitals title needs 4 capital As, and they own 3 sets.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 0 assumes the 3 sets cover the new title, but AWAKENS in capitals adds a 4th capital A.
  • C✗ 2 would only be right if they owned 2 sets. The first title needs 3 capital As (and 3 small es), so they own 3.
  • D✗ 3 assumes they own just 1 set, so 4 − 1 = 3.
  • E✗ 4 is the number of sets needed in total. They already have 3.
Tip First title: 3 capital As → 3 sets owned. All capitals: 4 As → 4 sets. Buy 1.

12(Q25 in the original paper) Percentages & proportionAnswer C

I run a bakery and sell two sizes of loaf. Small loaves (weighing 240 g)1 sell for 80p, and large loaves (weighing 400 g)1 sell for £1.20.

I buy dough, ready-made, at the start of each day. Small boxes (5 kg of dough)2 cost me £10.00 each, and large boxes (15 kg of dough)2 cost me £20.00 each.

In August, Bill used his new, UK-based Bridgford Bank credit card for the first time. The summary details for this card are shown below.

Bridgford Bank Credit Card Summary Details

No interest for the first 12 months1.

2.5% charged on cash withdrawals (minimum charge £3)2.

3.5% charged on all transactions made abroad (minimum £5)2.

All charges are added to the account3 at the time of the transaction and then the minimum monthly payment of 2% of the total shown on the statement3 is calculated at the end of each month.

He bought goods to the value of £24504. He also withdrew £300 in cash from a UK cash machine4. Then, on holiday in Spain4, he used the card to pay for £500 worth of goods4.

What would be the minimum payment5 for his card use in August?

  1. A£65.00
  2. B£65.16
  3. C£65.25
  4. D£65.50
  5. E£65.56

Reading it properly

  • 1No interest to add. Ignore.
  • 2Use the percentage, unless it comes to less than the minimum.
  • 3Charges go onto the total first, then take 2% of everything.
  • 4UK goods: no charge. UK cash: 2.5%. Spain: 3.5%.
  • 52% of the statement total.

Working it out

  1. Work out the charges (check each against its minimum):

    cash: 2.5% of £300 = £7.50 more than £3, so £7.50
    Spain: 3.5% of £500 = £17.50 more than £5, so £17.50
    UK goods: no charge
    Tip 2.5% of 300: 1% is 3, so 2.5% is 7.50. 3.5% of 500: 1% is 5, so 3.5% is 17.50.

  2. Add everything onto the statement, then take 2%:

    2450 + 300 + 7.50 + 500 + 17.50 = £3275
    1% = 32.75, so 2% = £65.50
D£65.50: 2% of £3275 (spending £3250 plus £7.50 and £17.50 of charges).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £65.00 is 2% of £3250, leaving out both charges.
  • B✗ £65.16 uses the minimum charges (£3 + £5) instead of the percentages: 2% of £3258. Minimums only apply when the percentage is smaller.
  • C✗ £65.25 adds only £12.50 of charges (£7.50 for cash but just the £5 minimum for Spain): 2% of £3262.50.
  • E✗ £65.56 charges 3.5% on the cash as well (£10.50 + £17.50): 2% of £3278. The cash came from a UK machine.
Tip Charges 7.50 + 17.50 = 25. Total 3250 + 25 = 3275. 2% = 65.50.

  2. B$1 210 000

25(Q50 in the original paper) CalendarsAnswer A

The company that I currently work for pays its employees monthly. Pay day is the fourth Thursday of every month or the 26th, whichever is earlier1. Since I joined the company the dates on which I have been paid (in order) have been:

24th
24th2
26th
26th
23rd
26th
25th (last month)3

On what date will I be paid this month4?

  1. A22nd
  2. B23rd
  3. C24th
  4. D25th
  5. E26th

Reading it properly

  • 1The 4th Thursday is always the 22nd to 28th. If it’s the 26th, 27th or 28th, pay day is the 26th instead.
  • 2Same date two months running: the clue that unlocks the months.
  • 3A real Thursday (25th is before the 26th).
  • 4The month after the 25th.

Working it out

  1. How does the 4th Thursday move from one month to the next? Four weeks is 28 days, so:

    28-day month (February): same date
    30-day month: 2 days earlier
    31-day month: 3 days earlier
    (if that drops below the 22nd, add 7: the date jumps a week later)

  2. Paid the 24th twice in a row, so the date didn’t move: the first of those months had 28 days. That’s February. Now follow the months:

    Mar 24 − 3 = 21, too early, + 7 = 28 (Apr) → paid 26th ✓
    Apr 28 − 2 = 26 (May) → paid 26th ✓
    May 26 − 3 = 23 (Jun) → paid 23rd ✓
    Jun 23 − 2 = 21, + 7 = 28 (Jul) → paid 26th ✓
    Jul 28 − 3 = 25 (Aug) → paid 25th ✓
    Aug 25 − 3 = 22nd in September
    Check It matches a real year: in 2022 the 4th Thursdays were 24 Feb, 24 Mar, 28 Apr, 26 May, 23 Jun, 28 Jul, 25 Aug and 22 Sep.
AThe 22nd: last month was August (31 days), so the 4th Thursday moves back 3 days from the 25th.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 23rd moves back only 2 days, as if last month had 30 days. It was August, with 31.
  • C✗ 24th moves back only 1 day. No month has 29 days except a leap February, and last month was August.
  • D✗ 25th keeps last month’s date, which only happens after a 28-day February.
  • E✗ 26th would need the 4th Thursday to be the 26th or later, but it moves back from the 25th.
Tip Two 24ths in a row = February then March. Step on: Apr 28, May 26, Jun 23, Jul 28, Aug 25, so Sep 22.
