1(Q2 in the original paper) Speed & meetingAnswer A

Stewart and Michael have arranged to meet. Michael is about to set off on his bicycle, and at the same time Stewart is going to run to meet him1.

Michael can cycle at a steady 20 kilometres per hour2 and Stewart can run at a steady 12 kilometres per hour2. They live 8 kilometres3 from each other.

How long will it be before they meet?

  1. A15 minutes
  2. B16 minutes
  3. C20 minutes
  4. D30 minutes
  5. E32 minutes

Reading it properly

  • 1Both start together and head towards each other, so both of them are closing the gap.
  • 2Moving towards each other? Add the speeds: 20 + 12 = 32 km every hour.
  • 3The gap they have to close between them.

Working it out

  1. Picture the road. Every hour, Michael eats 20 km of the gap and Stewart eats 12 km of it.

  2. closing speed = 20 + 12 = 32 km/h
    time = distance speed = 8 32 = 14 hour
    14 of 60 minutes = 15 minutes
  3. Check In 15 minutes Michael rides 5 km and Stewart runs 3 km. 5 + 3 = 8 km, the whole gap.
A15 minutes: the gap closes at 32 km/h, and 8 km is a quarter of 32.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 16 minutes is too long: in 16 minutes they would cover 32 × 16 60 ≈ 8.5 km, more than the 8 km gap. (16 is the average of the two speeds, not a time.)
  • C✗ 20 minutes is Michael’s speed number turned into minutes. In 20 minutes they would close 32 3 ≈ 10.7 km, too far.
  • D✗ 30 minutes uses the average speed, 16 km/h, for one person: 8 16 = ½ hour. But both are moving, so the speeds add.
  • E✗ 32 is the closing speed in km/h, not a number of minutes.
Tip Towards each other, so add: 32 km/h. 8 km is ¼ of 32, so ¼ hour = 15 minutes.

2(Q6 in the original paper) PercentagesAnswer B

Over the course of a year, Mr and Mrs Jones and their two sons spend an average of £120 a week1 at the local supermarket. 70% of this spending is the weekly family food bill, 20% goes on household goods and the final 10% on store-cupboard ingredients2. When their daughter returns from her gap year to live at home, the weekly food bill increases by 20%3 and their spending on household goods goes up 5%3, whilst they continue to spend the same on store-cupboard ingredients4.

How much is their new average weekly supermarket spend when their daughter returns home?

  1. A£136.80
  2. B£138.00
  3. C£144.00
  4. D£145.00
  5. E£150.00

Reading it properly

  • 1The starting total, split three ways.
  • 2Turn the percentages into pounds first: 70% → £84, 20% → £24, 10% → £12.
  • 3Each rise is a percentage of its own part, not of the whole £120.
  • 4The £12 does not change.

Working it out

  1. Split the £120 into its three parts:

    food: 70% of 120 = £84
    household: 20% of 120 = £24
    store-cupboard: 10% of 120 = £12

  2. Now apply each rise to its own part only:

    food: 20% of 84 = 16.80 84 + 16.80 = £100.80
    household: 5% of 24 = 1.20 24 + 1.20 = £25.20
    store-cupboard: still £12
  3. 100.80 + 25.20 + 12 = £138.00
    Tip Faster: just add the rises. 16.80 + 1.20 = £18, and 120 + 18 = £138.
B£138.00: the food bill rises by £16.80 and household goods by £1.20, so £18 more than £120.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £136.80 adds the food rise (£16.80) but forgets the 5% on household goods.
  • C✗ £144 adds 20% to the whole £120. Only the food bill goes up by 20%.
  • D✗ £145 needs a rise of £25, but the real rise is only £18. It comes from taking 20% of the whole £120 (£24) plus the £1.20 household rise: £145.20.
  • E✗ £150 adds 20% and 5% of the whole £120 (24 + 6 = 30). The percentages belong to the separate parts.
Tip Rises only: 20% of £84 = £16.80, 5% of £24 = £1.20. Total rise £18, so £138.

4(Q8 in the original paper) 3D viewsAnswer C

Below are pictures of three cylinders.

If a cylinder is cut into two equal halves1 with one straight cut2, which one of the following could not3 be a view of one of the halves from any direction4?

  1. Arectangle
  2. Bcircle
  3. Ctriangle
  4. Dsemicircle
  5. Eright-angled triangle

Reading it properly

  • 1Two equal halves: the cut has to pass through the centre of the cylinder.
  • 2One flat cut: across, lengthways or slanting.
  • 3Find the one shape you can never see.
  • 4Any direction: from the top, the end or the side. The cylinder can be tall or flat (that is why three are shown).

Working it out

  1. Try the three kinds of cut and look at one half from the side and from the end:

  2. cut across a shorter cylinder: rectangle (A) from the side, circle (B) from the top
    cut lengthways half a cylinder: semicircle (D) from the end
    slanting cut, corner to corner right-angled triangle (E) from the side

  3. Seen from the side with the cut edge-on, the cylinder is a rectangle and the cut is a straight line through its centre. The half you see is the part of the rectangle on one side of that line, so it always keeps some of the rectangle’s right-angled corners (A or E). Seen from the top or the end you get curves (B or D).

    A triangle with no right angle and its point in the middle of the top (C) never appears C

CC: every possible half shows right-angled corners or curves, never a pointed triangle like C.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is possible: cut across, then look at either half from the side (a squat cylinder looks like a rectangle).
  • B✗ B is possible: cut across, then look down on the half from above. You still see a full circle.
  • D✗ D is possible: cut lengthways down the middle, then look at the half end-on.
  • E✗ E is possible: cut slanting from one top edge to the opposite bottom edge, then look from the side.
Tip Halves need a cut through the centre. From the side that keeps right angles (A, E); from above or the end you get curves (B, D). C is the odd one.

5(Q12 in the original paper) Venn diagramsAnswer B

A merchant bank recently advertised for new graduates to fill positions as trainee analysts; 2301 people applied. Of these, 127 had maths2 as a component of their degree and 89 had economics2 as a component of their degree. Only 45 had neither maths nor economics3 as a component of their degree.

How many must have had both4 maths and economics as a component of their degree?

  1. A14
  2. B31
  3. C59
  4. D83
  5. E171

Reading it properly

  • 1Everyone: the whole box of the Venn diagram.
  • 2These two groups overlap. Anyone with both is counted in both numbers.
  • 3Take these out first: they sit outside both circles.
  • 4The overlap in the middle.

Working it out

  1. people with maths or economics (or both) = 230 − 45 = 185
  2. 127 + 89 = 216
    216 is more than 185 the overlap has been counted twice
    both = 216 − 185 = 31

  3. Fill in the Venn diagram to check:

    maths only = 127 − 31 = 96
    economics only = 89 − 31 = 58
    96 + 31 + 58 + 45 = 230 ✓
B31: 127 + 89 = 216 counts the overlap twice, but only 230 − 45 = 185 people did either subject.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 14 is 230 − 216: it forgets the 45 people who had neither subject.
  • C✗ 59 is 230 − 171, built on option E’s mistake.
  • D✗ 83 is 127 − 89 + 45: subtracting the two groups doesn’t find the overlap.
  • E✗ 171 is 127 + 89 − 45: it takes the “neither” group away from the wrong number.
Tip Either subject = 230 − 45 = 185. Overlap = 127 + 89 − 185 = 31.

6(Q13 in the original paper) Division & limitsAnswer A

A boxed up board game consists of the following components:

  • 1 playing board
  • 50 question cards
  • 6 playing pieces
  • 40 money tokens
  • 12 bonus tokens
  • 2 dice

The producers of the board game have all the component parts manufactured separately and sent to their warehouse, where they box them up and send them to retailers in cartons of 8 complete games1. In their warehouse they have: 98 playing boards; 5,000 question cards; 1,000 playing pieces; 5,000 money tokens; 1,000 bonus tokens and 300 dice2.

What is the maximum number of cartons3 of eight games they can dispatch from their warehouse?

  1. A10
  2. B11
  3. C12
  4. D20
  5. E83

Reading it properly

  • 1Two steps: first count complete games, then pack them 8 to a carton.
  • 2Divide each stock by how many one game needs.
  • 3Cartons, not games! Only full cartons of 8 count.

Working it out

  1. How many complete games could each part make on its own?

  2. The part that runs out first decides: the bonus tokens.

    1000 12 = 83 remainder 4 → 83 games
    83 8 = 10 remainder 3 10 cartons
    Tip 1000 12: 12 × 80 = 960, 12 × 3 = 36, so 12 × 83 = 996 with 4 left over.
A10 cartons: the bonus tokens only stretch to 83 games, which fill 10 cartons of 8 (with 3 games spare).

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 11 cartons need 88 games, but there are only enough bonus tokens for 83.
  • C✗ 12 is 98 8 rounded down: it only checks the playing boards.
  • D✗ 20 is 166 8 rounded down: it only checks the playing pieces.
  • E✗ 83 is the number of complete games, not cartons.
Tip Smallest of 98, 100, 166, 125, 83, 150 is 83 games. 83 8 = 10 full cartons.

7(Q14 in the original paper) Rotations & gridsAnswer B

In a game, circular counters are moved around a board which is a 4 x 4 grid. There are symbols on some of the squares on the board. At one point in the game, the board looks like this (all the circles represent counters which completely cover any symbol on the square that they occupy1):

Which one of the following diagrams could2 show the layout of the board (not necessarily viewed in the same direction as above3)?

  1. Agrid A
  2. Bgrid B
  3. Cgrid C
  4. Dgrid D
  5. Egrid E

Reading it properly

  • 1A counter can hide anything. But a square you can see that is empty must be empty, and the two symbols you can see must be there.
  • 2Find the only option that agrees with every visible square.
  • 3You may turn the board round. Watch which way the bar and hexagon then face!

Working it out

  1. Write down what the board really tells us: 8 squares are visibly blank, 6 are hidden (?), the bar lies flat in row 2 on the left, and the flat hexagon is in the bottom-right corner.

  2. Which turns are allowed? In every option the hexagon lies flat, like on the board. A quarter turn would stand it on its point, so the option must be either the right way up or turned upside down (half turn).

    right way up: hexagon must be bottom-right C or E
    upside down: hexagon must be top-left A or B

  3. Test those four:

    • C: its triangle in the top row, 3rd column sits on a visibly blank square ✗
    • E: its bar in the top row, 3rd column sits on a visibly blank square ✗
    • A upside down: its triangle (row 2, column 2) lands on row 3, column 3, which is blank ✗
    • B upside down: everything lands on a hidden square, the bar or the hexagon ✓
    only B fits
  4. Trap! D is the sneaky one: give it a quarter turn and every symbol lands in a hidden square, and its upright bar even becomes the flat bar. But the hexagon would then stand on its point, and the board’s hexagon is flat.
BGrid B, turned upside down: every symbol is under a counter except the bar and the hexagon, which match.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Upside down, A’s triangle in row 2, column 2 lands on a square we can see is blank.
  • C✗ C’s triangle (top row, 3rd column) is on a square that is visibly blank on the board.
  • D✗ A quarter turn puts the symbols in the right squares, but the flat hexagon would end up standing on its point.
  • E✗ E’s upright bar (top row, 3rd column) is on a visibly blank square.
Tip Hexagon is flat, so only half turns. Hexagon top-left (A, B) or bottom-right (C, E). Check the blank squares: only B survives.

8(Q18 in the original paper) Maps & distanceAnswer A

This diagram shows the positions of three ornithologists on a map. The squares on the map are 1000m along each side1.

The ornithologists are trying to find an injured falcon which is carrying a radio transmitter. Each ornithologist has a radio tracking device. The direction-finding part of the trackers is not working but the signal strength gives an indication of the distance to the bird.

The ornithologists have the following information about the distance from themselves to the bird.

Gordon is at position 22,06; signal strength indicates more than 500m and less than 2000m2.
Helen is at position 23,06; signal strength indicates more than 100m and less than 2000m2.
Ingrid is at position 23,08; signal strength indicates more than 1100m and less than 1800m3.

At which one of the following grid points could4 the bird be located?

  1. A22,07
  2. B21,06
  3. C22,05
  4. D24,07
  5. E23,07

Reading it properly

  • 1One square = 1 km. A diagonal across one square = √2 ≈ 1.41 km (Pythagoras).
  • 2Bands for Gordon and Helen. “Less than 2000 m” means exactly 2000 m is not allowed.
  • 3Ingrid’s band is the narrowest: start with her.
  • 4The point must fit all three bands at once.

Working it out

  1. Grid points can only be certain distances apart:

    1 square straight = 1000 m
    1 square diagonally = √(1² + 1²) = √2 ≈ 1414 m
    2 squares straight = 2000 m

  2. Ingrid needs more than 1100 m and less than 1800 m. 1000 m is too close and 2000 m too far, so the bird is exactly one diagonal step from Ingrid (23,08): that means 22,07 or 24,07.

  3. Check those two against Gordon (22,06):

    22,07: 1 square away = 1000 m between 500 and 2000 ✓
    24,07: 2 across and 1 up = √(4 + 1) = √5 ≈ 2236 m too far ✗

    And Helen to 22,07: one diagonal, 1414 m, between 100 and 2000 ✓. So 22,07

A22,07: 1000 m from Gordon, 1414 m from Helen and 1414 m from Ingrid, inside all three bands.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 21,06 is exactly 2000 m from Helen (needs less than 2000) and about 2830 m from Ingrid.
  • C✗ 22,05 is √10 ≈ 3160 m from Ingrid, far outside her 1800 m limit.
  • D✗ 24,07 is √5 ≈ 2240 m from Gordon, more than his 2000 m limit.
  • E✗ 23,07 is only 1000 m from Ingrid, but she needs more than 1100 m.
Tip Ingrid’s band (1100–1800) only allows a diagonal step: 22,07 or 24,07. Gordon rules out 24,07 (≈ 2.24 km). Answer 22,07.

10(Q20 in the original paper) Same methodAnswer B

A software manufacturer wants to fit some computer programs on to four 2GB discs. He reorders the programs in order of decreasing size1 and places the next programs to be saved in the first available disc that can take that program2.

Which one of the following uses the same method3 as that above?

  1. AA plumber is using lengths of pipe 12 feet long and wishes to cut some lengths. He looks at the combination of lengths to add up to 12 feet and cuts these lengths first. For the remainder he cuts the next length from the first available pipe that is long enough.
  2. BA plumber is using lengths of pipe 12 feet long and wishes to cut some lengths. He reorders the lengths in descending order and cuts the next length from the first available pipe that is long enough.
  3. CA plumber is using lengths of pipe 12 feet long and wishes to cut some lengths. He takes the lengths in the order listed and cuts the next length from the first available pipe that is long enough.
  4. DA plumber is using lengths of pipe 12 feet long and wishes to cut some lengths. He alternately cuts the longest and shortest pieces still needed from the first available pipe which is long enough.
  5. EA plumber is using lengths of pipe 12 feet long and wishes to cut some lengths. He reorders the lengths in ascending order and cuts the next length from the first available pipe that is long enough.

Reading it properly

  • 1Rule 1: sort them, biggest first.
  • 2Rule 2: each one goes into the first container with enough room.
  • 3Discs ↔ pipes, programs ↔ lengths. The match must keep both rules.

Working it out

  1. The method has two rules. Picture it with some example programs:

  2. Now check each plumber against both rules:

    Rule 1: biggest first?Rule 2: first one with room?
    Ano: hunts for combinations that make 12 ftyes (for the rest)
    Byes: descending orderyes
    Cno: order listedyes
    Dno: longest, shortest, longest…yes
    Eno: ascending = smallest firstyes
    only B keeps both rules B
BB: sort the lengths biggest first, then cut each from the first pipe that is long enough.

  • A✗ A looks for combinations that add up to exactly 12 feet first. That is a different plan, and nothing is sorted.

11(Q24 in the original paper) Money & profitAnswer C

Alan bought a silver jug at an auction for £801. A year later, the price of antique silver had risen and he decided to sell it at auction, for which he received £1001. He immediately regretted his decision and approached the buyer, who sold it back to him but he had to pay £1102. Another year later, he needed some money so sold the jug to a dealer for £1201.

How much profit did he make on the jug overall3?

  1. A£10
  2. B£20
  3. C£30
  4. D£40
  5. E£50

Reading it properly

  • 1Money in and out. Write each one down with a + or −.
  • 2Buying it back costs him £110, more than he sold it for.
  • 3Overall = all the money in minus all the money out.

Working it out

  1. Follow the cash, one deal at a time:

  2. money in: 100 + 120 = 220
    money out: 80 + 110 = 190
    profit = 220 − 190 = £30
    Tip Another way: two separate deals. 80 → 100 makes £20, then 110 → 120 makes £10. Total £30.
C£30: he took in £220 and paid out £190.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ £10 is only the second deal (bought at 110, sold at 120).
  • B✗ £20 is only the first deal (bought at 80, sold at 100).
  • D✗ £40 is 120 − 80: it ignores the extra £10 he lost by buying back for more than he sold.
  • E✗ £50 counts the £10 gap between 100 and 110 as a gain instead of a loss.
Tip In 100 + 120 = 220, out 80 + 110 = 190. Profit £30.

12(Q25 in the original paper) TimetablesAnswer D

An airplane flight crew starts its day in Rome and does two round trips1 to London in the day. On each arrival at an airport they take the next scheduled flight back2. The timetable is shown below (all times are local3):

Rome – LondonLondon - Rome
DepartArriveDepartArrive
09:0509:5510:3013:45
12:0512:5513:3016:45
15:0515:5516:3019:45
18:0518:5519:3022:45

How long is it from take-off on their first flight to landing on their last flight4 of the day?

  1. A4h 40min
  2. B7h 40min
  3. C8h 10min
  4. D10h 40min
  5. E13h 40min

Reading it properly

  • 1Rome → London → Rome, twice. Four flights.
  • 2They can’t catch a flight that leaves before they land.
  • 3London is an hour behind Rome. But the day starts and ends in Rome, so both times are on the same clock.
  • 4The whole working day: first take-off to last landing, waiting included.

Working it out

  1. Follow the crew through the timetable (the used flights are highlighted):

    09:05 Rome 09:55 London
    next back: 10:30 London 13:45 Rome
    next out after 13:45: 15:05 Rome 15:55 London (the 12:05 has already gone)
    next back: 16:30 London 19:45 Rome

  2. First take-off 09:05 and last landing 19:45 are both Rome times, so just subtract:

    09:05 → 19:05 = 10 h, → 19:45 = 40 min 10h 40min
    Tip Local times make the flights look odd (50 minutes out, 3 h 15 back). They really take 1 h 50 and 2 h 15. You don’t need this, because the start and end are in the same city.
D10h 40min: from 09:05 to 19:45, both Rome times.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 4h 40min (09:05 to 13:45) is only the first round trip.
  • B✗ 7h 40min (09:05 to 16:45) uses the 12:05 flight for the second trip, but that leaves before they land at 13:45.
  • C✗ 8h 10min adds up the flight times from the table (50 min + 3h 15) twice and leaves out the waiting.
  • E✗ 13h 40min (09:05 to 22:45) waits for the last flight of the day instead of the next one.
Tip 09:05 out, back 13:45, out 15:05, back 19:45. 19:45 − 09:05 = 10h 40min.

13(Q26 in the original paper) Folding & symmetryAnswer A

A sheet of paper has two holes1 cut from it. When it is folded in half2 the hole shown in the diagram below is seen.

Which one of the following could not3 be the unfolded sheet of paper? (The dotted line indicates where it was folded.)

  1. Asheet A
  2. Bsheet B
  3. Csheet C
  4. Dsheet D
  5. Esheet E

Reading it properly

  • 1One hole in each half of the sheet.
  • 2Folding flips one half over onto the other: the left hole becomes its mirror image.
