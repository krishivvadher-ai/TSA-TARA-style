1(Q1 in the original paper) Main conclusion
‘How much does an aeroplane weigh?’; ‘Why are manhole covers round?’. Despite the popularity of bizarre questions like this in interviews for jobs in the big technology companies,Context they have been proved to give little insight into the applicant’s ability or personality.Reason Applicants faced with such questions would be well-advised to reconsider their interest in working for such companies.Main conclusion A study which investigated why these approaches persist concluded that these ‘brainteaser’ questions are popular with interviewers who like to protect and enhance their self-esteem by appearing to know the answers to impossibly difficult questions. They enjoy the feeling of power and superiority over the candidate, revealing a narcissistic or sadistic personality.Evidence / example No-one wants to work for someone like that.Reason

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. ABrainteaser questions are popular in interviews with the big technology companies.✗ Background, brushed aside with “Despite”.
  2. BUse of brainteaser questions in interviews gives little insight into a candidate’s ability or personality.✗ A reason: the questions tell them nothing, so why put up with them.
  3. CInterviewers who use brainteaser questions show personality traits of narcissism or sadism.✗ The study’s finding: evidence for why you would not want that boss.
  4. DJob applicants faced with brainteaser questions should think carefully about working for that company.✓ The advice in the middle: applicants “would be well-advised to reconsider” working there. The useless questions and the study both back it up.
  5. ENo-one wants to work for someone with a narcissistic or sadistic personality.✗ The last sentence: a reason linking the study to the advice.
Tip The conclusion can sit in the middle. Ask which sentence all the others are there to support.
2(Q3 in the original paper) Flaw
A supermarket chain is to remove traditional plastic bags for loose fruit and vegetables from its stores by spring next year and 5p single-use plastic bags by March, replacing them with compostable bags in each case.Context This will save 134 million plastic bags, the equivalent of 500 tonnes of plastic a year, and make it the first nationwide chain to remove them from the fruit and vegetable aisles.Opposing view However, there are problems associated with the compostable bags that will replace the plastic ones.Reason This plan to replace plastic bags should be opposed.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt does not establish that saving 500 tonnes of plastic a year will not be beneficial.✗ It need not deny the benefit. It needs to show the new problems outweigh it.
  2. BIt considers only one alternative to the plastic bags.✗ Only this plan is being judged. Other alternatives don’t matter to whether it beats plastic.
  3. CIt assumes that compostable bags can be used for the same range of food items as plastic bags.✗ Nothing is assumed about which foods the bags can hold.
  4. DIt fails to show that the problems associated with compostable bags would be as severe as those associated with plastic.✓ Plastic has problems too. To oppose the switch you must show the compostable bags’ problems are at least as bad, and the argument never compares them.
  5. EIt fails to provide evidence that there are benefits to removing traditional plastic bags from stores.✗ Untrue: it does give a benefit (134 million bags, 500 tonnes of plastic saved).
Tip “X has problems, so reject X” forgets that what X replaces has problems too. Look for the missing comparison.
3(Q4 in the original paper) Principle
There is often talk of this or that job being ‘exploitative’ or of people on low wages being ‘exploited’ by their employers, as if there were something deeply unjust about paying someone poorly.Opposing view But this misses a fundamental point about injustice. You cannot be unjustly treated voluntarily: if you agree to the action, you are not being unjustly treated.Reason Anyone who accepts a job, regardless of the pay, is not being unjustly treated by their employer.Main conclusion Obviously this does not apply to someone forced into a job against their will.Opposing view But if they have accepted their job, even if the reasons pushing them to accept it might be deemed unfair in a wider sense, the act of employing them cannot itself be deemed unjust.Main conclusion

Which one of the following illustrates the principle used in the above argument?

  1. AAn aid worker who freely goes to work in a war zone and then gets kidnapped and held for ransom cannot complain about the actions of their captors.✗ They agreed to go to a war zone, not to be kidnapped. The harm was never agreed to.
  2. BA patient who gives fully informed consent to take part in a clinical trial for a new drug has no right to complain about experiencing the drug’s stated side effects.✓ She agreed to the trial knowing the side effects, so suffering them is not unjust. Agreeing to the very thing = no injustice.
  3. CA person who deviates from the terms of their contract cannot complain if they are dismissed for misconduct.✗ About breaking a contract and being punished. Nobody agreed to be dismissed.
  4. DIf someone gives out their credit card details to a cold-caller over the phone, they cannot complain about becoming a victim of fraud.✗ Handing over details is not agreeing to fraud: they were tricked.
  5. ESomeone who suffers as a result of the policies of a party they did not vote for has a right to claim they have been unfairly treated.✗ The reverse rule: “didn’t agree, so unfair”. The principle only says agreeing rules injustice out.

Working: write the principle first

Accepted the job not unjustly treated, whatever the pay (unless forced).
So the principle: if you freely agree to something, you are not treated unjustly by it.

BFully informed consent → the stated side effects happen → no complaint. ✓ Agreed to exactly this.
AAgreed to the war zone, not to the kidnapping. ✗
CBroke the contract → dismissed. Punishment for breaking an agreement, not consent to the treatment. ✗
DGave details, but never agreed to fraud: deceived, not consenting. ✗
EDid not agree → unfair. Turns the principle round. ✗
Tip The agreement must cover the very thing complained about. Only B has that.
4(Q5 in the original paper) Draw a conclusion
Genuine apologies are an expression of regret and repentance on the part of the person who has acted wrongly.Reason However, in some recent cases government ministers have apologised for unjust policies of previous governments, in which they personally played no part; some of these actions occurred before the person apologising was even born. Apologies for offensive comments or language are sometimes expressed in terms which suggest that the fault lies with those who are offended: ‘I am sorry if anyone was offended by what I said.’ Some people – especially children – apologise because they are being forced to do so by someone in a position of power over them, such as a parent.Evidence / example

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. AIt can be appropriate for someone to apologise even if they did nothing wrong.✗ The passage hints the opposite: such apologies are not genuine. Whether they are “appropriate” is never judged.
  2. BGovernments are deemed to have a continuing identity, despite changes in personnel.✗ Might explain the ministers, but the passage never says it.
  3. CPeople should apologise if what they say causes offence.✗ The passage criticises “sorry if anyone was offended”. It never says when people should apologise.
  4. DSome apparent apologies are not genuine.✓ A genuine apology is regret from the wrongdoer. Each example lacks that (not their fault, blames the offended, forced). So some apologies are not genuine.
  5. EYou can forgive somebody only if they give a sincere apology.✗ Forgiveness is never mentioned.
Tip A definition followed by examples: the conclusion is usually “these don’t meet the definition”.
5(Q9 in the original paper) Main conclusion
Part-time undergraduate numbers have fallen by more than 50% since 2010Evidence / example – this represents a lost opportunity for people wanting to improve their job options.Intermediate conclusion More needs to be done to provide support for people to take short, flexible part-time courses.Main conclusion A survey was conducted of 830 people who had considered studying part-time, but decided against it or dropped out. They were asked what barriers needed to be removed. Flexibility of courses was seen to be an important factor, with 44% wanting more flexible courses to fit around their other commitments. However, financial concerns were most important, with 59% wanting cheaper tuition fees and 37% wanting more support for living costs.Evidence / example

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. APart-time undergraduate numbers have fallen by more than 50% since 2010.✗ The starting fact: evidence.
  2. BThe decline in part-time undergraduate numbers is a lost opportunity for people wanting to improve their job options.✗ An intermediate step: why the fall matters.
  3. CMore needs to be done to provide support for people to take short, flexible part-time courses.✓ The call to action: “More needs to be done…”. The fall shows why it matters and the survey shows what support is needed.
  4. DFlexibility of courses was seen to be an important factor in people deciding against part-time study.✗ A survey finding: evidence about what would help.
  5. EFinancial concerns are the most important issue for people considering part-time study.✗ A survey finding, stretched from 830 people to everyone.
Tip “More needs to be done” is a classic conclusion. Survey percentages are nearly always evidence.
6(Q10 in the original paper) Weaken
The mobile phone was said to be the ultimate liberating device. Rather than being forced to stay, not just inside a house, not just in a particular room, but almost literally chained to a wall, you could now go anywhere to talk to someone.Opposing view The irony is, however, that mobile phones have actually made us a lot less mobile.Intermediate conclusion Not only are we more sedentary than ever before, but evidence suggests we now spend longer indoors than we did before mobile phones became prevalent.Evidence / example We should rename our beloved devices ‘immobile’ phones.Main conclusion Why would we feel the need to go anywhere, after all, if we can obtain all the information we need and communicate with anyone we want from the comfort of our own homes?Reason

Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the above argument?

  1. ANot everyone owns a mobile phone.✗ Phones can still cause the trend among the many who do own one.
  2. BThere are other sources of technology besides phones through which people can communicate effectively and procure information.✗ Other gadgets might add to the effect, but that doesn’t clear phones.
  3. CMany people use their phones to help monitor their physical activity in the hope that it will get them to exercise more.✗ Using phones “in the hope” of exercising doesn’t show anyone actually moves more.
  4. DPeople may want to leave their homes for reasons other than communicating with others or to obtain information.✗ People may want to go out, but the evidence says they stay in longer anyway.
  5. EPeople who do not own mobile phones exhibit the same trend towards spending longer indoors.✓ The argument says phones caused us to stay in more. If people without phones show the same trend, something else is causing it.
Tip A cause claimed from a before-and-after trend? Look for a group without the cause that shows the same effect.
7(Q11 in the original paper) Parallel reasoning
A lot of people who fear snakes imagine that their skin is slimy.Evidence / example Snakes are not slimy but have smooth, dry skin.Reason Therefore, if these people were better informed, they would lose their fear of snakes.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AMany people who are on Facebook think they have more friends than people who are not. They don’t have more friends, so they should stop using Facebook.✗ Has a false belief, but ends with what they should do, not what they would do once informed.
  2. BA lot of birds that get fed become lazy about finding food, and are less able to survive in the wild. If people knew this, they would stop feeding birds.✗ No false belief: it is a true fact people don’t know.
  3. CMany people who know that ice floats do not know it is less dense than water. Ice is less dense than water. If people knew this, they would know why ice floats.✗ A gap in knowledge leading to understanding, not a false belief behind an attitude.
  4. DA lot of so-called modern art is regarded as rubbish by people who visit galleries. Not all art is rubbish. If it were, people would not go to art galleries.✗ No false belief behind an attitude, and no “if informed, they would…”.
  5. EMany people who won’t vote Conservative think that Conservatives are all posh. If they knew that not all Conservatives are posh, they would be willing to vote for them.✓ Same shape: people with an attitude hold a false belief; it is false; once informed, they would drop the attitude.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: many people with attitude X (fear snakes) believe B (slimy) B is false so if they knew, they would drop X.

EMany with X (won’t vote Conservative) believe B (all posh) → B is false (“not all… posh”) → if they knew, they’d drop X (be willing to vote). ✓ Same shape.
AFacebook users believe B (more friends) → B false → they should stop. A recommendation, not “informed, they would”. ✗
BTrue fact (fed birds get lazy) → if people knew, they’d stop. No mistaken belief corrected. ✗
CMissing knowledge → if they knew, they’d understand. No attitude dropped. ✗
DArt seen as rubbish → not all art is → if it were, nobody would go. A different argument. ✗
Tip Match all three parts: an attitude, a false belief behind it, and “correct the belief and the attitude goes”.
8(Q15 in the original paper) Assumption
The government should be active in promoting marriage.Main conclusion Notwithstanding bad marriages,Opposing view research shows that married adults are happier, have better health, are less likely to be involved in crime, and enjoy many other advantages related to their personal welfare and the welfare of their family. Research also shows that children are most likely to flourish in the married-couple family.Evidence / example Marriage rates are declining and non-marital birth rates are rocketing, taking a toll on the nation’s welfare.Reason Government promotion of marriage is not only justified but also necessary.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the above argument?

  1. APeople are strongly against government promotion of marriage.✗ Strong opposition would count against the argument, not be assumed by it.
  2. BThe government has a right to influence the family arrangements of its citizens.✓ The benefits of marriage only make government promotion “justified” if the government has a right to influence how families are arranged.
  3. CPromotion of marriage is the most important endeavour for the government.✗ “Most important” is far too strong. It only needs to be worth doing.
  4. DThe government is already involved in the promotion of marriage.✗ Not needed: the argument works whether or not the government already does this.
  5. EThe nation’s welfare problems would be resolved if more people raised children within a marriage.✗ “Would be resolved” is too strong. Marriage only needs to help.
Tip Negation test: “the government has NO right to influence family life”. Then “justified” falls apart.
9(Q16 in the original paper) Principle
Many drivers think that the laws that make it compulsory to use a seat belt are an intrusion into their private lives – a denial of an autonomous lifestyle choice.Opposing view But state intervention in our freedom is justified when, because of our short-sightedness, impulsiveness or carelessness, we act in a way that actually or potentially restricts our freedom – especially when dangerous and irreversible consequences are involved. In those cases, the law should protect our future selves from the short-sighted and foolish choices of our earlier selves. It should require us to act in the way that we would act if we carefully took into account our long-term welfare.Reason The legal requirement to wear a seat belt is thus a protection of drivers’ freedom and future selves.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best illustrates the principle used in the above argument?

  1. APedestrians are legally prohibited from crossing railway tracks except at designated points.✓ A law that stops people taking a careless shortcut that could kill them: the law protecting our future selves from impulsive choices.
  2. BPassengers must be in possession of a valid ticket when boarding a train.✗ A rule about paying fares, not about protecting the traveller.
  3. CThe state funds a campaign to raise awareness of the health hazards of drug use.✗ Information only. Nobody is legally required to act.
  4. DCigarettes are legally required to be in plain, unbranded packs.✗ A rule for cigarette makers. It doesn’t require people themselves to act for their long-term welfare.
  5. ECyclists are advised to wear a helmet to protect their head.✗ Advice only. The principle is about the law requiring it.

Working: write the principle first

Careless choice that risks dangerous, irreversible harm to yourself the law should require what your careful self would do seat belts.
So the principle: the law may force people to protect themselves from their own short-sighted choices when the harm could be serious and lasting.

ALaw bans crossing tracks except at safe points: stops a careless shortcut that could kill you. ✓ Same principle.
BTicket rule: protects the fares, not the passenger. ✗
CA campaign informs; it doesn’t require anything. ✗
DA law on packaging for manufacturers; smokers are not required to do anything. ✗
E“Advised”, not legally required. ✗
Tip Check each part: a legal requirement, on the person themselves, to protect them from their own risky choice.
10(Q17 in the original paper) Flaw
Intensive farming, designed to give the greatest food yield per hectare, is widely believed to create higher levels of pollution and damage to the environment than organic farming.Opposing view But the reality is that the opposite is the case:Main conclusion a global research team measured what they call ‘externalities’ – the environmental costs such as greenhouse gas emissions, fertiliser and water use – of producing a given amount of food on both high-yield and low-yield farms. In its study of dairy farming in Switzerland, it found that high-yield systems were actually less damaging to the environment than organic, which by contrast took up twice as much land for the same volume of milk and caused at least one-third more soil loss than conventional dairy farming.Evidence / example

Which one of the following best expresses the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt confuses externalities with the central issues of food production and land use.✗ Externalities are the environmental costs being compared, so nothing is confused.
  2. BIt assumes that any land which is not used for farming will instead be used for environmental projects.✗ The argument says nothing about what spare land would be used for.
  3. CIt claims that high-yield farming is environmentally friendly, then draws this as a conclusion.✗ It gives evidence (the study) for its claim, so it is not circular.
  4. DIt bases a broad generalisation on the specific example of Swiss dairy farming.✓ One study of Swiss dairy farms is used to claim intensive farming in general is less damaging (“the opposite is the case”).
  5. EIt ignores the economic realities that are the real motives for intensive farming.✗ Economic motives don’t change which kind of farming harms the environment more.
Tip Compare the size of the evidence with the size of the conclusion: one example against a general rule.
11(Q21 in the original paper) Main conclusion
If we were ever to detect an alien transmission from outer space, ought we to reply?Context There are good grounds for keeping silent. After all, how do we know that any such aliens will be peaceable?Opposing view But the truth is that it is too late for such precautions.Main conclusion Since World War II, we’ve been broadcasting TV, radio, and radar inadvertently into the heavens.Reason Admittedly, these signals would be very weak at light years’ distance.Opposing view But the difficulty extraterrestrials would have in picking these up is nothing compared to the challenge they’d face in launching weaponry our way.Reason Any society with the capability to threaten Earth will certainly already have the kit required to pick up the leakage we’ve been wafting skyward for seven decades.Intermediate conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AThere are good grounds for keeping silent if we ever happen across an alien transmission.✗ The opposing view the author rejects.
  2. BWe do not know that any aliens we encounter will be peaceable beings.✗ The reason behind the opposing view.
  3. CIt is too late to worry about keeping silent if we come across an alien transmission.✓ “But the truth is that it is too late for such precautions”: keeping silent is pointless. C rewords it.
  4. DIt is easier for extraterrestrials to pick up signals than to launch weaponry.✗ A reason that leads to the last sentence.
  5. EA society which has the capability to threaten Earth likely possesses the technology to be aware of our existence.✗ An intermediate conclusion that supports “too late” (and softened from “certainly” to “likely”).
Tip “But the truth is…” straight after an opposing view is a strong conclusion signal.
12(Q22 in the original paper) Strengthen
We have long known that if women take folic acid supplements during the first months of pregnancy (and preferably just before getting pregnant too), it decreases the likelihood of babies being born with neural tube defects. Research has shown, however, that not enough women in the UK are choosing to take the supplements.Evidence / example Obviously, the Government’s public information campaign about folic acid supplementation is not succeeding.Intermediate conclusion It is time that the UK followed the lead of countries that require that flour and some other basic or staple foods are fortified with folic acid during the manufacturing process.Main conclusion This strategy provides the necessary levels of folic acid to women who become pregnant, without the need for supplements and without bringing any harm to the wider population.Reason

Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the above argument?

  1. ADiet trends mean that increasing numbers of women do not eat food made with flour.✗ Fewer women eating flour means fortified flour reaches fewer of them: this weakens.
  2. BWomen may not realise they are pregnant until after the first months of the pregnancy have passed.✓ If women don’t know they are pregnant in the key early months, they can’t choose supplements in time. Fortified food reaches them anyway.
  3. CIt is possible to add folic acid to flour and other foods at home after buying them.✗ Home fortification still relies on women choosing to do it, the very thing that is failing.
  4. DSome people object to what they see as excessive government interference with food production.✗ Objections to interference count against the plan.
  5. EWomen need adequate levels of calcium and vitamin D to ensure a healthy pregnancy.✗ Calcium and vitamin D have nothing to do with folic acid.
Tip Strengthen: find why the voluntary route can’t work, so fortifying food is needed.
13(Q23 in the original paper) Draw a conclusion
Conditions in some of the UK’s prisons are scandalous.Intermediate conclusion Inspectors found some prisons to be dirty, vermin-infested places, with frightening levels of violence and prisoners having easy access to illegal drugs.Evidence / example Some critics attribute the poor management of these prisons to the fact that the government has allowed private companies to run many prisons, although inspectors also criticised some state-run prisons. Others say the real problem is one of drastic funding cuts, affecting both state-run and privately-run prisons.Context Either way, we have allowed the situation to become very serious – and why? Because for the most part, we do not care.Intermediate conclusion The truth is that, personally, we can much better imagine that at some point we’ll need a clean, well-run hospital than a decent criminal justice system.Reason

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. APeople are more likely to support spending on hospitals than on prisons.✓ We don’t care about prisons because we can picture needing a hospital but not a prison. So people will back hospital spending more readily.
  2. BThe general public are not sufficiently aware of the scandalous prison conditions.✗ The passage says we don’t care, not that we don’t know.
  3. CThe government should not allow private companies to run prisons.✗ The passage reports this view, then sets it aside (“Either way”).
  4. DFunding cuts are the cause of the poor conditions in prisons, both state-run and private.✗ Also just a view it reports, not one it accepts.
  5. EHospitals have been made a greater priority for spending than prisons.✗ Nothing is said about what has actually been spent on hospitals.
Tip Two views followed by “Either way”: the conclusion won’t pick one of them.
14(Q27 in the original paper) Principle
Rhona is a self-employed IT technician. Some of her clients are also friends. When she checks a computer for problems, she sees what is stored on the hard disk.Context She has sometimes teased friends in front of others about the websites they have been visiting and the pictures she has seen stored on their computers.Evidence / example She should not have done this,Main conclusion because it is wrong for people to reveal sensitive information which they know only because of their work.Reason

Which one of the following illustrates the principle used in the above argument?

  1. AAnyone who, in the course of their work, discovers evidence of terrorism should report it to the police.✗ Says you should reveal what you find at work: the opposite.
  2. BPolice officers should be dismissed if they pass confidential information to journalists about cases they have investigated.✓ Police learn case details through their job, and passing them on is wrong (so they should be dismissed). Same rule.
  3. CDoctors and nurses should have access to patients’ medical records in order to give them the correct advice and treatment.✗ About having access to information, not revealing it.
  4. DEx-boyfriends and ex-girlfriends should not share personal or intimate information about their former partners, because such knowledge is private.✗ Right idea, but the information comes from a relationship, not from work.
  5. ETeachers often come to know quite a lot of information about the families of their pupils, which they should report if they have any concerns.✗ Says teachers should report what they learn: the opposite.

Working: write the principle first

Rhona sees private things through her work she reveals them wrong.
So the principle: do not reveal sensitive information you only know because of your job.

BPolice learn case details at work → pass them to journalists → wrong, so dismiss. ✓ Same principle.
AWork discovery → should report it. Revealing is right here. ✗
CAccess to records, nothing revealed. ✗
DDon’t reveal private things, but learned in a relationship, not a job. ✗
ELearned at work → should report concerns. Revealing again. ✗
Tip Both halves must match: learned through work, and revealing it is wrong. D only has the second half.
15(Q28 in the original paper) Parallel reasoning
Following a few simple rules can help you keep your job.Context For instance, if you arrive late for work, you will definitely get fired.Reason You lost your last jobEvidence / example so you must have arrived late for work.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AYou need a membership card to use the services of the gym. You do not have a membership card so you cannot use the gym.✗ Valid: the card is needed, there is no card, so no gym.
  2. BIf a company wants to be successful, it needs to insist on the punctuality of its employees. This company does not take the punctuality of its employees seriously, so it will not be successful.✗ Valid: punctuality is needed for success, it is missing, so no success.
  3. CDisciplinary action is initiated against all workers who do not wear a helmet on the building site. Meera must have always worn a helmet on the site because no disciplinary action has been initiated against her.✗ Valid: no helmet always leads to action; no action, so a helmet was worn.
  4. DIf you turn up the volume, the music gets louder. The music is very loud now, which means that you must have turned up the volume.✓ Same flaw: if A then B; B happened; so A. The music could be loud for another reason, just as you can be fired for another reason.
  5. EIn order to anger a hippo, it is enough to get close to it in a boat. They are getting close to this one, so it will become angry.✗ Valid: getting close is enough, and they are getting close.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: if A (late) B (fired). B happened (lost your job) so A. Flawed: B can happen in other ways.

DIf A (turn up volume) → B (louder). B (very loud) → so A. ✓ Same reversal.
ANeed A (card) for B (gym). No A → no B. Valid. ✗
BNeed A (punctuality) for B (success). No A → no B. Valid. ✗
CIf A (no helmet) → B (action). Not B → so not A. Valid. ✗
EA (getting close) is enough for B (angry). A → so B. Valid. ✗
Tip The original is flawed, so the answer must be flawed in the same way. Four valid options can be crossed out fast.
16(Q29 in the original paper) Main conclusion
As a result of a recent initiative, energy providers are encouraging their customers to have ‘smart meters’ installed in their properties.Context Smart meters are recommended because they help you to control your energy usage and therefore mean that you will have lower bills.Opposing view However, you should refuse to have one installed.Main conclusion The device only tells you how much energy you are using – it doesn’t suggest a cheaper alternativeReason and, as a result, the information is not useful to the customer.Intermediate conclusion In fact, the information is far more useful to the energy companies in saving them money.Reason

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. ASmart meters help you to control your energy usage.✗ The opposing view: why meters are recommended.
  2. BHaving a smart meter will lead to having lower bills.✗ Also the opposing view, which the author rejects.
  3. CYou should refuse to have a smart meter installed.✓ “However, you should refuse to have one installed”: the advice that everything after it supports.
  4. DSmart meters cannot suggest ways for you to reduce your energy usage.✗ A reason, and reworded: it says no cheaper alternative is suggested.
  5. EThe information provided by smart meters is only useful to the energy companies.✗ A reason, and overstated: “far more useful” is not “only useful”.
Tip “However, you should…” right after the other side’s case is the conclusion.
17(Q33 in the original paper) Assumption
Rats, like wood pigeons, badgers and foxes, are synurbic – i.e. a species that thrives in urban habitats.Context Cities provide perfect conditions for rats, offering warmth and shelter and plentiful supplies of food – and cities are getting bigger.Reason Human antipathy to rats is well founded: they breed quickly and spread disease. Until recently, unwanted rats have been controlled by the use of warfarin, a blood-thinning (anticoagulant) drug used medicinally on humans. It has been the perfect poison for rats, being odourless and colourless; rats will return to eat food laced with warfarin until a lethal dose is accumulated.Context But rats are developing resistance to warfarin and passing this resistance to their offspring. We do not yet have an alternative.Reason The rat population will continue to increase as cities grow.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the above argument?

  1. AAlternatives to warfarin to control the rat population will not be found.✓ “We do not yet have an alternative” only leads to “will continue to increase” if no alternative turns up.
  2. BWarfarin will become a less effective anticoagulant for humans.✗ Warfarin’s use in humans has nothing to do with rat numbers.
  3. COther synurbic animals are less dangerous than rats.✗ Other synurbic animals are only a comparison. The argument doesn’t need them.
  4. DWe have no protection against the diseases carried by rats.✗ Disease protection doesn’t affect whether rat numbers rise.
  5. EAll humans experience antipathy to rats.✗ “All humans” is too strong, and disliking rats doesn’t change their numbers.
Tip “Not yet” in a reason and “will continue” in the conclusion: the gap is the future.
18(Q34 in the original paper) Flaw
The dark tendencies humans may have, such as psychopathy (lack of empathy), narcissism (excessive self-absorption), egoism and sadism are based on the same single underlying source: a drive to maximize one’s individual interests, and to disregard or malevolently cause harm to others’ interests. These tendencies often underlie the most serious violent crime in any society, usually dealt with by imprisonment.Context Psychiatrists say that people with these characteristics are notoriously difficult to change.Evidence / example This suggests that these personality traits are likely to be inherited genetic dispositions over which people have no control. It is wrong, therefore, to punish people for the actions that have resulted from such tendencies.Intermediate conclusion The justice system should not imprison someone for actions over which they had no control.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt fails to show the research on which the psychiatrists’ conclusions were based.✗ Not quoting the research is not where the reasoning goes wrong.
  2. BIt ignores differences in punishments between different countries.✗ Other countries’ punishments are irrelevant.
  3. CIt implies that imprisonment is the only option for sentencing.✗ It argues against prison. It never says prison is the only option.
  4. DIt suggests that ways of altering serious antisocial behaviour can never be found.✗ It says “difficult to change”, not “can never be changed”.
  5. EIt assumes that the only objective of imprisonment is punishment.✓ “Wrong to punish” only leads to “should not imprison” if prison is just punishment. It also protects the public from dangerous people.
Tip Watch a word swap between steps: “punish” in one, “imprison” in the next. Do they mean the same?
19(Q35 in the original paper) Weaken
Surely there can be few things more commendable than donating to charitable organisations.Opposing view Yet there are compelling strategic reasons to rethink the charitable model used for tackling social problems in many countries.Main conclusion Relying on voluntary public donations to fund charitable work can leave important but less popular causes without the support they require.Reason For instance, many people are more willing to donate to charities assisting cancer patients or neglected animals than to those helping drug addicts, who are often viewed less sympathetically.Evidence / example Also, in some countries, the largest donors are wealthy individuals given tax reductions in exchange for their charitable donations. Such ‘tax breaks’ reduce the government’s revenue, meaning that they have less money to fund a more comprehensive public service approach to addressing society’s problems.Reason

Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the above argument?

  1. AMany non-wealthy taxpayers would prefer to give money to charities of their own choosing rather than to government agencies.✗ What donors prefer doesn’t show which model tackles social problems better.
  2. BCharities are often better than governments at testing innovative approaches that make a significant difference to tackling social problems.✓ If charities are often better at finding new approaches that really work, that is a strong strategic reason to keep the charity model.
  3. CThe number of charitable organisations is much higher in some parts of the world than in others.✗ Where charities are found doesn’t affect the case.
  4. DSome people in real need of assistance are more reluctant to accept help from charities than from government bodies.✗ This favours government help: it strengthens.
  5. EMost money donated to charity is spent on the administrative costs of running charities, rather than on the causes they are meant to help.✗ Waste in charities strengthens the case for rethinking them.
Tip To weaken “rethink X”, give a strong advantage of X that the argument ignores.
20(Q39 in the original paper) Main conclusion
In 1992, the bestseller Men Are from Mars, Women Are from Venus was published. Decades later, even in places where women and men occupy relatively similar positions in society, the notion of fundamental psychological differences between the sexes still has great appeal.Context However, this idea that ‘men and women are from different planets’ is as unfounded as it is enduring.Main conclusion Research has found no evidence of such profound, unalterable differences.Reason Studies have debunked, for instance, the myth that women’s communication is more cooperative, and men’s more competitive. Many observed differences have been shown to be context-dependent and disappear when social circumstances are changed.Evidence / example But a news headline of ‘Men and women pretty similar, research finds’ just wouldn’t grab the same attention, would it?Reason

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AThe idea that ‘men and women are from different planets’ has appeal even where relatively high levels of gender equality exist.✗ Background: the idea’s lasting appeal.
  2. BResearch has found no reliable evidence of fundamental or unalterable differences between men and women.✗ The main reason for “unfounded”, not the conclusion.
  3. CThe idea that ‘men and women are from different planets’ is as unsupported as it is persistent.✓ The “However” sentence: unfounded and enduring = unsupported and persistent. The research backs “unfounded”; the headline joke explains “enduring”.
  4. DOld-fashioned notions about the sexes remain a stumbling block to gender equality.✗ Gender equality being held back is never claimed.
  5. EMany apparent differences between women and men are actually dependent on the social context.✗ Evidence backing up the reason.
Tip The right answer is often a paraphrase: “unfounded… enduring” becomes “unsupported… persistent”.
21(Q40 in the original paper) Parallel reasoning
In order for a gritted road surface to freeze over, temperatures must fall to –10 °C or below. Above this temperature, grit will continue to melt ice on the road.Reason Since ice has formed even on treated surfaces in recent weeks,Evidence / example the temperature must have fallen below –10 °C at times.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AIn order to make bread successfully it is essential that the oven reaches a temperature of 200 °C or above. If this is not reached or maintained, the bread will not rise properly. Since this bread has not risen, the oven must not yet be up to temperature.✗ Reverses the rule: “not hot enough, so it won’t rise” does not give “not risen, so not hot enough”. Other things stop bread rising.
  2. BTemperatures of 37 °C and above cause chocolate to melt and release some of the oils it contains. When it cools again a whitish residue is left behind. Since this chocolate has a white residue, at some point it must have been kept at a temperature exceeding 37 °C.✗ 37 °C is shown to be enough to cause the residue, not needed for it. The residue could have another cause.
  3. CA fever is medically defined as an oral temperature exceeding 37.7 °C, but a diagnosis of fever may be given below this point. Since the patient’s notes state that he had a fever on admission to hospital, we can be confident that his temperature was 37.7 °C or above at that time.✗ It admits a fever can be diagnosed below 37.7 °C, so the temperature is not needed for the outcome. The conclusion doesn’t follow.
  4. DIron melts at temperatures in excess of 1538 °C. Without reaching this temperature it is impossible to cast iron. Although we do not know exactly how some of the earliest furnaces worked, since we have found evidence of cast iron in China from around 550 BCE, we can be sure that these ancient people found ways to reach temperatures exceeding 1538 °C.✓ Same shape: a temperature is needed for something; it happened; so that temperature must have been reached. Cast iron needs over 1538 °C, cast iron was found, so they got over 1538 °C.
  5. EIdeally coffee should be stored below 5 °C once ground in order to help preserve its flavour. Stale coffee tastes bitter and can have a musty aroma. Since the coffee has been kept at room temperature, we should expect complaints about its flavour.✗ Predicts a result from a cause. It doesn’t work back from an outcome to the temperature it needs.
Tip Ask whether the temperature is needed for the result (safe to work back) or just enough to cause it (not safe).

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: Y (gritted road freezes) needs X (–10 °C or below) Y happened (ice on treated roads) so X happened. Valid: work back from a result to a condition it needs.

DY (cast iron) needs X (over 1538 °C) → Y happened (cast iron from 550 BCE) → so X. ✓ Same shape. “We do not know how the furnaces worked” is just a side remark.
ANot X → not Y. Bread didn’t rise (not Y) → so not X. The rule run backwards. ✗
BX (37 °C or more) causes Y (residue) → Y → so X. X is enough, not needed. ✗
CX defines Y, but Y can be given without X → Y → so X. The premise itself says X is not needed. ✗
EX is ideal → not X → expect bad flavour. A forward prediction, nothing worked back. ✗
Tip Needed (necessary): if the result happened, the condition was met. Enough (sufficient): the result could have come another way.
22(Q41 in the original paper) Draw a conclusion

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. AThe placebo effect is considerably more powerful than people typically give it credit for.✗ How powerful the placebo effect is never comes up.
  2. BAll medicine involves interplay between the body and the mind.✗ “All medicine” goes far beyond the passage.
  3. CIf they want to be consistent, advocates of alternative medicine ought to be more accepting of the possible role that the placebo effect plays in their treatments.✓ They praise mind and body working together, yet object to the placebo effect, which is exactly that. To be consistent they should accept it.
  4. DSupporters of alternative medicine ought to reassess the effectiveness of alternative treatments in comparison to the treatments of mainstream medicine.✗ No comparison with mainstream treatments is drawn. The point is their inconsistency.
  5. EThe attempt to reduce any medical treatment to the purely physical fails to recognise the healing powers of the mind.✗ Repeats the advocates’ own view rather than what the passage shows about them.
Tip A rhetorical question that exposes a contradiction usually leads to “they should be consistent”.
23(Q45 in the original paper) Flaw

Which one of the following best expresses the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AThe tenants who were evicted may have been unsatisfactory in other respects.✗ They were evicted “simply for complaining”, and refusing repairs would still be wrong.
  2. BPeople who rent accommodation from private landlords are not necessarily vulnerable.✗ The conclusion only covers people who can’t afford to buy. It doesn’t touch the main jump.
  3. CHarsh penalties for abusive landlords would not necessarily be effective.✗ Penalties are never proposed.
  4. DSome people who could afford to buy their own home may prefer not to do so.✗ The conclusion is about people who can’t afford to buy, so this is irrelevant.
  5. ENot-for-profit housing associations may be no better than commercial companies or private landlords.✓ It assumes not-for-profit bodies would treat tenants better. They might be no better, so the remedy is not supported.

24(Q47 in the original paper) Principle

Which one of the following illustrates the principle used in the above argument?

  1. AWe should not praise a five-year-old child who picks up a golf club for the first time and consistently strikes the ball cleanly.✓ Striking cleanly on the first go is pure natural ability, so by the principle the child should not be praised for it.
  2. BThe music Mozart composed towards the end of his life should be thought of more highly than that which he wrote when very young.✗ Ranks his later works above his early ones. It doesn’t say a natural gift deserves no praise.
  3. CIf we know that a writer has spent a long time drafting and re-drafting their work, then their work is automatically deserving of praise.✗ The reverse direction (effort earns praise), and “automatically” goes beyond the argument.

Abdi succeeded on natural ability, with no training nobody should be admired just for a natural gift (like being tall) actors don’t deserve high regard.

BLater works rated above early ones. A ranking, not “no praise for a gift”. ✗
