1(Q1 in the original paper) Main conclusionAnswer D
This week a controversial chef is urging the great British public to sample a fabulous new meat. But instead of a buying bonanza the chef’s comments have provoked outrage because the meat in question is horse.Context Even the staunchest meat-eaters are up in arms. Says a rival chef: ‘I would never eat horse meat and would never serve it to my customers. It’s not part of our food culture. It’s unthinkable.’Opposing view But aren’t we being just the teeniest bit irrational?Main conclusion After all, in France and Belgium, two of our closest neighbours, there are whole shelves of horse meat alongside the beef and chicken. Here we eat lamb and pig, duck, even deer, ostrich and kangaroo.Evidence / example If we eat these, what is logically different about horse? Nothing.Intermediate conclusion

Which one of the following most closely expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AThere is no logical difference between lamb or beef and horse.✗ The last step (“what is logically different? Nothing”). It is a reason for calling us irrational.
  2. BEating horse meat in Britain is unthinkable.✗ This is the rival chef’s view, which the author argues against.
  3. CThe British should follow the example of France and Belgium.✗ France and Belgium are evidence. The author never says we should copy them.
  4. DThe British response to eating horse meat is a little irrational.✓ The question “aren’t we being just the teeniest bit irrational?” is the claim. The neighbours and the list of meats we already eat back it up.
  5. EMeat-eaters have no good reason for objecting to horse.✗ Close to the “Nothing” step, so it is a reason, and it is stronger than “a little irrational”.
Tip A question can be the conclusion. “But aren’t we…?” turns against the chef and means “we are”.

2(Q3 in the original paper) FlawAnswer D
The most popular theory about the origin of the Moon is that it was formed from debris breaking off the Earth in a planetary collision 4.5 billion years ago.Context If this were true, the Moon would be made of the same material as is found on Earth.Reason Examination of Moon rocks shows this to be the case, although there is little iron in Moon rocks.Evidence / example However, this can be explained because, according to the theory, the material that formed the Moon would have come from the Earth's crust, not its iron-rich core.Reason Thus we should accept the theory as true.Main conclusion

Which one of the following identifies the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AThe popularity of a theory is not what determines its truth.✗ Popularity is only mentioned. The conclusion rests on the rock evidence, not on how popular the theory is.
  2. BEvidence that is inconsistent with a theory proves that the theory is false.✗ The iron puzzle is explained by the theory itself, so there is no inconsistency to point to.
  3. CThe explanation offered in the passage is not consistent with the theory.✗ The explanation comes “according to the theory”, so it is consistent with it.
  4. DEvidence that confirms a theory's predictions does not prove that the theory is true.✓ The rocks match what the theory predicts, but a matching prediction only supports a theory. Another theory could predict the same rocks.
  5. EA theory that is not consistent with the facts should be rejected, not modified.✗ The theory is not modified: the crust point is part of the original theory.
Tip “If theory, then X. X is true. So the theory is true” is a classic jump. X fits, but it does not prove.

3(Q4 in the original paper) Draw a conclusionAnswer C
The International Code of Zoological Nomenclature states that the earliest recorded scientific name for an organism becomes the official name.Context This seems fair,Opposing view but the system is flawed.Main conclusion Textbooks show the sequence of fossil ancestors of the modern horse as: Pliohippus, Merychippus, Mesohippus and the first horse ancestor Hyracotherium. The last of these looks out of place. Hyracotherium was once, more sensibly, called Eohippus; this descriptive name means "dawn horse". The suffix -hippus (horse) is consistent with others in the list. Eohippus also sounds pleasant. The name was changed when it was discovered that a fossil fragment that had been named Hyracotherium was really from the same animal that had, until then, been called Eohippus.Evidence / example

Which one of the following conclusions is best supported by the passage above?

  1. ACommon sense is not a major factor in scientific naming decisions.✗ Too broad: one horse name says nothing about all scientific naming.
  2. BA system of naming organisms based on the date the name was first registered is not the most appropriate.✗ Too strong. One bad result does not show a better system exists, and the passage admits the rule “seems fair”.
  3. CThe rules of the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature do not always produce the most sensible name for animals.✓ One case where the rule swapped a sensible name (Eohippus) for a worse one shows the rules do not always give the most sensible name.
  4. DThe name of Hyracotherium should be changed back to Eohippus.✗ A recommendation the passage never makes.
  5. EThe rules of the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature need to be changed to allow for common sense.✗ Goes further than the passage: it points out a flaw but does not call for new rules.
Tip One example can only support a cautious “not always” conclusion, not “change the rules”.

4(Q5 in the original paper) AssumptionAnswer A
At Everthorpe Prison in Yorkshire, prisoners made no attempt to escape using ladders placed against the outside walls by drug dealers and prostitutes plying their trades.Evidence / example A prison officer spokesperson said that this was because the authorities had made prisons too comfortable and thus failed to deter criminals.Main conclusion A large number of released prisoners quickly reoffended and returned to prison, where they had all the comforts of home: a television in each cell, breakfast in bed, a wage and cash bonuses for good behaviour.Evidence / example

Which one of the following is an implicit assumption upon which the spokesperson's argument rests?

  1. APrisoners would not reoffend if prisons were less comfortable.✓ “Too comfortable, so they fail to deter” only works if less comfort would stop prisoners reoffending.
  2. BMany criminals are habitual offenders.✗ Habit is another explanation for reoffending, so it weakens the spokesperson’s point.
  3. CNot all prisoners are interested in drugs or prostitutes.✗ Interest in drugs or prostitutes has nothing to do with why prisoners stay.
  4. DThe conditions inside prisons are determined by the prison officers.✗ He blames “the authorities”, so he does not assume prison officers set the conditions.
  5. EReoffending rates have recently risen.✗ Not needed. The argument is about a large number reoffending, not a rising rate.
Tip Negation test: “prisoners would reoffend anyway, however uncomfortable”. Then comfort is not the cause and the argument falls.

5(Q9 in the original paper) StrengthenAnswer D
The recent campaign to reduce the number of people who drive while over the legal alcohol limit was very successful.Context But reducing it further is going to be difficult without addressing one of the main problems: people in rural areas have no choice, in the absence of public transport, but to use their cars.Reason People who live in towns and cities can use buses, trains or readily available taxis if they want to go out for a drink. Furthermore, they don't have to go far to find pubs and clubs.Comparison Unless the Government wants the level of drink-driving to increase, it needs to ensure that far more pubs and clubs are built in rural areas.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, would most strengthen the above argument?

  1. AThe legal alcohol limit for drivers is likely to be lowered in the near future.✗ A lower limit affects drivers everywhere. It gives no reason why rural pubs are the answer.
  2. BThe public have been made much more aware of the dangers of drink-driving.✗ Better awareness suggests drink-driving will keep falling anyway: this weakens.
  3. CMany pubs in rural areas are closing because of the reduced numbers of customers.✗ Pubs are closing for lack of customers, so building more looks pointless: this weakens.
  4. DThe population of rural areas is predicted to increase over the next twenty years.✓ More people in the countryside means more people who must drive to the pub. So drink-driving will increase unless pubs come closer.
  5. EThe police concentrate their drink-driving campaign in towns and cities.✗ About where the police work, not about the distance to pubs. If anything it points to a policing fix.
Tip The conclusion warns of an increase. Look for something that makes the rural problem grow.

6(Q10 in the original paper) FlawAnswer B
Another pair of British media personalities have announced their separation.Context Both are well-paid personalities who profit enormously from the publicity they attract from their behaviour. Media personalities like them usually employ agents who can generate publicity, arrange well-paid personal appearances and sell stories and photo shoots to glossy magazines.Evidence / example These agents probably generate more income for some media personalities than their original activity, such as singing.Reason Therefore it's clear that talent is no longer rewarded.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AThese personalities may not be talented.✗ If they lack talent, their pay was never a reward for talent. That does not show the reasoning is broken.
  2. BMany performers may be rewarded for their own original talent as well.✓ Earning more from publicity does not mean earning nothing from talent. Their singing can still be rewarded too.
  3. CAgents generate a large amount of income for their clients.✗ This just repeats the premise.
  4. DAgents may be destroying talent by their actions.✗ A new claim that would, if anything, back the conclusion. It is not a flaw.
  5. EPersonalities do not have to choose to employ agents.✗ Whether they choose agents has nothing to do with whether talent is rewarded.
Tip Watch the jump from “some” and “more income” to the sweeping “talent is no longer rewarded”.

7(Q11 in the original paper) Main conclusionAnswer A
Films are expensive to produce and must take a significant amount of money at the box office to cover costs before any profit is made.Reason Thus filmmakers are primarily interested in making a film that will appeal to a large number of people and so make a lot of profit.Intermediate conclusion This often leads to the production of superficial, violent films that attract a large audience.Reason Therefore, the Government should invest in the film industry.Main conclusion If it did that, filmmakers would not need to be motivated entirely by the aim for profit.Reason

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AThe Government should invest in the film industry.✓ “Therefore, the Government should invest” is the claim. Everything before it and the “If it did that” sentence after it support it.
  2. BFilmmakers need to ensure that their films will attract large audiences.✗ An intermediate step (“Thus filmmakers are…”), which supports the conclusion.
  3. CGovernment investment in the film industry would reduce the need to aim for profit.✗ The reason given straight after the conclusion.
  4. DThe output of the film industry contains too many violent films.✗ Implied, but never stated, and it is a reason for acting, not the action.
  5. EAiming solely for profit leads to the production of superficial, violent films.✗ A reason, and it overstates it: the passage says “often”, not always.
Tip “Therefore… should” usually flags the conclusion, even when a reason follows it.

8(Q15 in the original paper) Draw a conclusionAnswer A
Those in favour of coursework contributing to A-Level grades say that this is much fairer than assessment by exam only, as it means that students who are willing to work hard but who perform poorly in exams will have a better chance of doing well.Opposing view However, this arrangement currently allows far more opportunity for cheating, casting doubt on its fairness.Intermediate conclusion For example, there is a growing market for customised essays, available (for a fee) via the Internet. At the moment the only deterrent is a teacher's vigilance, but while teachers might identify work that seems atypical for individual students, they won't necessarily detect when, for example, a student has had an unacceptable amount of help from family or friends.Evidence / example

Which one of the following conclusions is best supported by the passage above?

  1. AAssessment by coursework is not necessarily fairer than assessment by examination.✓ Supporters say coursework is “much fairer”; cheating “casts doubt” on that. So it is not necessarily fairer. Cautious, like the passage.
  2. BThe opportunities for cheating in coursework mean that A-Levels should be assessed by exams only.✗ Too strong: the passage casts doubt, it does not call for exams only.
  3. CTraditional examinations are still the fairest way of grading A-Level students.✗ “Fairest” is never shown. The passage only questions coursework.
  4. DThere is no entirely fair way of assessing students at A-Level.✗ Too strong: nothing shows exams are unfair.
  5. EExaminations do not assess how hard a student has worked.✗ This comes from the supporters’ side, not from where the passage is heading.
Tip “Casting doubt” evidence leads to a “not necessarily” conclusion. Match the strength.

9(Q16 in the original paper) AssumptionAnswer C
Largely because of the influence of celebrity TV chefs and increased customer demand, many supermarkets have recently enlarged the amount of game (e.g. rabbits, pheasants and venison) in their meat sections.Context Factory farming is seen as cruel to the animals involved. Rabbit meat, in particular, is selling well as it is tastier than chicken and is cheaper than beef or pork. Game is also low in fat and cholesterol.Reason If people are really concerned about animal welfare they should take advantage of the increased availability of game in the supermarkets.Main conclusion They will also be getting a nutritious and cheap diet.Reason

Which of the following best expresses the underlying assumption in the above argument?

  1. ASupermarkets want to improve their animal welfare image.✗ The supermarkets’ image plays no part in the reasoning.
  2. BThe influence of celebrity chefs on shoppers cannot be underestimated.✗ The chefs are only background for why game is on sale.
  3. CNo game meat is produced using factory farming methods.✓ Buying game only helps animal welfare if the game is not factory farmed. The passage never says so; it just assumes it.
  4. DCheapness does not necessarily provide good value.✗ This would undermine the “cheap diet” point, so it is not assumed.
  5. ESupermarkets always respond to customer demands.✗ “Always” is too strong, and the argument does not need it.
Tip Link the reason (factory farming is cruel) to the advice (buy game). The missing bridge: game is not factory farmed.

10(Q17 in the original paper) StrengthenAnswer C
The production of standard disposable nappies from petrochemicals is wholly unsustainable.Intermediate conclusion It is estimated that, while in disposable nappies, a baby will generate two tonnes of used nappies which will end up in landfill.Evidence / example Washable nappies have a significantly reduced environmental impact at production and at eventual disposal. Landfill sites are already in short supply, with recycling programmes aiming to reduce waste.Reason However, if the Government wants to meet its sustainability targets it must do more to increase the use of washable nappies.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, would most strengthen the above argument?

  1. AThe washing process of nappies involves further energy consumption.✗ Washing uses energy, a cost of washables: this weakens.
  2. BSome disposable nappies are made of natural polymers and are compostable.✗ Some disposables can be composted, so they are not all a landfill problem: this weakens.
  3. CMost disposable nappies would take in excess of 500 years to bio-degrade in landfill conditions.✓ If disposables sit in scarce landfill for 500+ years, they really are “wholly unsustainable”, so the push for washables is needed.
  4. DSeveral local authorities have incentive programmes to encourage parents to choose washable over disposable nappies.✗ Something is already being done, so it does not show the Government must do more.
  5. EBy 2014 all local authorities must offer doorstep recycling and many already offer composting bins and appliance collection.✗ Recycling and collection services say nothing about nappies.
Tip Strengthen the weakest link: here, just how bad disposables are for landfill.

11(Q21 in the original paper) StrengthenAnswer E
In recent years there has been a great deal of media attention focused on the private lives of politicians and world leaders, with particular attention paid to their personal relationships.Context There are those who suggest that this attention is unwarranted because only their professional abilities are important to their political role.Opposing view This attitude fails to consider that the public have little insight into the day-to-day lives of world leaders and, indeed, few points of reference. Personal lives offer common ground. In democratic states leaders are elected based on how the public perceive them, and private lives can offer an insight into an individual's values.Reason The personal aspects of a politician's life should continue to be reported if the electorate find it important.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the above argument?

  1. AAll those in the public eye are subjected to media attention.✗ Says the attention happens, not that it is justified.
  2. BSome politicians have high profile affairs.✗ Shows there is something to report, but not why voters need to know it.
  3. CAttention on their private lives can cause leaders to lose their focus.✗ A cost of the attention: this weakens.
  4. DMany of the public are very interested in the family lives of sports personalities.✗ Sports personalities are not elected, so this is irrelevant.
  5. EMost politicians claim to be respectable and honest in all aspects of their lives.✓ If politicians themselves claim to be honest in all parts of life, their private lives become a fair test of the values voters elect them on.
Tip The key reason is “private lives offer an insight into values”. Find the option that makes those values relevant.

12(Q22 in the original paper) Parallel reasoningAnswer B
Criminals often give themselves away by their lifestyle.Reason My neighbour is a criminalMain conclusion because he bought and is living in a very expensive house,Evidence / example which I know he could not afford as he is officially working only in a poorly paid job.Reason

Which one of the following most clearly parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AThe cat keeps bringing mice into the house. He never eats them, so I assume he hunts them either because that is his instinct or for pleasure.✗ Rules out one motive (food) and lists the rest. No result that breaks a limit.
  2. BIt is impossible to write more than four pages in half an hour. You have just handed in seven pages of writing. You must, therefore, have spent longer than half an hour on it.✓ Same shape: the result is more than the stated limit allows, so something beyond the official story must be going on.
  3. CThe bank was extremely well guarded and robbing it would require very careful planning. The thieves who broke in yesterday must have spent months planning every detail of the crime.✗ “B needs A; B happened; so A”. No official story is contradicted (and “months” is added).
  4. DAll my friends save money through the year and then go on holiday. I am saving every month, so I think I should be able to go on holiday soon.✗ Copies what friends do to predict a holiday. A comparison, not a contradiction.
  5. EIf the police want to identify criminals they need to watch how they are spending their money. They do want to catch them, so they watch out for people buying cars or houses they cannot legally afford.✗ Same topic (criminals, houses they cannot afford), but it is “if they want X they must do Y”. Different shape.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: official limit he can only afford what a poorly paid job allows. Result he has a very expensive house, beyond that limit. So he must have something hidden (crime).

BLimit: at most four pages in half an hour. Result: seven pages. So you must have had something extra (more time). ✓ Same shape.
ANot for food, so instinct or pleasure. Eliminating a motive, no limit broken. ✗
CRobbery needs planning; it happened; so they planned. Nothing they were known to have is exceeded. ✗
DFriends save then holiday; I save; so I will holiday. Copying a pattern. ✗
EIf they want X, they must do Y; they want X; so they do Y. Topic match only. ✗
Tip Ignore the crime topic (it is the trap in E). Look for “the result is more than the known means allow, so there must be a hidden extra”.

13(Q23 in the original paper) PrincipleAnswer C
There is often concern about the large quantities of money earned by top singers.Context It could be argued that there are many ways in which this money could be spent that would be beneficial to a large number of people.Opposing view However, these people are receiving high earnings because they are the best at what they doMain conclusion and those who are better than others at what they do should be rewarded.Reason

Which one of the following best illustrates the principle underlying the argument above?

  1. ATo become a doctor requires a much longer course of study than other professions; therefore, doctors should receive a higher rate of pay than members of those other professions.✗ Rewards a longer training, not being better than others.
  2. BWhen making the decision about who should be accepted onto the course, the bottom third of the applicants based on the results were eliminated immediately.✗ Removes the worst. It is a selection, not a reward for the best.
  3. CSince efficiency is the most important aspect of the work, the end of year bonus was awarded to the member of staff who had made the best use of time over the year.✓ Principle: whoever is best at what they do should be rewarded. The bonus goes to the person who was best at the thing that matters most (efficiency).
  4. DWhen deciding on the member of staff to be promoted, the director of the company looked at the records of the applicants and chose the one who had done the most overtime in the past year.✗ Rewards the most hours worked, which is not the same as doing the job best.
  5. EThe prize for the best film was decided by taking the one that made the most money at the box office in its opening week.✗ Uses money to decide what is “best”: the passage goes the other way (being best earns the money).

Working: write the principle first

Singers earn a lot because they are the best at what they do and the best should be rewarded.
So the principle: reward people for being better than others at what they do.

CEfficiency is what counts → bonus to the most efficient person. Best performer rewarded. ✓
APay for a longer course. Rewards the training, not being better. ✗
BCut the bottom third. Punishes the worst; no reward. ✗
DMost overtime. Rewards quantity of hours, not quality. ✗
EMost money = best film. Earnings decide “best”, the wrong way round. ✗
Tip Check what is actually being rewarded in each option: skill, effort, hours, money or luck.

14(Q27 in the original paper) Main conclusionAnswer D
There is a naive view that the problem of rapid population growth can be effectively solved if enough money is invested in family planning services.Opposing view But it is no accident that the world's highest population growth rates are in poverty-stricken sub-Saharan Africa.Intermediate conclusion Child mortality in this region remains stubbornly high, so it makes sense for women to have lots of children to ensure that at least one son survives into their old age. Children are also economic necessities for peasant families engaged in traditional agriculture; in many systems the larger the family, the more land is allocated to it.Reason It follows that only by reducing the demand for large families through economic development can the problem of overpopulation be solved.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AThe need for economic development is greatest in sub-Saharan Africa.✗ Africa is the example. The passage never says the need is greatest there.
  2. BThe demand for large families is mainly caused by high child mortality.✗ One of two reasons, and “mainly” is never claimed.
  3. CThe main consequence of a lack of economic development is overpopulation.✗ Never said. The passage says poverty drives growth, not that overpopulation is its main result.
  4. DOverpopulation can be properly tackled only by economic development.✓ “It follows that only by… economic development” is the conclusion, and D rewords it.
  5. ETo believe that family planning services can reduce population growth is to be naive.✗ The passage only calls the money-alone view naive. E overstates it and is the rejected view, not the conclusion.
Tip “It follows that” at the end signals the conclusion. The opening “naive view” is the view being rejected.

15(Q28 in the original paper) Parallel reasoningAnswer E
It is clear that telepathy is not possibleMain conclusion because, if it were, people would be able to transfer their thoughts to each other for important purposes – for example, passing an examination.Reason But this does not happen. The only supposed demonstrations of telepathy are about trivial matters.Evidence / example

Which one of the following arguments has the same logical structure as the above argument?

  1. AIt is obvious that humans cannot travel through time. No one has ever done this, except in novels and films, and we know that these are fiction.✗ No “if… then” at all: it just says it has never been done.
  2. BIf humans were descended from apes, they would have some of the same patterns of behaviour. There are some patterns of behaviour common to humans and apes. So humans are descended from apes.✗ “If A then B; B; so A” runs the rule backwards.
  3. CIf hypnotism could cure illnesses, we would not have to rely on drugs. But hypnotism cannot cure illness. So we do have to rely on drugs.✗ “If A then B; not A; so not B” denies the start, not the result.
  4. DIt is evident that human life is the most intelligent form of life on Earth, because humans can exploit other life forms for their own purposes, and that is exactly what the most intelligent life form would have to be able to do.✗ “A must be able to do B; humans do B; so humans are A”. Backwards again.
  5. EThere is no evidence that intelligent life exists beyond our planet. If there were intelligent life elsewhere in the universe, we would by now have had clear evidence of its existence. So there is no intelligent life other than that on Earth.✓ Same structure: if A, then we would see B; we do not see B; so not A.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: if A (telepathy possible) then B (used for important things like exams). Not B (it does not happen) so not A.

EIf A (intelligent life elsewhere) → B (clear evidence by now). Not B (no evidence) → so not A. ✓ Same shape (the order of the sentences is shuffled).
ANever done → impossible. No conditional rule. ✗
BIf A → B. B → so A. Affirms the result. ✗
CIf A → B. Not A → so not B. Denies the start. ✗
DThe most intelligent must be able to exploit; humans exploit → so humans are the most intelligent. Backwards. ✗
Tip Mark each sentence as “if A then B”, “A”, “not A”, “B” or “not B”, whatever order they come in.

16(Q29 in the original paper) AssumptionAnswer A
For the second year running the Silver Star Prize for art was awarded to a video artist, raising again the big question: What is great art?Context Many have condemned this year's choice on the grounds that a documentary-style video film cannot be considered as creative art at all in the way that, say, painting and sculpture can.Opposing view The Silver Star jury, however, praised the 'emotional force of the work and its complexity beneath an apparently simple surface'.Evidence / example If they are right in this evaluation then clearly video is as much a medium for great art as any other form of expression.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the argument above?

  1. AAny work with emotional force and complexity is capable of being great art.✓ “Emotional force and complexity, so video can be great art” only works if emotional force and complexity are enough to make great art possible.
  2. BThe decision of the jury to award the prize for a video was the right one.✗ The argument says “if they are right”, so it does not assume the jury was right.
  3. CNo-one can really answer the question: What is great art?✗ The argument gives an answer (emotional force and complexity), so it does not assume there is none.
  4. DThis year's winning exhibit was deceptively simple.✗ Again covered by “if they are right”. It is the jury’s view, not assumed.
  5. EPainting and sculpture are the highest forms of creative art.✗ This sides with the critics and would weaken the argument.
Tip When a conclusion starts “If they are right…”, the jury’s view is not assumed. Look for the link from their words to “great art”.

17(Q33 in the original paper) WeakenAnswer C
'Organic food is no healthier than other produce, scientists claim.' This was the headline in an article published in 2009 in The Independent. It reported that a comprehensive review of 50 years of evidence showed no appreciable difference in nutrient levels between organic and conventional farm produce.Evidence / example If the review is correct shoppers will think twice about buying organic food, especially if the price difference remains significant.Intermediate conclusion Only if people really do receive some health benefit in return for the extra money will the current upward trend in organic sales continue.Main conclusion

Which one of the following presents the strongest challenge to the above argument?

  1. AShoppers who read The Independent are more likely to buy organic produce than readers of other newspapers.✗ If organic buyers read the headline, they are more likely to think twice: this fits the argument.
  2. BMost shoppers take more notice of price than they do of nutritional benefits when choosing to buy.✗ Price-conscious shoppers would think twice about dearer organic food: this supports it.
  3. CIf the review is correct, shoppers will have received no health benefit in the past 50 years, yet organic sales have continued to rise.✓ Sales have risen for 50 years with no health benefit. So rising sales do not depend on a health benefit, and the “only if” claim fails.
  4. DIf organic food had more nutritional value than conventional farm produce, scientists would have found out long before now.✗ Backs up the review’s finding: this supports it.
  5. EIf there is no appreciable difference in nutrient levels there is no advantage for shoppers in switching back to conventional produce.✗ Ignores price (conventional food is cheaper) and does not touch the “only if” claim.
Tip An “only if” claim is beaten by one case where the result happened without the condition.

18(Q34 in the original paper) FlawAnswer B
There is widespread dissatisfaction and disillusionment in Britain with politics and with politicians, who are seen as untrustworthy. Many of the electorate do not even bother to vote in a general election. Which one of the following identifies the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt assumes that most Swiss citizens vote in general elections.✗ Swiss general election turnout is never part of the reasoning.
  2. BIt assumes that there is only one reason why some Swiss citizens are happier.✓ More referendums and more happiness go together, but other things could make those Swiss areas happier. It treats the link as the cause.
  3. CIt assumes that British citizens would trust politicians if there were more referendums.✗ The conclusion is about being happier, not about trusting politicians.
  4. DIt assumes that there is only one reason why the British are disillusioned with politics.✗ Tempting, but the argument never names a cause of British disillusion. The broken step is reading cause into the Swiss survey.
  5. EIt assumes that better policies emerge when important decisions are made by referendum.✗ Better policies are never mentioned; the claim is only about happiness.
Tip Two things found together (referendums, happiness) does not mean one causes the other.

24(Q46 in the original paper) Parallel reasoningAnswer D
Our football team always loses the match if it's raining.Reason They won last Saturday,Evidence / example so it can't have been raining.Main conclusion

Which of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AIf you work part-time, you earn less than people who work full-time. I work full-time; therefore, I earn more than people who work part-time.✗ “If part-time, earn less; I am not part-time; so I earn more” denies the start, not the result.
  2. BMy aunt is always happy if she's bought a new hat. She was happy today, so she must have bought a new hat.✗ “If hat, happy; happy; so hat” runs the rule backwards.
  3. CJohn always stays at home if there is snooker on TV. There is snooker on TV tonight, so John will stay at home.✗ “If snooker, home; snooker; so home” is valid, but it starts from A, not from not-B.
  4. DDavid always enjoys films if they have a big star in them. David didn't enjoy that film, so it can't have had a big star in it.✓ Same shape: if A, always B; not B; so not A.
  5. EMy cat has gone missing because I forgot to feed her. My cat always goes missing if I forget to feed her.✗ Conclusion first, then “if forget, missing”. Still runs from the result back to the cause.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: if A (rain) always B (lose). Not B (they won) so not A (no rain).

DIf A (big star) → always B (enjoys). Not B (didn’t enjoy) → so not A (no big star). ✓ Same shape.
AIf A (part-time) → B (earn less). Not A (full-time) → so not B (earn more). Denies the start. ✗
BIf A (hat) → B (happy). B → so A. Affirms the result. ✗
CIf A (snooker) → B (home). A → so B. Valid, but a different shape. ✗
EIf A (forget) → B (missing). B → so A. Affirms the result. ✗
Tip The original is valid: if A always gives B, then no B means no A. Look for the option that also starts from “not B”.

Tipping because it is expected wrong. A tip is a reward for good service so reward those who perform well, not everyone by default.
