1(TSA 2017, Q48) Logic with unknownsAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Algebra & unknowns question in the past papers. Four equations in five unknowns tempts you to say nothing can be found, but subtracting one word from another isolates some letters; the skill is deciding exactly which letters stay locked together.

Each word in a word game is scored by adding up the values of its letters1. Each letter has the same value whenever it appears but different letters have different values. I know the word values for TEAR, RITE, TREE and RAT2, but none of the letter values.

How many of the letter values3 can I now calculate?

  1. A0
  2. B1
  3. C2
  4. D3
  5. E4

Reading it properly

  • 1Word value = sum of its letters. E appears twice in TREE, so it counts twice there.
  • 2Four words, five different letters: T, E, A, R, I.
  • 3Count the letters you can pin down exactly.

Working it out

  1. Write each word as a sum:

    TEAR = T + E + A + R
    RITE = R + I + T + E
    TREE = T + R + E + E
    RAT = R + A + T

  2. Subtract words that share letters:

    TEAR − RAT = E ✓
    TREE − 2E = T + R (only the pair)
    RAT − (T + R) = A ✓
    RITE − (T + R) − E = I ✓

  3. T and R appear together in every word, so you only ever learn T + R, never T or R alone.

    E, A and I 3 letter values
D3 letters (E, A and I). T and R only ever appear as a pair.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 0 is too few: TEAR − RAT gives E straight away.
  • B✗ 1 stops after finding E. Once you know T + R, A and I follow too.
  • C✗ 2 misses one of A or I. Both come out once you know T + R.
  • E✗ 4 would need T or R on its own, but every word contains both T and R, so they can’t be separated.
Tip TEAR − RAT = E. TREE gives T + R, then RAT gives A and RITE gives I. T and R never split: 3.

2(TSA 2023, Q38) Maps & colouringAnswer E

Why it's herethe hardest Maps, routes & networks question in the past papers. The busiest-looking maps are not necessarily the ones that need most colours; you have to look for a group of countries that all touch one another (or a country ringed by an odd number of neighbours), and checking five maps takes time.

The following pictures are blank maps. The continuous lines indicate country borders, the dotted lines show the borders of the maps. These maps need to be painted so that each country is filled with one colour and that no two neighbouring countries (countries with a common border) have the same colour1. The fewest number of colours should be used2 on a given map.

Which one of the following blank maps will require 4 different colours3 to be painted?

  1. Amap A
  2. Bmap B
  3. Cmap C
  4. Dmap D
  5. Emap E

Reading it properly

  • 1Countries that share a stretch of border must be different colours. Edges of the dotted frame don’t count.
  • 2So for each map, find the smallest number of colours that works.
  • 3“Requires 4” = three colours are not enough. Look for four countries that all touch each other.

Working it out

  1. Plan: try to paint each map with just 3 colours. Start with the biggest country, then go round its neighbours. If you get stuck, that map needs a 4th colour.

    Tip Trick for the neighbours of one country: if they form a ring and each touches the next, an even number of them can swap between 2 colours. An odd ring (like 3) forces a 4th colour.

  2. Here are the maps painted (the numbers are the colours):

  3. Why A–D manage with 3:

    A: the big country (3) has neighbours in a chain 2, 1, 2, 1: they just alternate
    B: the big middle country (1) has 6 neighbours in a chain, not a closed ring: alternate 2 and 3
    C: the round middle country (1) is ringed by 4 countries: an even ring, so 2, 3, 2, 3
    D: the enclosed middle country (1) is ringed by 4 countries: even again

    But in E the small enclosed country (4) is ringed by just 3 countries (the big right one, the long left one and the bottom-left corner), and those 3 all touch each other:

    4 countries all touching each other 4 colours E
EMap E: four of its countries all border each other, so 3 colours can’t work.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A can be done with 3 colours: the big country’s neighbours alternate in a chain.
  • B✗ B looks busy, but 3 colours work: the big middle country’s neighbours form a chain that alternates 2, 3, 2, 3…
  • C✗ C’s round middle country has 4 neighbours in a ring (even), so 3 colours are enough.
  • D✗ D’s enclosed middle country has 4 neighbours in a ring (even), so 3 colours are enough.
Tip Look for a small enclosed country with an odd number (3) of neighbours that all touch: only E has one.

3(TSA 2017, Q44) Nets & foldingAnswer A

Why it's herethe third-hardest Nets & folding question in the past papers. Every net has the right number of squares and two L-shaped ends, so counting does not help; you must check that each small square is hinged to an edge it really touches on the box. The one misplaced square is easy to overlook on an unfamiliar shape.

The drawing below shows an ‘L’ shaped box.

Which one of the nets below cannot1 be folded up to form the ‘L’ shaped box? (A dotted line indicates a fold and a solid line indicates a cut2.)

  1. Anet A
  2. Bnet B
  3. Cnet C
  4. Dnet D
  5. Enet E

Reading it properly

  • 1Four nets work. Hunt for the one face that ends up in the wrong place.
  • 2A solid line inside a net means those squares are not joined. Dotted lines show which edges hold faces together.

Working it out

  1. First list the faces of the box. There are two L-shaped ends (3 squares each) and a band of six faces going round the edge of the L:

    Every net has 14 squares, so counting won’t help. Check which edge each face is joined to.

  2. Name the faces in net A. The two L shapes are the ends. The 1 × 2 strip between them is the tall side, the strip below the right L is the bottom, the square on the right L’s inner corner is the step, with the top of the tall part above it. On the left L, the square sitting on the foot is the top of the foot. Then there is one square left over:

    one face lands on the back L, and the end of the foot is missing A
    Tip Fast check for each net: find the two L’s, then make sure each small square is attached to an edge it really touches on the box. The square for the end of the foot must be attached to the end of a foot (or to the bottom strip or the top of the foot, at the right edge).
ANet A: its top-left square folds onto the back L, so the end of the foot is never covered.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ B: the two L’s are joined by the tall side; the top of the foot, step, top of the tall part, end of the foot and bottom are all on the right edges.
  • C✗ C: the two 1 × 2 strips meet end to end at the corner (tall side and bottom), and each small square sits on a correct edge.
  • D✗ D: the row of four squares wraps round the L in order: end of foot, top of foot, step, top of the tall part. It folds up.
  • E✗ E: every face is joined along an edge it really touches, so it folds into the box.
Tip Name the faces of net A: two L’s, tall side, bottom, step, top, foot top… and the last square is stuck to the far edge of the foot top, where the back L goes. A can’t fold.

4(TSA 2015, Q50) Spatial reasoningAnswer E

Why it's herethe second-hardest 3D views & cubes question in the past papers. It feels as if only a straight-on view and two side views are possible, but the order of two trees swaps whenever you cross a line through them (including the diagonals of the square), so you must find every such line that crosses field Y.

Four trees stand at the corners of square field X1. They are photographed from field Y2, from every possible direction, close up and from a distance3, but never so that one tree stands in front of another4.

In how many different orders, left to right5, can the trees appear in the photographs?

  1. A1
  2. B2
  3. C3
  4. D4
  5. E5

Reading it properly

  • 1Trees 1 and 2 are nearest field Y; 4 and 3 are behind them.
  • 2The photographer is always somewhere in field Y, below the square.
  • 3Moving sideways and moving nearer or further can both change the order.
  • 4The order only changes at the exact moment two trees line up. Those lines are the borders between different orders.
  • 5Count the regions of field Y with different orders.

Working it out

  1. Key idea: as you walk around, two trees swap places in the photo only when you cross the straight line through them. So draw every line through two trees and see which ones run into field Y.

    • 1–2 (front row) and 4–3 (back row): run sideways, never enter Y.
    • 1–4 and 2–3 (the sides): run straight down into Y.
    • 3–1 and 4–2 (the diagonals): carry on down into Y, out to the left and right.

  2. Those four lines cut field Y into 5 regions. Each region gives its own order:

    straight in front: 1 4 3 2
    left, close: 4 3 1 2 · left, further back: 4 1 3 2
    right, close: 1 2 4 3 · right, further back: 1 4 2 3
    number of orders = 5
    Check Check the middle: stand in front, between the two side lines. Tree 1 is on your far left, 2 on your far right, and the back trees 4 and 3 appear in between.
E5 orders: one straight in front, two on the left and two on the right.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1 assumes you always see 1 4 3 2, as from straight in front. Walking to the side changes it.
  • B✗ 2 would be just “left side” and “right side”, missing the view from the front and the diagonal swaps.
  • C✗ 3 only uses the side lines (left, middle, right) and forgets the diagonals, which matter “from a distance”.
  • D✗ 4 misses one region, usually the middle view or one of the diagonal swaps.
Tip Order changes only when you cross a line through two trees. Side lines (2) and diagonals (2) enter field Y, cutting it into 5 regions: 5 orders.

5(TSA 2014, Q50) Charts & proportionAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest Charts & graphs question in the past papers. The bar shows money but the pies show people, so each section must be divided by a different ticket price; two of the pies are then very alike and only a careful estimate of the adults' share separates them. You have met this one before: it was in the 2014 paper you sat.

The cost of entry to the Victoria and David Museum is as follows:

Adults £71
Children £41
Senior Citizens £51

This chart shows the breakdown of yesterday's total takings of £6792 by age group.

Which one of the following pie charts shows the number of visitors3 in each age group that paid to enter the museum yesterday?

  1. Apie A
  2. Bpie B
  3. Cpie C
  4. Dpie D
  5. Epie E

Reading it properly

  • 1Divide money by these prices to get people.
  • 2The bar shows money, not people.
  • 3They want people, so convert first.

Working it out

  1. Read the bar roughly (it doesn’t need to be exact), then turn money into people by dividing by the ticket price:

    total ≈ 56 + 34 + 30 = 120 people
    adults ≈ 56 120 ≈ 47% (just under half)
    children ≈ 28%, seniors ≈ 25% (about a quarter each)

  2. Find the pie with adults just under half and children and seniors about a quarter each: pie B.

    Trap! Pie A is the money split (adults ≈ 57% of the takings). That is the trap for anyone who forgets to divide by the prices.
BPie B: adults just under half, children and seniors about a quarter each.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Pie A matches the money (adults ≈ 58%), not the number of people.
  • C✗ Adults are only about 38% and seniors far too big.
  • D✗ Adults are exactly half, which would need 56 adults = 64 others. Not true.
  • E✗ Adults are about 69%, far too many.
Tip People = £ price: about 56, 34, 30. Adults just under half, the other two about equal: B. A is the money trap.

6(TSA 2016, Q50) Measures & roundingAnswer C

Why it's herethe hardest Area, perimeter & volume question in the past papers. Dividing wall area by roll area ignores the pattern: each drop must start at the same point in the 60 cm repeat, so you need to work out how much roll each drop really uses and how many whole drops one roll gives.

Rolls of a particular wallpaper have a repeating pattern every 60 cm1 of their length. Each roll is 10 m long and 50 cm wide2. A wall 2.5 m high and 6.2 m wide3 is to be papered from ceiling to floor with single lengths of paper hung vertically (drops).

How many rolls of wallpaper will be needed so that the pattern on each drop matches the next4? (No joins can be made in one vertical drop.5)

  1. A3
  2. B4
  3. C5
  4. D6
  5. E7

Reading it properly

  • 1The pattern repeats every 60 cm down the roll.
  • 2Each roll: 10 m of paper, 0.5 m wide.
  • 3Each drop is 2.5 m long, and 6.2 m of wall must be covered.
  • 4Every drop must start at the same point in the pattern, so drops can only start every 60 cm along the roll.
  • 5Each drop is one piece. Offcuts too short for a drop are wasted.

Working it out

  1. How many drops?

    6.2 0.5 = 12.4 12 is not enough, so 13 drops

  2. How much roll does each drop use? It is 2.5 m long, but the next drop must start at the same point in the pattern, so you skip to the next whole number of 60 cm steps:

    60, 120, 180, 240, 300 cm
    2.5 m drop uses 3 m of roll (0.5 m wasted)

  3. Cut up one roll:

    drops start at 0, 3 and 6 m; a 4th would need 9 m to 11.5 m, but the roll is only 10 m
    13 3 = 4⅓ round up 5 rolls
C5 rolls: 13 drops, and each roll gives only 3 pattern-matched drops.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 3 rolls only covers 12 drops if you get 4 drops a roll and round 12.4 down. Both are wrong: 13 drops, 3 per roll.
  • B✗ 4 rolls ignores the pattern: 4 plain drops of 2.5 m fit on a roll (13 4 → 4). The same comes from wall area 15.5 m² 5 m² = 3.1 → 4. Both forget the waste.
  • D✗ 6 rolls would mean only about 2 drops per roll (e.g. allowing 3.6 m per drop) for 12 drops. But 3 m per drop is enough, so 3 drops fit.
  • E✗ 7 rolls is 13 drops at 2 per roll, from leaving an extra 60 cm repeat on every drop (3.6 m each). 3 m is already a whole number of repeats.
Tip 13 drops. Each uses 3 m (the first multiple of 60 cm that is at least 2.5 m), so 3 drops per 10 m roll. 13 3 → 5 rolls.

8(TSA 2010, Q49) Counting possibilitiesAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Counting & combinations question in the past papers. It is very easy to undercount: think carefully about every position the four digits could take in a number beginning 58, and about every way a loose scrap of paper could have been picked up.

All the telephone numbers in my village have six digits and begin with 581. I have accidentally torn up2 and thrown away a piece of paper on which I had written the telephone number of a friend who lives nearby. The only piece I can find is the scrap shown below:

How many different phone numbers in my village could be consistent3 with this extract?

  1. A2
  2. B10
  3. C11
  4. D12
  5. E22

Reading it properly

  • 1Every number looks like 5 8 _ _ _ _.
  • 2A torn scrap: it could come from anywhere in the number, and you can’t tell which way up it is!
  • 3Count every number the scrap could have come from.

Working it out

  1. The scrap shows 4 digits in a row. Where can “8011” sit inside 5 8 _ _ _ _?

    • It can’t start on the 5.
    • Start on the 8 of 58: 5 8 0 1 1 ? last digit anything: 10 numbers.
    • Start at the 3rd digit: 5 8 8 0 1 1 1 number.

  2. Now turn the scrap upside down. 8, 0 and 1 all look the same turned round, so it reads 1108. It can’t start on the 8 of 58 (that would need a 1 there), so only: 5 8 1 1 0 8 1 number.

    10 + 1 + 1 = 12
D12: 580110 to 580119, 588011, and 581108 (scrap upside down).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 2 only puts the scrap at the very end (588011 and 581108), missing that it can start on the 8 of 58.
  • B✗ 10 only counts 580110 to 580119.
  • C✗ 11 forgets the scrap might be upside down.
  • E✗ 22 thinks upside down gives another 11, but “1108” can’t start on the 8 of 58, so it adds only 1.
Tip 58011? gives 10, 588011 gives 1, and upside down 581108 gives 1. Total 12.

9(TSA 2010, Q50) Dice & 3D viewsAnswer A

Why it's herethe third-hardest 3D views & cubes question in the past papers. Noting which faces are seen together does not settle it, and the 'opposites add to 7' rule is a decoy; you have to use the way the pips on the asymmetric faces are turned between views to deduce extra neighbours.

On a normal die, opposing faces add up to 7. Four views of the same die on which the numbers are arranged differently from a normal die1 are shown below. The unseen face has the number two on it.2

How many spots are on the face opposite the two3 on this die?

  1. A1
  2. B3
  3. C4
  4. D5
  5. E6

Reading it properly

  • 1So “opposites add to 7” is useless here. Don’t answer 5!
  • 2The 2 never appears: we must find its opposite by elimination.
  • 3Opposite faces never touch. Find a face that touches four others: the one left over is its opposite.

Working it out

  1. Faces seen together touch each other: 1 & 3, 6 & 5, 4 & 3, 6 & 1. Now look at how the pips are turned:

    • Views 1 and 3: the same 3 is in front, but its diagonal has flipped direction. The die was turned a quarter turn about the front face, so the top (1) moved to a side and a side face (4) came to the top. So 1 touches 4.
    • Views 2 and 4: the same 6 is on top, but its rows have turned from across to front-to-back. The die was spun a quarter turn, so the new front (1) was beside the old front (5). So 1 touches 5.

  2. So the 1 touches 3, 4, 5 and 6. A face touches 4 others and is opposite exactly 1:

    opposite the 2 = 1
A1: the 1 touches 3, 4, 5 and 6, so it must be opposite the 2.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ The 3 and the 1 are seen together in view 1, so they touch.
  • C✗ The quarter turn of the 3 shows the 4 was beside the 1, so they touch.
  • D✗ 5 is the normal-die answer (2 + 5 = 7), but this die isn’t normal. The spin of the 6 shows 5 is beside the 1.
  • E✗ The 6 and the 1 are seen together in view 4, so they touch.
Tip 1 touches 3 and 6 (seen together), 4 and 5 (the quarter turns). The only face left for it to face is the 2.

10(TSA 2019, Q50) Nets & diceAnswer D

Why it's herethe second-hardest Nets & folding question in the past papers. Checking which faces are neighbours is not enough: each numeral has a direction, and the die that looks most natural has one numeral turned the wrong way. You have to track how every numeral rotates as the net folds.

The net below can be folded to make a Roman numerical dice1.

Which one of the following dice could have been made2 as a result of folding the net?

  1. Adice A
  2. Bdice B
  3. Cdice C
  4. Ddice D
  5. Edice E

Reading it properly

  • 1The numerals are drawings: the way their lines point matters, not just which number it is.
  • 2Four of the dice are impossible. Check which faces are next to each other and which way they point.

Working it out

  1. Fold the net with VI facing you: III folds up to the top, II to the right, I round to the back, IV under I to the bottom, and V to the left. So the opposite pairs are VI–I, II–V, III–IV.

  2. Every die shows I on its right face. Look at the ring of faces round I, laid out flat as in the net (seen from outside):

    With I upright on the right, the face above I is on top and the face to I’s left is on the left: top III, left II. Turn the die over (I looks the same) and you get top IV, left V.

  3. A: top III, left V ✗ (III on top needs II on the left)
    B: left II, but the top is not III ✗
    E: V on top ✗ (then I’s line would lie flat)
    C: top III, left II: right faces, check the directions…
    D: top IV, left V: right faces, check the directions…

  4. C: III’s lines must point straight at the I face, but in C they run alongside I’s edge ✗
    D: IV’s top points at I, and V opens towards I ✓

    could have been made D
DDice D: IV on top pointing at I, V on the left opening towards I, I on the right.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ With III on top and I on the right, the left face must be II. V is on the far side. (Its V also opens away from I.)
  • B✗ II on the left with I on the right means III must be on top, but B has a different numeral there.
  • C✗ The right three faces, but the III is turned: its lines should run towards the I face, not parallel to the edge they share.
  • E✗ V can’t be on top when I is upright on the right: the faces above and below I are III and IV.
Tip I upright on the right forces top III/left II or top IV/left V. That leaves C and D; III’s lines must point at I, so C fails. D.

Tracking six lines through that flip for five charts, at the very end of the paper, is demanding.

Keith likes to draw ‘bump charts’ to look at relationships. For instance, if 6 individuals were ordered by height, and it happened that the order was the same by weight, the bump chart would look like this:

Or if height was maintained for order on the left hand but lightness rather than heaviness on the right hand1, the bump chart would be this:

If one axis is reversed in order2 (as in heaviness goes to lightness), which one of the following bump charts would not be symmetrical about a vertical line midway between the axes3?

  1. Achart A
  2. Bchart B
  3. Cchart C
  4. Dchart D
  5. Echart E

Reading it properly

  • 1Reversing an axis flips its order upside down: position 1 becomes 6, 2 becomes 5, 3 becomes 4.
  • 2Do the flip first, then test the symmetry. (Flipping either axis gives the same answer.)
  • 3Mirror symmetry: every line from left a to right b needs a twin from left b to right a (a flat line a to a is its own twin).

22(TSA 2012, Q44) 3D shapes & mirror imagesAnswer C

Why it's herethe hardest 3D views & cubes question in the past papers. Five 11-cube solids drawn from different viewpoints, and the odd one out differs only by being a mirror image: you have to reduce each to its skeleton and pair them up, which is very hard to hold in your head against the clock.

Five pictures of solids made by sticking cubes together1 are shown below.

Which one of the following (A-E) is a different shape from all the others2?

  1. Asolid A
  2. Bsolid B
  3. Csolid C
  4. Dsolid D
  5. Esolid E

Reading it properly

  • 1Each solid can be turned any way, so describe it by its structure, not by how it is drawn.
  • 2“All” is the key word: the shapes come in matching pairs, and one is left without a partner.

Working it out

  1. Count the cubes: every solid has 11, so that doesn’t help. Look for the skeleton instead. Each solid is:

    • a corner cube with two straight arms at right angles, each 4 cubes long (counting the corner);
    • plus two 2-cube stubs, one on the 3rd cube of each arm (counting from the corner).

    So the only difference is which way the two stubs point.

  2. Find the stubs in each picture:

    • A: the arms lie flat on the table. One stub lies flat too (pointing away from the other arm); the other is the tower going up.
    • D: the arms are the tall column and the arm coming forward. One stub hangs down (in the same flat plane, pointing away from the column); the other sticks out sideways to the left.
    • C: the arms are the column and the bottom row. One stub hangs down (flat, pointing away from the column); the other goes backwards from near the top of the column.
    • B: arms = column and bottom row. One stub comes forwards along the floor, the other goes backwards from the column: opposite ways, both out of the flat L.
    • E: arms flat on the table. One stub goes up, the other hangs down: again opposite ways, both out of the L.
    B and E have both stubs sticking out in opposite directions B = E

  3. A, C and D all have one flat stub and one sticking-out stub. Now check which way it sticks out. Turn each one so its flat part looks the same (corner top-left, arms going right and down, flat stub pointing left):

    In A and D the sticking-out stub comes towards you; in C it goes away. So A = D, and C is their mirror image.

    pairs: A–D and B–E the one with no partner is C
  4. Tip In the exam you don’t need to prove the mirror image. Once you spot that A matches D and B matches E, C must be the answer.
CC: A and D are the same solid, B and E are the same solid, and C (a mirror image of A and D) matches none of them.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is the same solid as D (turn D so its column lies flat on the table).
  • B✗ B is the same solid as E: both have their two stubs pointing in opposite directions.
  • D✗ D is the same solid as A: same flat part, and the extra stub sticks out on the same side.
  • E✗ E is the same solid as B.
Tip Same skeleton every time: two 4-cube arms with a 2-cube stub on each. Pair them by stub directions: A–D, B–E. C is left over.

23(TSA 2020, Q46) Rates & weeksAnswer C

Why it's herethe hardest Rates, ratio & proportion question in the past papers. Dividing the tablets by the weekly amount only gives an average; the question asks for the maximum, which depends on how the weekly pattern falls at the end of the box, and it asks for extra days, not total days.

My dog’s coat had been looking dull, so I bought some conditioning tablets. The pet shop stocks these in boxes of 300 tablets1, and for a dog the size of mine the recommendation (which I have followed) is 6 tablets per day2.

Now, several months later, his coat is much shinier. Opening a new box of tablets today, I have decided to reduce his dosage. I shall just give him 6 tablets per day, twice a week, and on the remaining days each week he can have 4 tablets each day3.

What is the maximum number of extra days4 that this box of conditioning tablets will now last?

  1. A10 days
  2. B15 days
  3. C16 days
  4. D24 days
  5. E25 days

Reading it properly

  • 1300 tablets in the box.
  • 2Old way: 300 6 = 50 days.
  • 3Two 6-days + five 4-days each week = 32 tablets a week.
  • 4“Maximum”: you choose which days are the 6-days, so put the 4-days where they help. Then compare with 50.

Working it out

  1. old: 300 6 = 50 days
    new week: 2 × 6 + 5 × 4 = 12 + 20 = 32 tablets

  2. How many full weeks fit in 300?

    9 × 32 = 288 (10 × 32 = 320 is too many)
    9 weeks = 63 days, with 300 − 288 = 12 tablets left

    To make the last 12 last as long as possible, use the 4-tablet days: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 tablets, so 3 more days.

  3. 63 + 3 = 66 days
    extra = 66 − 50 = 16 days
    Trap! If the leftover 12 went on 6-tablet days (6 + 6), it would last only 2 days: 65 days, 15 extra. “Maximum” means start the week with the 4s.
C16 extra days: 66 days (9 weeks + three 4-tablet days) instead of 50.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Uses an average of 5 a day ((6 + 4) 2): 300 5 = 60, so 10 extra. But there are five 4-days and only two 6-days a week.
  • B✗ Spends the last 12 tablets on two 6-tablet days, so 65 days. That’s not the maximum.
  • D✗ 24 extra means 74 days, but even 10 weeks would need 320 tablets.
  • E✗ Gives 4 a day every day: 300 4 = 75, so 25 extra. Forgets the two 6-tablet days each week.
Tip 32 a week. 9 weeks = 288 = 63 days. 12 left = three 4-days. 66 − 50 = 16.

24(TSA 2013, Q36) AveragesAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest Averages question in the past papers. The 'just under' and 'just over' averages have to be turned into firm bounds on the total distance, and miscounting the days (or the target total) by one changes the answer.

Last month, in an attempt to improve my fitness, I began to run daily. The maximum distance1 I have time to fit in on any particular day is 8 miles, but my aim is to maintain an average of at least 5 miles per day2.

After 15 days my average was just under 6 miles per day3, then the following day I increased my average to just over 64. Since then (for the last 9 days, up to and including today5) I have been able to run only 2 miles each day.

What is the minimum number of days from now6 until I can bring my average back up to 5 miles per day?

  1. A2 days
  2. B4 days
  3. C9 days
  4. D18 days
  5. E26 days

Reading it properly

  • 1The best catch-up day is 8 miles.
  • 2Average 5 means the total must be at least 5 × (number of days).
  • 3Total after 15 days: just under 15 × 6 = 90.
  • 4This is the one that matters: after 16 days the total is just over 16 × 6 = 96.
  • 59 more days at 2 miles: + 18 miles. Today is day 16 + 9 = 25.
  • 6So run the maximum 8 miles every day from now on.

Working it out

  1. Where am I today?

    days so far: 16 + 9 = 25
    miles so far: just over 96 + 9 × 2 = just over 114
    needed for an average of 5: 25 × 5 = 125
    behind by just under 11 miles

  2. Each new day the target goes up by 5. Running 8 gains 8 − 5 = 3 miles a day on it.

    3 days: 3 × 3 = 9 still about 2 short ✗
    4 days: 4 × 3 = 12 back above target ✓
    answer = 4 days
  3. Check With totals: after 4 more days, just over 114 + 32 = just over 146, and 29 × 5 = 145 ✓. After 3: just over 138, but 28 × 5 = 140 ✗.
B4 days of 8 miles: each gains 3 miles on the target, and he is about 11 behind.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Two 8-mile days win back only 6 miles; he is about 11 behind.
  • C✗ 9 undoes the nine 2-mile days one for one ((5 − 2) × 9 = 27 behind, 3). That ignores the 16 miles he was ahead on day 16.
  • D✗ 18 aims for an average of 6 again, not 5: 150 − 114 = 36 behind, gaining only 2 a day.
  • E✗ 26 is about how many days have passed. He only needs to win back about 11 miles, at 3 a day.
Tip Day 25, just over 114 miles, need 125: about 11 short. Each 8-mile day gains 3: 4 days.

25(TSA 2011, Q38) Comparing dealsAnswer A

Why it's herethe hardest Choosing a method question in the past papers. Several options give information that is sometimes enough to decide, which is tempting; you need the one that is always sufficient. Think about how the two total costs change as the number of brochures grows.

John wishes to have 500 copies1 of a brochure printed and is offered a choice of two price structures:

1. An equal cost for each brochure printed, regardless of number.2
2. An initial fixed cost followed by a lower cost than structure 1 for each brochure.3

Which one of the following sets of information would be sufficient4 for him to be able to decide which of the two price structures would be least expensive?

  1. AThe number of brochures for which the total bill would be the same by both structure 1 and structure 2.
  2. BAn example showing the cost of 500 brochures for structure 1 and 200 brochures using structure 2.
  3. CThe cost per brochure using structure 1 and the initial cost using structure 2.
  4. DAn example showing the cost of 200 brochures using structure 1 and 200 brochures using structure 2.
  5. EThe cost of 1 brochure using structure 1 and 2 brochures using structure 2.

Reading it properly

  • 1The number that matters: 500.
  • 2Structure 1: cost = price × number. A line from 0, fairly steep.
  • 3Structure 2: starts higher (the fixed cost) but climbs more slowly.
  • 4It must settle the question whatever the actual prices turn out to be.

Working it out

  1. Sketch both structures as cost lines:

    Structure 2 starts higher but grows more slowly, so the lines cross exactly once, at some number N. Below N structure 1 is cheaper; above N structure 2 is cheaper.

  2. So knowing N is all John needs: compare it with 500. That is option A.

  3. Check the others really aren’t enough:

    • B: structure 2’s cost for 200 doesn’t tell you its cost for 500, because you can’t split the fixed part from the per-brochure part.
    • C: structure 2’s per-brochure price is missing.
    • D: tells you which is cheaper at 200 only. If structure 1 wins at 200, structure 2 could still win at 500.
    • E: 2 brochures by structure 2 mixes up the fixed cost and the price per brochure.
AA: the break-even number tells you which structure is cheaper on each side of it, so you just compare it with 500.

Why not the others?

  • B✗ One cost for structure 2 (at 200) can’t be split into fixed cost and price per brochure, so its cost for 500 is unknown.
  • C✗ Structure 2’s price per brochure is missing, so its total for 500 can’t be found.
  • D✗ This only compares the two at 200 brochures. If structure 1 is cheaper there, structure 2 might still be cheaper at 500.
  • E✗ The cost of 2 brochures by structure 2 is fixed cost + 2 × price: one total, two unknowns. Not enough.
Tip Two straight lines cross once. Know the crossing point and you know which is cheaper on each side of it. A.
