1(TSA 2022, Q42) Money & logicAnswer D

Why it's herejudged the 8th-longest sustained workout of the 25. A strong candidate needs about 3 minutes on it. Three payments, six different coins and a fixed price all have to fit together at once. You need to see what 'all different' forces, then build or test sets of coin pairs for the candidate prices while checking every condition.

In my currency, there are six types of coin1 in circulation:

200p    100p    50p    20p    10p    5p

I start with two coins. I visit a shop and purchase two bars of chocolate and pay using the two coins2. My change consists of two coins.

The next day I re-visit the shop and purchase another one bar of chocolate with that change2. My new change, again, consists of two coins.

The six coins I possess during these two days are all different3. I only have two coins at any particular time. The price of a bar of chocolate does not change4.

What is the price of a bar of chocolate5?

  1. A15p
  2. B25p
  3. C30p
  4. D55p
  5. E60p

Reading it properly

  • 1Six types, and six different coins: so I have each coin exactly once. Total 200 + 100 + 50 + 20 + 10 + 5 = 385p.
  • 2Day 1: start pair − 2 bars = change pair. Day 2: change pair − 1 bar = final pair.
  • 3The key clue! The 6 coins split into 3 pairs: start, middle, final.
  • 4The same price P both days.
  • 5Test the options.

Working it out

  1. Call the price P. Working back from the final pair:

    middle pair = final pair + P
    start pair = middle pair + 2P = final pair + 3P
    all six coins: 3 × final pair + 4P = 385p

  2. Try each option: (385 − 4P) 3 must be a total two coins can make.

    15p: (385 − 60) 3 = 108⅓ ✗
    25p: (385 − 100) 3 = 95, but no two coins make 95p ✗
    30p: (385 − 120) 3 = 88⅓ ✗
    55p: (385 − 220) 3 = 55 = 50p + 5p ✓
    60p: (385 − 240) 3 = 48⅓ ✗

  3. Check 55p all the way through:

    final 50p + 5p = 55p
    middle 55 + 55 = 110p = 100p + 10p ✓
    start 110 + 110 = 220p = 200p + 20p ✓
    price = 55p
D55p: start with 200p + 20p, change 100p + 10p, then change 50p + 5p.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 15p doesn’t split the six coins into three pairs: the final pair would have to be 108⅓p.
  • B✗ 25p needs a final pair worth 95p, and no two of the coins make 95p.
  • C✗ 30p: the final pair would be 88⅓p, impossible.
  • E✗ 60p: the final pair would be 48⅓p, impossible.
Tip Each coin used once, total 385p. 3 × final pair + 4 × price = 385. Only 55p works: final 55 (50 + 5), middle 110 (100 + 10), start 220 (200 + 20).

2(TSA 2011, Q19) 3D viewsAnswer B

Why it's herejudged the 3rd-longest sustained workout of the 25. A strong candidate needs about 3½ minutes on it. Working only from the plan, you build each of the four side views by tracking where the nearest surface steps forward or back, including through the gaps between the letters. Then you have to compare line positions with the options in proportion, which needs care all the way through.

This is the view from above1 of the large three-dimensional logo situated in the forecourt of the head office of Justin Thyme Enterprises:

It was sculpted from a single cube of marble, and has a uniform cross-section from top to bottom2.

Which of the following is not3 a possible side view of the logo?

  1. Aview A
  2. Bview B
  3. Cview C
  4. Dview D
  5. Eview E

Reading it properly

  • 1A plan: the letters J, T, E (Justin Thyme Enterprises!) seen from the sky.
  • 2The shape is just pushed straight up, so each side view is a rectangle split by vertical lines.
  • 3There are 4 sides to look from. Four options match them; one doesn’t.

Working it out

  1. From the side you see a vertical line wherever the nearest surface jumps forwards or backwards. So walk round the plan and look from each of the four sides, marking the jumps:

  2. front (bottom of the plan): J foot, gap, T leg, gap, E base = 5 strips → C
    back (top of the plan): long top bar, then the J’s hook in two steps = 3 strips → D
    right: three arms of the E with two gaps = 5 strips → A
    left: the J’s stem and its curled foot = 2 strips → E
    B: 4 strips (2 | 5 | 1 | 1) ✗ no side gives that
    not possible = B
  3. Tip Count the strips first. Only if two options tie do you need to measure widths.
BView B: it has 4 strips, but the four sides give 5, 3, 5 and 2.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is the view from the right: the three arms of the E stick out, with the gaps between them set back.
  • C✗ C is the view from the front: the J’s foot, the T’s leg and the E’s base, with two gaps between them.
  • D✗ D is the view from the back: the long top bar, then the J’s hook stepping back twice.
  • E✗ E is the view from the left: the J’s tall stem and its curled-up foot.
Tip Count strips from each side: front 5, back 3, right 5, left 2. B has 4, so it can’t be a side view.

There are five towns at various positions along a major ring road that encircles a city. Traffic travelling clockwise1 around the ring road is provided with information in the form of five ‘distance’ signs at various points along the road as shown.

5(TSA 2019, Q30) Games & strategyAnswer E

Why it's herejudged the 4th-longest sustained workout of the 25. A strong candidate needs about 3½ minutes on it. You have to think like both players: for each pair Alex might take first you must find the opponent's best reply among the pairs left, and if she goes second you must check every possible opening against her best answer. Twelve adjacent pairs, two different scoring rules and a tie-break condition keep you juggling throughout.

Alex and Sue are playing a game with a set of nine cards, numbered from 1 to 9. A 3 × 3 grid of cards is dealt onto the table. The cards for the next round have just been dealt and are as shown below.

518
362
974

The first player chooses two cards and scores the difference between the two numbers1. The second player then chooses two cards from the seven that are left and scores the average of the two numbers2. The highest score wins the round. Both players must choose cards that are either horizontally or vertically next to each other3 in the grid.

For this round of the game Alex has to decide whether to play first or second. She wants to make sure that she wins the round4. If she has more than one possible winning move then she wants to achieve the highest score possible5.

What should Alex choose to do?

  1. Aplay first and choose the 1 and 8
  2. Bplay first and choose the 3 and 9
  3. Cplay first and choose the 6 and 7
  4. Dplay first and choose the 7 and 9
  5. Eplay second

Reading it properly

  • 1First player: difference (big gap wins).
  • 2Second player: average (two big cards win).
  • 3Only touching pairs, side by side or up and down. No diagonals.
  • 4She must win whatever Sue does.
  • 5Only a tie-breaker between winning moves. Winning comes first.

Working it out

  1. Find the best pairs:

    biggest difference: 1 and 8 → 7
    biggest average: 9 and 7 → 8

  2. If Alex plays first, 9 and 7 (average 8) beat her unless she takes the 9 or the 7. Check every option:

    1 and 8 → 7, Sue takes 9 and 7 → 8 ✗
    3 and 9 → 6, Sue takes 6 and 7 → 6.5 ✗
    7 and 4 → 3, Sue takes 3 and 9 → 6 ✗
    7 and 9 → 2, Sue takes 8 and 2 → 5 ✗
    6 and 7 → 1, Sue takes 3 and 9 → 6 ✗

    Playing first always loses.

  3. If Alex plays second: if Sue leaves 9 and 7, Alex scores 8, more than any difference. If Sue takes the 9 or the 7, Sue scores at most 6, and Alex still has a better pair (6 and 7 → 6.5, or 3 and 9 → 6, or 8 and 2 → 5) every time.

    second always wins play second
EPlay second: whatever Sue picks, Alex can find a touching pair with a higher average.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1 and 8 scores 7, but Sue replies with 9 and 7 for an average of 8.
  • B✗ 3 and 9 scores 6, but Sue takes 6 and 7 for 6.5.
  • C✗ 6 and 7 scores only 1; Sue takes 3 and 9 for 6.
  • D✗ 7 and 9 scores only 2; Sue takes 8 and 2 for 5.
Tip 9 and 7 average 8 beats the best difference (7). Blocking them leaves you a tiny difference. So go second.

6(TSA 2008, Q37) Shapes & countingAnswer B

Why it's herejudged the 23rd-longest sustained workout of the 25. A strong candidate needs about 2½ minutes on it. Finding a layout that works is fairly quick, but being sure that nothing uses fewer hurdles means thinking about how shared sides and the outside edge trade off.

Peter needs to separate his sheep into 16 square pens1. Each side of a pen is formed by a free-standing barrier known as a hurdle2. Each pen must be individually accessible on at least one side3 so that sheep can be penned or released without a risk of the others escaping.

What is the smallest4 number of hurdles that Peter must take out to the fields in order to accomplish his task?

  1. A40
  2. B42
  3. C48
  4. D49
  5. E64

Reading it properly

  • 116 pens × 4 sides = 64 sides, but two pens side by side can share a hurdle.
  • 2One hurdle per side of a pen.
  • 3Every pen must have a side on the outside. No pen can be boxed in by other pens.
  • 4Share as many hurdles as you can.

Working it out

  1. Count sides: a shared hurdle does 2 sides, an outside hurdle does 1.

    16 × 4 = 64 sides
    hurdles = (64 + outside hurdles) 2 keep the outside short

  2. Try some layouts:

  3. The 4 × 4 square is cheapest (40) but its middle 4 pens can’t be reached. Two rows of 8 keeps every pen on the edge:

    rows of hurdles: 3 lines × 8 = 24
    columns of hurdles: 9 lines × 2 = 18
    total = 24 + 18 = 42
    Check Could anything beat 42? It would need an outside of 18 hurdles or less. The only shapes that small are 3 × 6 or 4 × 5 blocks with a few pens missing, and they always leave pens boxed in the middle. So 42 is the best.
B42 hurdles: two rows of 8 pens, so every pen has an outside side.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 40 is the 4 × 4 square, but the four middle pens have no outside side.
  • C✗ 48 is a hollow 5 × 5 ring of pens. Every pen is reachable, but the inside of the ring wastes hurdles.
  • D✗ 49 is one long line of 16 pens (32 + 17). Fewer pens share sides.
  • E✗ 64 gives every pen its own 4 hurdles, sharing nothing.
Tip Every pen on the edge with the most sharing: 2 rows of 8. Hurdles: 3 × 8 + 9 × 2 = 24 + 18 = 42.

7(TSA 2022, Q19) Rotas & logicAnswer B

Why it's herejudged the 13th-longest sustained workout of the 25. A strong candidate needs about 3 minutes on it. Every possible swap must be checked against both rules for everyone it affects, including the person who moves to Tuesday; the rejected swaps fail for different reasons, so none can be skipped.

A youth club is open six evenings every week. There are nine volunteers who run the club. Each evening three of the volunteers are on duty.

When the rota is drawn up for any week, the following conditions must be met:

  • Each volunteer has two evenings of duty, but never two consecutive evenings1.
  • No two volunteers are on duty together twice in the same week2.

Next week’s rota has just been published, as follows:

MondayTuesdayWednesdayThursdayFridaySaturday
MelEveJanMelEveFay
RodFayLeoPatJanLeo
SamTimSamTimRodPat

Fay wants to swap her Tuesday duty with one of the others to be on duty on Monday or Thursday instead3.

Who is the only4 volunteer on the rota for Monday or Thursday that Fay could swap with if the conditions described above are still to be met?

  1. AMel
  2. BPat
  3. CRod
  4. DSam
  5. ETim

Reading it properly

  • 1Rule 1: no Mon + Tue, Tue + Wed, and so on, for anybody.
  • 2Rule 2: any two people can share at most one evening.
  • 3A swap moves two people: Fay goes to their Monday or Thursday, and they go to Fay’s Tuesday.
  • 4Check the rules for both people after the swap, and for whoever they end up working with.

Working it out

  1. The people on Monday or Thursday are Mel, Rod, Sam (Mon) and Mel, Pat, Tim (Thu). Whoever swaps moves to Tuesday, joining Eve and Tim.

  2. Knock them out one by one:

    Tim: already on Tuesday, can’t take a second Tuesday ✗
    Sam (Mon, Wed): would work Tue + Wed, two in a row ✗
    Rod (Mon, Fri): on Tuesday he’d meet Eve, and he meets Eve on Friday too ✗
    Mel’s Monday: Mel works Tue + Thu, meeting Tim on both ✗
    Mel’s Thursday: Mel works Mon + Tue, two in a row ✗

  3. Only Pat is left. Check it really works:

    the only one who can swap Pat
BPat: Fay takes Pat’s Thursday and Pat takes Fay’s Tuesday, and both rules still hold.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Mel fails both ways: swapping Monday puts Mel with Tim on Tuesday and Thursday; swapping Thursday gives Mel Monday and Tuesday in a row.
  • C✗ Rod moving to Tuesday would be on duty with Eve twice (Tuesday and Friday).
  • D✗ Sam moving to Tuesday would work Tuesday and Wednesday, two evenings in a row.
  • E✗ Tim is already on Tuesday, so swapping would give him Tuesday twice and lose his Thursday.
Tip Tim (already Tue) and Sam (Wed is next to Tue) are out at once. Rod meets Eve twice. Mel meets Tim twice or works Mon + Tue. Pat is left.

8(TSA 2020, Q24) Timetables & schedulingAnswer C

I had planned my day trip to the stately home very carefully, but then the bus was late. I won’t arrive until 11:30 am1 and will have to leave by 4:15 pm2. There were five things that I had wanted to do and I had written these down in the table below:

  1. Avisit the museum
  2. Bvisit the maze
  3. Ctake the guided tour
  4. Dwalk in the grounds
  5. Ehave lunch in the café

Reading it properly

  • 1Too late for the 11 am museum visit!
  • 211:30 to 4:15 is 4 h 45 min = 285 minutes.
  • 3Only the 2 pm museum is left: 2:00 to 2:55.
  • 4The 3 pm tour ends at 4:20, after you must leave. So the tour can only be 1:00 to 2:20.

    museum: 11 am is gone, so 2:00 → 2:55
    tour: 3 pm ends 4:20 (too late), so 1:00 → 2:20
    they overlap from 2:00 to 2:20 you must drop the museum or the tour
    Check Also, all five add up to 55 + 55 + 80 + 75 + 25 = 290 minutes, but there are only 285. Something has to go.

  2. Try dropping the museum. The tour fills 1:00–2:20. Lunch must start by 2:00, so it has to be before the tour (about 12:30–12:55).

    So keeping the tour doesn’t work.

  3. Drop the tour instead:

    maze 11:45–12:40, lunch 12:40–1:05, museum 2:00–2:55, walk 2:55–4:10 ✓
    miss out the guided tour
CMiss the guided tour: then maze, lunch, museum (2 pm) and the walk all fit before 4:15.

  • A✗ Without the museum the tour (1:00–2:20) forces lunch before 1 pm, and then the maze and walk (130 min) can’t fit in the 115 min after 2:20.
  • B✗ Dropping the maze leaves the museum (2:00) and the tour (1:00–2:20) still clashing.
  • D✗ Dropping the walk still leaves the museum and tour clash.
  • E✗ Dropping lunch still leaves the museum and tour clash.
Tip Museum must be 2:00 (11:00 is gone). Tour must be 1:00 (3:00 ends 4:20). They overlap, so the answer is A or C. Without the tour it all fits.

9(TSA 2018, Q42) Digits & logicAnswer E

Why it's herejudged the 19th-longest sustained workout of the 25. A strong candidate needs about 2½ minutes on it. Pure reasoning: you have to work out what 'ten different digits' forces about the six numbers, then push the largest number down and the smallest up at the same time.

In the main draw of a lottery, six of the balls (which are numbered from 1 to 491) are selected at random, then the numbers chosen are rearranged and displayed in ascending numerical order.

For instance:   3   17   20   29   34   45

In one draw recently, I noticed that the six numbers were made up of a total of ten digits, all different2. The lowest number on this occasion was 1 and the highest number was 49, so the range of the six numbers was 48, the greatest possible range for the lottery main draw.

What is the smallest possible range3 when the six numbers in the main draw of the lottery have a total of ten digits, all different?

  1. A21
  2. B23
  3. C27
  4. D31
  5. E32

Reading it properly

  • 1Two-digit balls can only start with 1, 2, 3 or 4. There is no ball 0.
  • 2Ten different digits = every digit 0 to 9 exactly once.
  • 3Range = highest − lowest. Make the top small and the bottom big.

Working it out

  1. How many two-digit numbers? Six numbers with ten digits:

    6 numbers + 1 extra digit per two-digit number = 10
    four two-digit numbers and two one-digit numbers

  2. The four two-digit numbers need four different tens digits, and only 1, 2, 3, 4 are allowed:

    tens digits are exactly 1, 2, 3 and 4 → the top number is in the 40s
    0 can’t be a ball on its own, so it is a units digit: put it in 40 → top = 40
    the two single balls come from 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 → biggest choice 8 and 9 → lowest = 8
  3. range = 40 − 8 = 32
E32, for example 8, 9, 15, 26, 37, 40.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 21 would need the top ball in the 20s or 30s, but four different tens digits force a ball in the 40s.
  • B✗ 23 has the same problem: the top ball is at least 40, and the lowest is at most 8, so the range is at least 32.
  • C✗ 27 would need a top of 35 or so: impossible, since one two-digit ball must start with 4.
  • D✗ 31 is 40 − 9: it forgets there are two single-digit balls. If 9 is one, the other is smaller and becomes the lowest.
Tip Four two-digit balls → tens 1, 2, 3, 4 → top at least 40 (use the 0). Two single balls from 5–9, best 8 and 9. 40 − 8 = 32.

The see-through block hides its real shape behind overlapping lines, so you first have to work out exactly what is solid, then check each view's proportions, notch position and inner lines against it.

The see-through1 glass block below can be viewed from six sides, including from above and below2.

Which one of the following is not3 a possible view?

  1. Aview A
  2. Bview B
  3. Cview C
  4. Dview D
  5. Eview E

Reading it properly

  • 1See-through: hidden edges show as lines too. So the view from the back is just the front view flipped, and so on.
  • 2Six views: front, back, left, right, top, bottom. Find which drawing matches none.
  • 3Four drawings are real views; find the odd one out.

Working it out

  1. Measure the block using the drawing’s edges as units: 7 long, 5 deep, 4 tall. At the front is a step 2 deep and 2 high. Behind it the block is 4 high, with a slot 2 wide and 1 deep cut into the back, all the way down.

  2. Now match each pair of views (opposite views are mirror images, because it is see-through):

    front / back: 7 × 4, a line across halfway (the step), two lines up (the slot sides) B ✓
    left / right: an L-shape 5 wide, tall part 3, step 2, and a line 1 in (the slot) A and D ✓
    top / bottom: 7 × 5 with a 2 × 1 notch in the back edge and a line 2 from the front C ✓

  3. E is 5 wide and 6 tall, and draws the slot as a closed box. No edge of the block is 6 long, and the slot is cut through the back face, so it must be a gap in the outline (as in C), not a box inside it.

    not possible E
EE: wrong shape (5 × 6) and the slot is drawn boxed in, but it is open at the back.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is the view from the left: L-shape with the tall part on the left and the slot’s face 1 unit in.
  • B✗ B is the view from the back: the step line across the middle and the two slot sides (3, 2, 2).
  • C✗ C is the view from below: 7 × 5, the slot as a notch in the back edge, the step line 2 from the front.
  • D✗ D is the view from the right: A’s mirror image.
Tip Side views are L-shapes (A, D), front/back is the 2 × 3 grid (B), top/bottom has the notch (C). E has a size the block doesn’t have.

23(TSA 2018, Q30) Systematic searchAnswer C

Why it's herejudged the 12th-longest sustained workout of the 25. A strong candidate needs about 3 minutes on it. Three coin values, two ordering conditions and an exact total: you need a systematic search that rules cases out quickly without missing the one that works.

Recently, packets of Amblers crisps have had ‘money-off’ coupons inside them. Some coupons are worth 9p, some are worth 14p and some are worth 20p.

George has been collecting these coupons. He has more 14p coupons than 9p coupons, and more 9p coupons than 20p coupons1. The total value of all of his coupons is exactly £1.502.

How many coupons has George collected?

  1. A9
  2. B10
  3. C11
  4. D12
  5. E13

Reading it properly

  • 1Order rule: number of 14p > number of 9p > number of 20p. So he has at least one 9p and at least two 14p.
  • 2150p. Several mixes make 150p, so the order rule picks the right one.

Working it out

  1. The 20p count is the smallest, so try 0, 1, 2, … 20p coupons and see what the 9p and 14p coupons must make:

    1 × 20p: 9s and 14s make 130 → 2 × 9 + 8 × 14 = 18 + 112 = 130 ✓
    2 × 20p: 9s and 14s make 110 → 6 × 9 + 4 × 14 ✗ (fewer 14s than 9s)
    0 × 20p: 9s and 14s make 150 → 12 × 9 + 3 × 14 ✗ (fewer 14s than 9s)
    3 or more × 20p: then at least four 9p and five 14p, already 36 + 70 + 60 = 166p ✗
    Tip Finding 9s and 14s that make 130: 130 − 14 × 8 = 18 = 2 × 9. Try counting down the 14s from 9: 130 − 126 = 4 ✗, 130 − 112 = 18 ✓.
  2. 2 + 8 + 1 = 11 coupons
C11 coupons: two 9p, eight 14p and one 20p.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 9 coupons is 5 × 14p + 4 × 20p = 150p, but that has no 9p at all, breaking the order rule.
  • B✗ 10 coupons is 4 × 9p + 1 × 14p + 5 × 20p = 150p, but it has more 20p than 14p.
  • D✗ 12 coupons is 6 × 9p + 4 × 14p + 2 × 20p = 150p, but it has more 9p than 14p.
  • E✗ 13 coupons is 10 × 9p + 3 × 20p = 150p, with no 14p coupons at all.
Tip Smallest group is 20p, so try one 20p: 130p left = 2 × 9 + 8 × 14. Order 8 > 2 > 1 works. 11.

    A, B, C, D: each has one short slanted edge = the tiny snip, and splits into the two pieces ✓
