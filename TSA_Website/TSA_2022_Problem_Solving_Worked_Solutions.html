1(Q2 in the original paper) Arithmetic & trial

A quiz consists of 7 rounds, each of which contains 20 questions. Each question is worth one point. At the end of the quiz, but before any of the answers or scores are revealed, each team is allowed to select one of the rounds and the score for that round is tripled1. In addition, the scores for the rounds immediately before and after the chosen round are doubled2. The winning team at last week’s quiz had the following scores in each of the rounds.

round1234567
score141662010184

The winning team’s final score was 1363.

Which round4 did the winning team choose to have the score tripled?

  1. Around 2
  2. Bround 3
  3. Cround 4
  4. Dround 5
  5. Eround 6

Reading it properly

  • 1Tripled = the round counts 3 times, so it adds 2 extra lots of its score.
  • 2Doubled = each neighbour adds 1 extra lot of its own score.
  • 3Compare 136 with the normal total to see how many extra points there were.
  • 4Only 5 choices (rounds 2 to 6). Just test them.

Working it out

  1. Normal total first (no tripling or doubling):

    14 + 16 + 6 + 20 + 10 + 18 + 4 = 88
    136 − 88 = 48 extra points to find

  2. Choosing a round gives extra = 2 × (that round) + (round before) + (round after). Try round 3:

  3. Check the other options too (it only takes a few seconds):

    round 2: 2 × 16 + 14 + 6 = 52 140
    round 3: 2 × 6 + 16 + 20 = 48 136 ✓
    round 4: 2 × 20 + 6 + 10 = 56 144
    round 5: 2 × 10 + 20 + 18 = 58 146
    round 6: 2 × 18 + 10 + 4 = 50 138
    answer: round 3
    Tip Work with the extra points (48), not the whole new total. The numbers stay small and you add up the 7 rounds only once.
BRound 3: the extra points are 12 + 16 + 20 = 48, and 88 + 48 = 136.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Round 2 gives 2 × 16 + 14 + 6 = 52 extra, so a final score of 140.
  • C✗ Round 4 gives 2 × 20 + 6 + 10 = 56 extra, so 144. Choosing the highest-scoring round feels right but gives too much.
  • D✗ Round 5 gives 2 × 10 + 20 + 18 = 58 extra, so 146.
  • E✗ Round 6 gives 2 × 18 + 10 + 4 = 50 extra, so 138. Close, but not 136.
Tip Normal total 88, so 48 extra. Extra = 2 × chosen + both neighbours. Round 3: 12 + 16 + 20 = 48.
2(Q6 in the original paper) Mixtures & percentages

Rob has two different mixtures of apple juice and orange juice.

mixture 1   40% apple juice1 and 60% orange juice
mixture 2   70% apple juice1 and 30% orange juice

Rob wants to use these two mixtures to make 6 litres2 of a juice mixture which is 50% apple juice3 and 50% orange juice.

How much of mixture 24 does Rob need to use?

  1. A1 litre
  2. B2 litres
  3. C3 litres
  4. D4 litres
  5. E5 litres

Reading it properly

  • 1Just follow the apple. If the apple comes out right, the orange is right too.
  • 2Total volume. Half of it (3 litres) must be apple.
  • 350% is nearer to 40% than to 70%, so he needs more of mixture 1.
  • 4They want the 70% one, which is the smaller share.

Working it out

  1. Target: 50% of 6 litres = 3 litres of apple.

    Think of a see-saw. The mix sits at 50%, which is 10 away from mixture 1 and 20 away from mixture 2. The nearer mixture must be the heavier one:

  2. So mixture 1 : mixture 2 = 20 : 10 = 2 : 1. Split 6 litres in the ratio 2 : 1:

    6 ÷ 3 = 2 litres per share
    mixture 1 = 4 litres, mixture 2 = 2 litres

  3. Check the apple:

    4 × 40% = 1.6 litres
    2 × 70% = 1.4 litres
    1.6 + 1.4 = 3 litres = half of 6 ✓
    Tip No ratio trick needed: just test the options. 2 litres of mixture 2 means 4 of mixture 1, and the apple is 1.6 + 1.4 = 3. Done.
B2 litres of mixture 2 (with 4 litres of mixture 1).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 1 litre of mixture 2 + 5 of mixture 1 gives 0.7 + 2.0 = 2.7 litres of apple: only 45%.
  • C✗ 3 litres of each just averages 40% and 70%, which is 55% apple, not 50%.
  • D✗ 4 litres is the amount of mixture 1. Giving the bigger share to mixture 2 gets the ratio upside down (60% apple).
  • E✗ 5 litres of mixture 2 gives 3.5 + 0.4 = 3.9 litres of apple: 65%.
Tip 50% is twice as close to 40% as to 70%, so the ratio is 2 : 1. A third of 6 litres = 2 litres of mixture 2.

Fran is a lecturer1 and regularly needs to contact all of the departments in the university.

How many of the extension numbers are never2 needed by Fran?

  1. A0
  2. B1
  3. C2
  4. D3
  5. E4

Reading it properly

  • 1Only the lecturer column matters for Fran.
  • 2Numbers that appear somewhere in the table but not in Fran’s column.
  • 3Read down this one column only. The neighbouring columns are traps.

Working it out

  1. List every different number in the whole table: 117, 232, 239, 387, 558, 661. That’s 6 numbers.

  2. Now read down the lecturer column:

    661, 117, 387, 117, 117, 232, 387, 661

  • D✗ 3 comes from the parent column (232, 558 and 661 missing).
  • E✗ 4 is how many numbers Fran does use, not how many she never uses.
Tip Six different numbers overall. Lecturer column uses 117, 232, 387, 661. Missing: 239 and 558, so 2.
4(Q8 in the original paper) Charts & percentages

Over the past decade the popularity of coffee has soared in the United Kingdom. As a result, the number of coffee shops across the UK has risen. The table below shows a breakdown of the number of chain and independent coffee shops in East London between 2013 and 2019.

2013201420152016201720182019
chain1620354570100130
independent25154550100140
total218255090120200270

Which graph shows the information1 in the table above?

  1. Agraph A
  2. Bgraph B
  3. Cgraph C
  4. Dgraph D
  5. Egraph E

Reading it properly

  • 1Every bar reaches 100, so the graphs show percentages of each year’s shops, not numbers. Black = chain.
  • 2Use the totals to turn chain shops into a percentage.

Working it out

  1. Every bar is full height (100), so each bar shows the share of that year’s shops. From the key, the black part is chain shops.

  2. Chain share = chain ÷ total:

    2013: 16 ÷ 18 ≈ 89%
    2014: 20 ÷ 25 = 80%
    2015: 35 ÷ 50 = 70%
    2016: 45 ÷ 90 = 50%
    2017: 70 ÷ 120 ≈ 58%
    2018: 100 ÷ 200 = 50%
    2019: 130 ÷ 270 ≈ 48%

  3. Now look for the two giveaways: black rises in 2017, and 2019 is just under 50.

    A: keeps falling after 2016 ✗
    C: 2017 too high (about 63), 2019 too low (about 43) ✗
    D, E: black starts at about 11%: that’s the independent share ✗
    B: 89, 80, 70, 50, 58, 50, 48 ✓
    answer: graph B
BGraph B: chain shops fall from 89% to 50%, rise to 58% in 2017, then 50% and 48%.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A keeps falling after 2016 (about 40%, 32%, 31%). But in 2017 chain shops are 70 of 120, which is more than half.
  • C✗ C has the right shape, but 2017 is too high (about 63%, not 58%) and 2019 too low (about 43%, not 48%).
  • D✗ D starts with black at about 11%. In 2013 the chain shops were 16 of 18, nearly all of them.
  • E✗ E is the independent share drawn in black: the colours are swapped. The key says black is chain.
Tip 2013: 16 of 18 is about 89% chain (kills D and E). 2017: 70 of 120 is 58%, up from 50% (kills A). 2019: 130 of 270 is just under half (kills C).
5(Q12 in the original paper) Best value & rates

The shampoo that I normally buy costs £1.961 for a 500 ml2 bottle and always lasts for 40 days1.

Last month I decided to try a new brand that is 36p cheaper3 and contains 550 ml2. However, because of the amount that I have had to use to wash my hair properly, it has run out after only 25 days4.

Based upon my experience, by how many pence per day5 is my normal shampoo better value than the new brand?

  1. A1.5
  2. B2.4
  3. C4.9
  4. D6.4
  5. E9.5

Reading it properly

  • 1Normal: 196p over 40 days.
  • 2The ml are a distraction. What matters is how many days each bottle lasts.
  • 3New price = 196 − 36 = 160p.
  • 4New: 160p over 25 days.
  • 5Cost per day for each, then the difference.

Working it out

  1. Cost per day = price ÷ days it lasts. Work in pence.

  2. normal: 196 ÷ 40 = 4.9p a day
    new: 160 ÷ 25 = 6.4p a day
    difference: 6.4 − 4.9 = 1.5p
    Tip 196 ÷ 40: 200 ÷ 40 = 5, then take off 4 ÷ 40 = 0.1, so 4.9. And 160 ÷ 25 = 160 × 4 ÷ 100 = 6.4.
A1.5p a day: 4.9p (normal) against 6.4p (new).

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 2.4 uses the new price for both: 160 ÷ 25 − 160 ÷ 40 = 6.4 − 4.0.
  • C✗ 4.9 is the normal shampoo’s cost per day, not the difference.
  • D✗ 6.4 is the new shampoo’s cost per day, not the difference.
  • E✗ 9.5 is far too big: the daily costs are only 4.9p and 6.4p, so the gap must be less than 6.4p.
Tip 196 ÷ 40 = 4.9p. (196 − 36) ÷ 25 = 6.4p. Difference 1.5p. Ignore the ml.
6(Q13 in the original paper) Tables & rules

Kate and Tek are choosing a cottage to rent for their family holiday. They have searched online and reduced their choice to five cottages. The facilities offered by these cottages are shown in the following table.

cottagenumber of bedroomslarge gardenwifiparkingpets allowed4distance to nearest storecost per week
Acorns3noyesnoyes2 km$460
Beeches3yesyesnoyes1 km$490
Chestnuts4noyesyesno2 km$510
Denders4yesyesyesno4 km$500
Eglers2noyesyesno2 km$450

Ideally, Kate and Tek would like a cottage with at least three bedrooms, a large garden, wifi, parking, and they would like to be within 3 km of a store2. They realise that it is unlikely that all their wishes will be met, so they decide to compromise. They insist that the cottage must have at least three bedrooms1, but they will be happy if at least three of their other wishes3 are satisfied. If more than one cottage is suitable, then they will choose the one that is the cheapest5 to rent.

Which cottage will Kate and Tek choose?

  1. AAcorns
  2. BBeeches
  3. CChestnuts
  4. DDenders
  5. EEglers

Reading it properly

  • 1A must. Anything with fewer than 3 bedrooms is out straight away.
  • 2The other wishes: garden, wifi, parking, store within 3 km. That’s 4 wishes.
  • 3They need 3 or 4 of those 4.
  • 4Pets are not on their wish list. Don’t count this column.
  • 5Only compare prices among the cottages that pass.

Working it out

  1. Bedrooms first: Eglers has only 2, so it’s out (even though it’s the cheapest).

  2. Count the 4 other wishes for each of the rest (ignore pets):

  3. Beeches $490 < Denders $500 < Chestnuts $510
    cheapest Beeches
BBeeches: 3 bedrooms, garden, wifi and a store 1 km away, for $490.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Acorns ($460) meets only 2 wishes: wifi and the store. Count “pets allowed” as a wish by mistake and it seems to pass.
  • C✗ Chestnuts is suitable, but at $510 it is the dearest of the three that pass.
  • D✗ Denders is suitable (the store is 4 km away, so 3 wishes), but $500 is more than Beeches.
  • E✗ Eglers is the cheapest of all ($450) but has only 2 bedrooms, and 3 bedrooms is a must.
Tip Cut Eglers (2 bedrooms). Acorns has only wifi + store. Of Beeches, Chestnuts and Denders, Beeches is cheapest at $490.
7(Q14 in the original paper) Equations & logic

Alex and Anita play a game where they need to form words from letters. The value of each letter is a whole number of points (which can be 0)1. Alex and Anita earn points by adding together the value of the letters in the words they form.

The value of the last three words they formed is shown in the table below.

wordvalue
MOAN7
MOON8
NOON10

Anita has just formed the word ANNA.

What is the value of the word ANNA2?

  1. A4
  2. B6
  3. C8
  4. D10
  5. E12

Reading it properly

  • 1You can’t find every letter exactly, and you don’t need to.
  • 2ANNA = 2 A + 2 N. You only need A + N.

Working it out

  1. Compare words that are almost the same, and pair up the letters:

  2. MOON − MOAN: O − A = 8 − 7 = 1
    NOON: N + O + O + N = 10, so N + O = 5
    A + N = (O − 1) + N = 5 − 1 = 4
    ANNA = 2 × (A + N) = 2 × 4 = 8
    Check Try real values to check: O = 3, N = 2, A = 2, M = 0. MOAN = 7, MOON = 8, NOON = 10 ✓, ANNA = 8. Other values that fit (say O = 1, N = 4, A = 0, M = 2) also give 8.
C8: A + N = 4, and ANNA is two lots of A + N.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 4 is A + N, only half the word. ANNA has two As and two Ns.
  • B✗ 6 would need A + N = 3, meaning A is 2 less than O. MOON and MOAN differ by only 1.
  • D✗ 10 treats A as worth the same as O, as if ANNA were NOON.
  • E✗ 12 gets the difference the wrong way round (A = O + 1), so A + N = 6. MOAN is the smaller word, so A is worth less.
Tip MOON − MOAN gives O = A + 1. NOON gives N + O = 5, so N + A = 4. ANNA = 2 × 4 = 8.
8(Q18 in the original paper) Money & comparing costs

Sean is taking his car to Sunny Island. He lives 38 miles away from the mainland terminal where the seaport and airport are based1. He has researched the travel costs and come up with two possible options. The ferry ticket (car and passenger) is $802. Alternatively, a plane ticket is $50 and the seaport company will transport his car (port to port) for $1003. Fuel costs are $1.50 per mile4.

How much money will Sean save5 by taking the cheaper option?

  1. A$13
  2. B$20
  3. C$30
  4. D$70
  5. E$127

Reading it properly

  • 1The seaport and the airport are in the same place. He drives the same 38 miles whichever he picks.
  • 2Option 1: $80 covers both Sean and his car.
  • 3Option 2: $50 for Sean + $100 for the car = $150.
  • 4Fuel is the same for both options, so it cancels out of the saving.
  • 5Saving = dearer option − cheaper option.

Working it out

  1. Draw the two journeys:

  2. ferry: $80
    plane + car shipped: $50 + $100 = $150
    saving = 150 − 80 = $70
    Tip Anything that is the same in both options (here the 38-mile drive) can be ignored when you only want the difference.
D$70: the ferry ($80) is $70 cheaper than the plane plus car shipping ($150).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ $13 = 70 − 57: takes the $57 of fuel off one option only. Both options need the same drive.
  • B✗ $20 = 100 − 80: compares the car shipping with the ferry and forgets Sean’s $50 plane ticket.
  • C✗ $30 = 80 − 50: compares the ferry with the plane ticket alone and forgets the $100 for the car.
  • E✗ $127 = 70 + 57: adds the fuel to the plane option only. He drives to the terminal either way.
Tip Same drive both ways, so ignore fuel. Plane + car = $150, ferry = $80. Save $70.
9(Q19 in the original paper) Rotas & logic

A youth club is open six evenings every week. There are nine volunteers who run the club. Each evening three of the volunteers are on duty.

When the rota is drawn up for any week, the following conditions must be met:

  • Each volunteer has two evenings of duty, but never two consecutive evenings1.
  • No two volunteers are on duty together twice in the same week2.

Next week’s rota has just been published, as follows:

MondayTuesdayWednesdayThursdayFridaySaturday
MelEveJanMelEveFay
RodFayLeoPatJanLeo
SamTimSamTimRodPat

Fay wants to swap her Tuesday duty with one of the others to be on duty on Monday or Thursday instead3.

Who is the only4 volunteer on the rota for Monday or Thursday that Fay could swap with if the conditions described above are still to be met?

  1. AMel
  2. BPat
  3. CRod
  4. DSam
  5. ETim

Reading it properly

  • 1Rule 1: no Mon + Tue, Tue + Wed, and so on, for anybody.
  • 2Rule 2: any two people can share at most one evening.
  • 3A swap moves two people: Fay goes to their Monday or Thursday, and they go to Fay’s Tuesday.
  • 4Check the rules for both people after the swap, and for whoever they end up working with.

Working it out

  1. The people on Monday or Thursday are Mel, Rod, Sam (Mon) and Mel, Pat, Tim (Thu). Whoever swaps moves to Tuesday, joining Eve and Tim.

  2. Knock them out one by one:

    Tim: already on Tuesday, can’t take a second Tuesday ✗
    Sam (Mon, Wed): would work Tue + Wed, two in a row ✗
    Rod (Mon, Fri): on Tuesday he’d meet Eve, and he meets Eve on Friday too ✗
    Mel’s Monday: Mel works Tue + Thu, meeting Tim on both ✗
    Mel’s Thursday: Mel works Mon + Tue, two in a row ✗

  3. Only Pat is left. Check it really works:

    the only one who can swap Pat
BPat: Fay takes Pat’s Thursday and Pat takes Fay’s Tuesday, and both rules still hold.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Mel fails both ways: swapping Monday puts Mel with Tim on Tuesday and Thursday; swapping Thursday gives Mel Monday and Tuesday in a row.
  • C✗ Rod moving to Tuesday would be on duty with Eve twice (Tuesday and Friday).
  • D✗ Sam moving to Tuesday would work Tuesday and Wednesday, two evenings in a row.
  • E✗ Tim is already on Tuesday, so swapping would give him Tuesday twice and lose his Thursday.
Tip Tim (already Tue) and Sam (Wed is next to Tue) are out at once. Rod meets Eve twice. Mel meets Tim twice or works Mon + Tue. Pat is left.

Students studying geography at college were tested at the start of the year and at the end of the year. Their marks are shown in the following table.

The following scatter graph shows the marks for the 12 students, but one of the points has been incorrectly plotted1.

Which student2 has a mark that is incorrectly plotted on the scatter graph?

  1. AFfion
  2. BGary
  3. CHuw
  4. DKen
  5. EMei

  • 1No numbers on the axes! The grid is 10 × 10 and marks go up to 100, so each square = 10 marks.
  • 2Only 5 students to check: Ffion, Gary, Huw, Ken, Mei.
  • 3Gary and Ken both started on 48, so their dots must be in the same column.

  3. Ken (48, 60) ✓
    Gary should be (48, 50), but the dot is at (48, 80) ✗
    Ffion (60, 56) ✓ Mei (60, 80) ✓ Huw (40, 35) ✓
    wrong one Gary
    Tip Pairs that share a starting mark (Gary and Ken on 48; Ffion and Mei on 60; Huw and Pete on 40) are the quickest to check: just look up one column.

  • E✗ Mei (60, 80) is there. The stray dot sits right next to Mei’s on the 80 line, which makes Mei easy to blame by mistake.
Tip Gary and Ken both start on 48. The 48 column has dots at 60 and 80, not 50 and 60. Ken is fine, so Gary is wrong.
11(Q24 in the original paper) Speed, distance & time

Iman and Maya ran a 100 metre race. Iman started 2 seconds after Maya and finished 3 seconds before her1.

If each girl ran at a constant speed2 throughout the race, and Iman’s speed was 25% faster than Maya’s3, for how many seconds did Iman run4?

  1. A15 seconds
  2. B18 seconds
  3. C20 seconds
  4. D22 seconds
  5. E25 seconds

Reading it properly

  • 1Iman’s running time is 2 + 3 = 5 seconds less than Maya’s.
  • 2So time = 100 m ÷ speed for each girl.
  • 3Trap! 25% faster does not mean 25% less time. Speed × 1.25 means time × 0.8.
  • 4Iman’s own running time, not the clock time when she finishes.

Working it out

  1. Picture the race on a clock that starts when Maya sets off:

  2. Same distance, so time goes the opposite way to speed:

    speed × 1.25 = speed × 54
    so time × 45: Iman takes 45 of Maya’s time
    the missing 15 of Maya’s time = 5 s
    Maya = 25 s, Iman = 25 − 5 = 20 s

  3. Check with speeds:

    Maya: 100 ÷ 25 = 4 m/s
    Iman: 100 ÷ 20 = 5 m/s
    5 is 25% more than 4 ✓
    Tip Quickest exam route: test the options. Iman t, Maya t + 5, and (t + 5) ÷ t must be 1.25. Only 20 works: 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25.
C20 seconds: Maya takes 25 s at 4 m/s, Iman takes 20 s at 5 m/s.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 15 s treats “25% faster” as “25% less time” (Maya 20 s, Iman 15 s). Then Iman’s speed is 6.67 m/s against 5 m/s: 33% faster, not 25%.
  • B✗ 18 s would make Maya’s time 23 s, and 23 ÷ 18 ≈ 1.28: Iman would be about 28% faster, not 25%.
  • D✗ 22 s is the clock time when Iman crosses the line (she started at 2 s). She only ran for 20 s.
  • E✗ 25 s is Maya’s time, not Iman’s.
Tip Iman’s time is 5 s less. 25% faster means time × 4/5, so 1/5 of Maya’s time = 5 s. Maya 25, Iman 20.
12(Q25 in the original paper) Best value & percentages

Five popular makes of fishcake are shown in the table below, with comparisons of their ingredients, the weight of each type and the cost for a pack of two1.

typefishpotatocoatingweight of each fishcake2cost of pack of 2 fishcakes
Arctic45%40%15%100 g£3.00
Banquet40%50%10%100 g£2.50
Chilco55%35%10%150 g£5.00
Dyner50%35%15%150 g£4.00
Evertop35%45%20%50 g£1.00

Ester wants to buy the fishcakes that contain the greatest amount of fish per pound spent3.

Which make of fishcake should Ester buy?

  1. AArctic
  2. BBanquet
  3. CChilco
  4. DDyner
  5. EEvertop

Reading it properly

  • 1The price is for 2 fishcakes.
  • 2The weight is for 1 fishcake. So a pack holds twice this.
  • 3Grams of fish ÷ price. The highest % of fish is not enough on its own.

Working it out

  1. Fish in a pack = 2 × weight × fish %. Then divide by the pack price:

    Arctic: 200 g × 45% = 90 g, ÷ £3 = 30 g per £
    Banquet: 200 g × 40% = 80 g, ÷ £2.50 = 32 g per £
    Chilco: 300 g × 55% = 165 g, ÷ £5 = 33 g per £
    Dyner: 300 g × 50% = 150 g, ÷ £4 = 37.5 g per £
    Evertop: 100 g × 35% = 35 g, ÷ £1 = 35 g per £
  2. most fish per pound Dyner
    Tip Every price is for 2 fishcakes, so the “× 2” doesn’t change the order. You could use one fishcake per half-price instead and still pick Dyner.
DDyner: 150 g of fish for £4, which is 37.5 g per pound.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Arctic gives only 90 g of fish for £3: 30 g per pound, the worst.
  • B✗ Banquet gives 80 g for £2.50: 32 g per pound.
  • C✗ Chilco has the highest fish % (55%), but at £5 it gives only 33 g per pound.
  • E✗ Evertop is the cheapest pack and gives 35 g per pound: close, but less than Dyner’s 37.5 g.
Tip Fish grams per £: 90/3 = 30, 80/2.5 = 32, 165/5 = 33, 150/4 = 37.5, 35/1 = 35. Dyner wins.

The following chart shows the average salary of politicians in selected countries in absolute terms on the left (in thousands of dollars)1 and as a multiple of their country’s gross domestic product (GDP) per person on the right2.

18(Q37 in the original paper) Equations & scoring

In a ball game points are accumulated by different combinations of four winning shots called a chop, a creamer, a glink and a yip. Playing the game for the first time, the only knowledge I have about the scoring system is that a chop is worth one point more than a creamer1, and a creamer is worth twice as many points as a glink1. I lose narrowly by 47 points2 to 503, scoring 3 chops, 4 yips, 3 creamers and 4 glinks2. My victorious opponent scored 5 chops, 3 yips, 3 creamers and 2 glinks3.

Which one of the following statements about the scoring system is the only one that is true4?

  1. AA creamer is worth more points than a chop.
  2. BTwo chops are worth less than three yips.
  3. CA glink is worth 2 points.
  4. DThree yips are worth more than three creamers.
  5. EA chop is worth 6 points.

Reading it properly

  • 1Everything except the yip can be written using the glink: creamer = 2 × glink, chop = creamer + 1.
  • 2My 47 points.
  • 3The opponent’s 50 points.
  • 4Find the actual values, then test all five statements.

Working it out

  1. Call a glink g and a yip y. Then everything else follows:

  2. Write each score using g and y:

    me: 3(2g + 1) + 3(2g) + 4g + 4y = 16g + 3 + 4y = 47
    16g + 4y = 44 ÷ 4: 4g + y = 11
    opponent: 5(2g + 1) + 3(2g) + 2g + 3y = 18g + 5 + 3y = 50
    18g + 3y = 45 ÷ 3: 6g + y = 15

  3. The two short equations differ only in the g’s:

    (6g + y) − (4g + y) = 15 − 11
    2g = 4, so g = 2, and y = 11 − 8 = 3
    creamer = 4, chop = 5
    me: 3 × 5 + 3 × 4 + 4 × 2 + 4 × 3 = 15 + 12 + 8 + 12 = 47 ✓
    opponent: 5 × 5 + 3 × 4 + 2 × 2 + 3 × 3 = 25 + 12 + 4 + 9 = 50 ✓
    Tip No algebra? Try glink = 1: then 4 + y = 11 gives y = 7, and the opponent would need 6 + 7 = 15, but it’s 13. Try glink = 2: y = 3 and 12 + 3 = 15 ✓.

  4. Test the statements:

    A: creamer 4 more than chop 5? ✗
    B: 2 chops = 10 less than 3 yips = 9? ✗
    C: glink = 2 ✓
    D: 3 yips = 9 more than 3 creamers = 12? ✗
    E: chop = 6? No, 5 ✗
    answer C
CA glink is worth 2 points (creamer 4, chop 5, yip 3).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A chop is a creamer + 1, so a creamer can never be worth more than a chop (4 against 5).
  • B✗ Two chops are 10 points and three yips are 9. The chops are worth more.
  • D✗ Three yips are 9 points but three creamers are 12. The yips are worth less.
  • E✗ A chop is 5 points, not 6. (6 would make the glink 2.5 and the scores wouldn’t fit.)
Tip Glink g, creamer 2g, chop 2g + 1. My score gives 4g + y = 11, the opponent’s 6g + y = 15. So 2g = 4, glink = 2.

  1. Asign 1

Reading it properly

  • 1Every sign lists the towns in the same clockwise order, each further on than the last.
  • 2Just one wrong number. The gaps between neighbouring towns must agree on every sign.
  • 3Find the sign whose gaps don’t match the others.

Working it out

  1. The distance from one town to the next is the same whichever sign you read it from. So subtract neighbouring numbers on each sign:

    sign 1: Q→R 22, R→S 17, S→T 15, T→P 24
    sign 2: R→S 17, S→T 15, T→P 24, P→Q 19
    sign 3: S→T 15, T→P 24, P→Q 19, Q→R 22
    sign 4: T→P 22, P→Q 21, Q→R 22, R→S 17 ✗
    sign 5: P→Q 19, Q→R 22, R→S 17, S→T 15

  2. Every sign agrees except sign 4. Draw the ring with the agreed gaps:

    sign 4: Tunborough 9, so Portville should be 9 + 24 = 33 (not 31)
    then Queensgate 33 + 19 = 52 ✓ (matches the sign)
    the faulty sign sign 4
    Check Fixing one number (31 → 33) mends both bad gaps on sign 4 at once, which confirms it is a single error.
DSign 4: Portville should be 33 km, not 31 km.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Sign 1 gives gaps 22, 17, 15, 24, all matching the other signs.
  • B✗ Sign 2 gives gaps 17, 15, 24, 19, all matching.
  • C✗ Sign 3 gives gaps 15, 24, 19, 22, all matching.
  • E✗ Sign 5 gives gaps 19, 22, 17, 15, all matching.
Tip Subtract neighbours on each sign. Tunborough → Portville is 24 everywhere except sign 4 (31 − 9 = 22). Sign 4.
