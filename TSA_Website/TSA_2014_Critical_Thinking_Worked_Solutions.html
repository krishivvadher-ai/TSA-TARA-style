1(Q1 in the original paper) Main conclusionYour attempt: C ✓Answer C
In the past 15 years the government has poured millions of pounds into promoting sport to try to increase adult participation. The results show no lasting effect. A survey showed that unless children 'catch the sport habit' between the ages of seven and fourteen years they are unlikely to participate in sport as an adult, and that the sports they adopt as children are likely to be the sports they play as adults.Evidence / example Schools need and should be given money to give children an interest in sport.Main conclusion Spending money trying to persuade adults to take up sport is like locking the stable door after the horse has bolted.Comparison

Which one of the following best expresses the conclusion of the argument in the passage above?

  1. AIf people do not gain an interest in sport when they are young they never will.✗ Too strong (“never”), and it is only the survey finding, a reason.
  2. BThe government has wasted millions of pounds promoting sport to adults.✗ A side point drawn from the evidence, not what we are told to do.
  3. CThe government should give funds to schools to help children catch the sport habit.✓ The “should” sentence: money for schools to get children into sport. Everything else is there to support it.
  4. DSchools need more time in the curriculum to promote sport.✗ Curriculum time is never mentioned; the argument is about money.
  5. EThe sports children adopt will determine what they play as adults.✗ This is part of the survey, so it is evidence.
Tip Look for the “should”. The survey and the stable-door comparison both point to it.

2(Q3 in the original paper) FlawYour attempt: A ✗Answer C
Sales of printed books are steadily declining, with sales dropping to a seven-year low in 2011. Independent bookshops are closing all over the country and chain bookstores are likely to be hit next. Sales of e-books, meanwhile, are booming, with many predicting that 50 per cent of all books sold within 10 years will be digital downloads. Anecdotal evidence suggests that people enjoy the convenience of digital media.Evidence / example E-books have triumphed: they have led readers away from traditional forms of print to a digital world.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is a flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt bases its conclusion on predictions of future sales rather than hard evidence.✗ There is hard evidence: a seven-year low in sales and shops closing. The prediction is a side detail.
  2. BIt ignores the advantages that e-books have over printed books.✗ E-books’ advantages would help the argument, not expose a flaw.
  3. CIt assumes that e-books have not attracted new readers.✓ “Led readers away from print” assumes e-book buyers are ex-print readers. They could be brand-new readers.
  4. DBy focussing on the closure of bookshops it ignores the creation of new electronic markets.✗ New electronic markets fit “digital has triumphed”, so this doesn’t undermine it.
  5. EIt cites anecdotal evidence.✗ Using anecdotal evidence is a small weakness, but it isn’t why the jump to “led readers away” fails.
Tip Print down + e-books up does not prove the same people switched.

3(Q4 in the original paper) AssumptionYour attempt: B ✓Answer B
When Arnold Schwarzenegger was the Governor of California, he looked for ways to make savings in an effort to balance the California budget.Context He was particularly concerned about the high cost of school text books, which he considered to be outdated.Reason Schwarzenegger, who portrayed a time-travelling robot killer in the Terminator films in which a computer network takes over the world, looked to information technology to solve the problem of the high cost of text books.Context He proposed that as an alternative to books, students could be given hand-held devices that store up-to-date books electronically and allow them access to online work for maths and science. Since students are experienced with computers and will readily accept the new technology,Reason this is not just Hollywood sci-fi but common sense.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the above argument?

  1. AThe Governor based his decisions on his film experiences.✗ The film detail is colour, and the argument doesn’t need it.
  2. BThe proposed use of information technology will save money.✓ The whole point was to save money. If the devices cost more, the plan isn’t “common sense” at all.
  3. CStudents will like these devices better because they play computer games.✗ The argument says students will accept it; it doesn’t need the computer-games reason.
  4. DThe Governor considered text books to be outdated.✗ This is stated in the passage. An assumption is unstated.
  5. EStudents cannot currently access online work for maths and science.✗ Not needed: the devices could still be sensible even if some online access exists.
Tip Negation test: “the devices will NOT save money”. Then the argument collapses, so B is assumed.

4(Q5 in the original paper) AssumptionYour attempt: E ✓Answer E
Recent news stories have mistakenly implied that animals can understand mathematics better than humans.Main conclusion Participants in a study were asked to state a choice of one of three doors, behind one of which there was a prize. Then the researcher opened one of the other two doors, revealing that there was no prize behind it. Asked if they would like to change their choice, most chose not to change. Switching to the remaining closed door would have increased their chance of winning. Even after playing the game many times, which allowed them to observe that they would win more often if they switched, most did not do so. When pigeons played a similar game, after a few attempts they switched every time.Evidence / example So the pigeons learnt from experience, whereas the people overanalysed and became confused.Intermediate conclusion

Which one of the following is an assumption underlying the above argument?

  1. ABirds are better than human beings at learning from experience.✗ Too broad (all birds, all learning), and the argument doesn’t need it.
  2. BMost people did not notice that they won more often if they switched.✗ This goes against the passage, which says they could observe it.
  3. CThe people were no better than the pigeons at calculating probability.✗ If people were no better at probability, that would back up the news stories, not the argument.
  4. DMost people thought that the probability of winning remained the same.✗ This might explain people’s choices, but the conclusion doesn’t depend on it.
  5. ELearning from experience is not a mathematical process.✓ Pigeons won by learning from experience. That only shows they don’t understand maths better if learning from experience isn’t maths.
Tip Watch the jump from “learnt from experience” to “doesn’t understand maths”.

5(Q9 in the original paper) WeakenYour attempt: C ✓Answer C
Action should be taken to prevent the use of children as low-cost labour in poor Asian countries in industries such as electronics, garment, shoe and toy manufacture. Nothing short of a ban on child labour is acceptable.Main conclusion Child labour is an attractive proposition for employers: children have quick fingers, good eyesight and are often more productive than adults and do not have to be paid very much. And there's a ready supply, thanks to the number of children dropping out of education because their parents cannot afford to educate them.Context If children have to work they are denied their childhood and education.Reason

Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the above argument?

  1. ALaws governing the minimum age for employment do not exist everywhere.✗ Missing laws make the case for action stronger.
  2. BIt is well known that children work more consistently than adults.✗ This explains why employers like child labour; it doesn’t touch the reason for a ban.
  3. CWithout the children's wages, the family could not afford to educate them anyway.✓ The ban is meant to give children their education back. If they still couldn’t afford school, the ban doesn’t do that.
  4. DWithout cheap child labour, the cost of manufactured goods would rise.✗ Higher prices are a cost, but they don’t attack the reason (childhood and education).
  5. EInternational agencies have long been pressing for free education for all.✗ Pressure for free education fits the argument’s aim, so it doesn’t weaken it.
Tip Reason → conclusion: work denies education → ban it. Attack whether a ban really gives education back.

6(Q10 in the original paper) FlawYour attempt: E ✓Answer E
During the Industrial Revolution, savvy entrepreneurs realised canals were a cost-effective, efficient way to move goods around the country. Gradually, however, canal carriage was replaced with other modes of transportation that were faster and cheaper. In twenty-first century Britain, most businesses choose to use road haulage as a means of transportation.Context With the rise in fuel costs and introduction of 'low emission' zones, road haulage is now becoming less practical and economical.Reason As the roads grind to a halt, it will not be long before the canals once again become the backbone of goods transportation.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in the above argument?

  1. ACanal transport is unlikely to be as fast as road haulage.✗ Compares canals with roads, but the argument says roads are failing anyway.
  2. BCanals are not as convenient as the road network.✗ Same problem as A: it doesn’t touch the leap to canals specifically.
  3. CIt may be possible to make road haulage cheaper.✗ A possible future fix, not the flaw in the reasoning.
  4. DIt may be very expensive to re-open the disused canals.✗ A practical cost, but even cheap canals wouldn’t mend the leap to canals.
  5. ECanals may not be the only alternative to road haulage.✓ “Roads are getting worse, so canals will win” ignores rail, air and anything else.
Tip Two options (road or canal) are treated as the only two.

7(Q11 in the original paper) Main conclusionYour attempt: A ✓Answer A
The presence of an ethical symbol, such as 'right' or 'wrong', in a proposition adds nothing to its factual content.Main conclusion If I say to someone, 'You acted wrongly in stealing that money', I am not stating anything more than if I had simply said, 'You stole that money'.Evidence / example In adding that this action is wrong I am not making any further statement about it. I am simply evincing moral disapproval.Intermediate conclusion It is as if I had said, 'You stole that money' in a tone of horror, or written it with exclamation marks after it. The tone, or exclamation mark, adds nothing to the literal meaning of the sentence. It merely serves to show that the expression is attended by certain feelings in the speaker.Comparison

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AAdding to a statement the words 'right' or 'wrong' does not alter its factual content.✓ The first sentence is the claim; the stealing example and the exclamation-mark comparison back it up.
  2. B'You acted wrongly in stealing that money' just means 'you stole that money'.✗ This is the example used as evidence.
  3. CWhen somebody states that something is wrong, they are making a statement of fact.✗ The opposite of what the argument says.
  4. DHow people feel about an action is not relevant to the issue of whether it is right or wrong.✗ The argument never says feelings are irrelevant to right and wrong.
  5. ESaying that something is right or wrong is just a way of showing disapproval.✗ A supporting step (what “wrong” does instead). It explains the main claim.
Tip Ask “which sentence do the others prove?” It is the first one.

8(Q15 in the original paper) Draw a conclusionYour attempt: C ✓Answer C
The funding of the BBC through a universal licensing system is considered by many to be outdated and unfair. There seem to be only a limited number of options for the continued funding of this public broadcasting network. Firstly, the licence fee system could continue as at present. Secondly, the BBC could raise its own revenue as a commercial company through advertising and sponsorship. Finally, the BBC could be paid for like other parts of the public sector, through general taxation. Funding of the BBC through taxation may raise questions about the BBC's independence from government. This independence might also be put at risk if the BBC had to operate as a commercial company. It might be unduly influenced by its advertising and sponsorship customers unless serious safeguards are put in place.Reason

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. AThere is no alternative to the licence fee.✗ There are alternatives: two are listed.
  2. BThe independence of the BBC is inevitably at risk.✗ “Inevitably” is too strong, and the risk applies only to the alternatives.
  3. CAlthough not perfect, the licence fee is the best option.✓ Three options; the other two both risk independence; the licence fee is criticised but has no such risk, so it is the best of a flawed bunch.
  4. DGeneral taxation will not be as efficient in raising revenue as the licence fee.✗ Efficiency is never discussed.
  5. ECommercial advertising on the BBC will damage the revenue prospects of the commercial broadcasters.✗ Other broadcasters’ revenue is never discussed.
Tip When a passage lists options and knocks out all but one, the conclusion is “the one left is best”.

9(Q16 in the original paper) AssumptionYour attempt: C ✓Answer C
A palaeontologist has argued that T.rex could not have been a predator.Main conclusion He says this because few predators have small eyes (needed to see prey) small arms (needed to hold prey), and huge legs (meaning slow speed), all of which are true of T.rex and that there is no evidence for predation such as bones with T.rex teeth embedded in them.Reason Instead, he argues, it must have been a scavenger as its skull shows it had a good sense of smell and its legs were suited for walking long distances.Intermediate conclusion Other scientists disagree, pointing out that many predators do not use forelimbs in hunting and that the speed of any dinosaur is very hard to assess.Opposing view

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the palaeontologist's argument?

  1. APredators do not leave their teeth embedded in the bones of their victims.✗ If this were true it would undercut his “no teeth in bones” point, so he can’t be assuming it.
  2. BGeneralisations cannot be made between modern and prehistoric periods.✗ Irrelevant to his reasoning.
  3. CT.rex could not have been both a scavenger and a predator.✓ “It was a scavenger, so it was not a predator” only works if it can’t be both.
  4. DAll predators must be able to move quickly.✗ “All” is too strong: he only says few predators have huge legs.
  5. EThere are not enough fossilised remains yet for scientists to make a judgement.✗ The opposite of his confident conclusion.
Tip “Instead” is the clue: he treats scavenger and predator as either/or.

10(Q17 in the original paper) StrengthenYour attempt: C ✓Answer C
There was a 17 per cent decline in public library use in the UK between 1993 and 2003 according to a report by CABE (The Commission for Architecture and the Built Environment).Evidence / example They suggest this is not due to a lack of public interest in reading books. Rather it is the authoritarian style and image of libraries with their rules about silence and fines for late returns of books.Intermediate conclusion Those few libraries that have moved away from this image such as the one in Bournemouth, which provide cafes and 'chill-out' zones, have seen a 40 per cent increase in library use.Evidence / example Therefore it is clear that the future of the function of the UK library service will be more assured if all libraries follow this lead.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, would most strengthen the above argument?

  1. AThe decline in public library use has not occurred in other European countries.✗ Other countries tell us nothing about the cause in the UK.
  2. BThe main users of public libraries are school children looking for reference books.✗ Doesn’t link to the image or the cafés.
  3. CBookshops have experienced an increase in sales since 1993.✓ More book-buying shows people still want to read, so the problem really is the libraries’ image.
  4. DThe use of computers in libraries has increased over the same period.✗ Computer use going up doesn’t back up the “image” explanation.
  5. EFew people who use new-style libraries use them to borrow books.✗ This weakens it: new-style users aren’t borrowing books, so the library’s function isn’t being saved.
Tip The weak spot is “not due to a lack of interest in reading”. Find evidence that people still want to read.

11(Q21 in the original paper) FlawYour attempt: D ✗Answer B
The Government are transferring the cost of a university education from the state to the student.Context This is described as a reform which will put universities' finance on a sustainable footing with more freedom and less bureaucracy. In addition it will lead to a greater choice for students with a focus on high quality teaching.Reason The rise in tuition fees to £9,000 per year, making the cost of a three–year degree £27,000, should not be considered as too high.Intermediate conclusion After all, the cost of a Mercedes Benz B-Class Sports Model is about the same price on the road as a three-year degree. This should put the rise in tuition fees into perspectiveComparison and we can conclude that the tuition fees are very reasonable.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in this argument?

  1. AA university education is worth more than a car.✗ A counter-opinion, not the flaw (and it would even suggest the fees are fine).
  2. BThe cost of a car is not relevant.✓ “Costs the same as a car, so it’s reasonable” is the only step to the conclusion, and a car’s price tells us nothing about whether £27,000 is reasonable.
  3. CTransferring the cost from the state to the student is not fair.✗ Fairness isn’t the reasoning used to reach “reasonable”.
  4. DThere is no evidence that students will have greater choice.✗ This attacks a side claim, not the step to the conclusion. Flaw questions want the broken link.
  5. EUniversities will have to raise the fees in future.✗ Future fee rises have nothing to do with this reasoning.

12(Q22 in the original paper) Parallel reasoningYour attempt: D ✓Answer D
You probably don't have to be a cat-lover to enjoy The Great Race – an excellent short film about a Yorkshireman who goes running with (and against) his one-eyed cat.Reason Now he wants to make it a full-length film,Context but this is a mistake.Main conclusion Eight minutes of feline fun is about as much as any audience can take.Reason Cats are like babies in this respect: endlessly fascinating to their owners, enjoyable only in short bursts for everyone else.Comparison

Which one of the following most clearly parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AAlmost any subject can be made into a short film. However, there are very few which are interesting enough to be made into full-length movies.✗ A general claim about subjects; nothing about outsiders enjoying only a short dose.
  2. BWhen you study history the first things you learn about a new topic or period can be dull: the interest lies in the increasing fascination of the arguments and detail you discover as you read more deeply and get to know it, and the people, better.✗ The reverse: more is better.
  3. CIt is always a mistake to assume that just because people like you, they share your enthusiasm for babies and cats.✗ Borrows the cats-and-babies topic, but reasons about assuming others share your enthusiasm.
  4. DMy friends are just back from holiday. I shall be keen to see a few photographs but dread being invited round for a whole evening being enthusiastically shown pictures of the same group standing in front of different places.✓ Same skeleton: a little is lovely, a lot is too much for everyone except the enthusiast.
  5. EThe problem with making films about subjects such as cats and babies is that there is not enough variety in their behaviour to be interesting for more than a short period of time.✗ Same topic (cats and babies) but a different reason (lack of variety). Matching the topic is the trap.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: X is enjoyable in a short dose for everyone its fans find it endlessly fascinating, but everyone else only enjoys short bursts so a long version is a mistake.

DX = holiday photos · short dose = “a few photographs” (keen) · fans = the friends “enthusiastically showing” · long version = “a whole evening” (dread). ✓ Same shape.
A“Few subjects are interesting enough for full length”: no short-versus-long dose for outsiders. ✗
BGets better the more you see: opposite direction. ✗
CAbout assuming people share your enthusiasm: no conclusion about length. ✗
EGives a different reason (too little variety) and keeps the same topic. ✗
Tip Parallel questions: ignore the topic, copy the shape. Write the argument with X and Y and test each option.

13(Q23 in the original paper) PrincipleYour attempt: E ✗Answer C
The Universities Minister faced a torrent of criticism when he suggested that students could find themselves "cut-price degrees" at the last minute as universities charging the new maximum fee of £9,000 would slash prices to fill any vacant places.Context The chair of a group of student unions attacked the proposal, saying: "If a student qualifies for a place they will pay one amount, while someone who fails but then gets an unfilled place at the end will somehow pay less."Evidence / example Clearly this turns justice on its head.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best matches the principle underlying the above argument?

  1. AIf you wait until the last minute to book a holiday you can often pay less than if you booked well in advance.✗ This just describes last-minute discounts as normal; it doesn’t condemn them.
  2. BSomeone who applies for a job after the closing date should not be appointed over someone who applied in time.✗ About being late, not about doing worse. A late applicant hasn’t failed anything.
  3. CIt would be wrong in any competitive event to reward those who are second-best more than those who win outright.✓ Principle: the one who does worse (fails) shouldn’t get a better deal (pay less) than the one who does better.
  4. DEducation should not be treated like a commodity which can be bought and sold.✗ A different principle (education as a product).
  5. EA convicted criminal should not be released early just because the prisons are overcrowded.✗ About leniency because of lack of space. It matches the situation (spare places), not the principle (worse shouldn’t beat better).

Working: write the principle first

Student who qualifies pays more. Student who fails pays less. “Injustice”.
So the principle: never reward the weaker performer more than the stronger one.

C: second-best rewarded more than the winner → wrong. ✓ Same principle in a new setting.

14(Q27 in the original paper) Main conclusionYour attempt: E ✗Answer B
We need to find solutions to the problem of high levels of carbon dioxide emissions. A number of solutions are being proposedContext but one of the most promising is that of using willow trees as a fuel for generating electricity. There are many environmental advantages in using willow.Intermediate conclusion Burning wood has the environmental advantage that in doing so one releases into the atmosphere only as much carbon dioxide as the trees themselves absorbed. In addition, plantations of willows will not only support the insects on which songbirds feed, but also, being trees that thrive in wet areas, they will help to conserve our threatened wetlands. Other countries, such as Sweden, have already recognised the advantages of using willow for fuel.Evidence / example We should follow their example.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the argument above?

  1. AWe need to solve the problem of the high rate of carbon dioxide emissions.✗ The background problem that sets the scene.
  2. BWe should use willow trees as a means of generating electricity.✓ “We should follow their example” = we should use willow to generate electricity. That’s what everything else supports.
  3. CUsing willow as a fuel will reduce the level of carbon dioxide emissions.✗ One of the reasons (and it overstates it: “reduce”).
  4. DPlanting willows on a large scale will help conserve our wildlife.✗ One of the advantages: evidence.
  5. EThere are many environmental advantages in using willow trees for fuel.✗ An intermediate conclusion. It is a reason for the final “should”.
Tip When the last line says “We should follow their example”, turn it into plain words: we should use willow for fuel.

15(Q28 in the original paper) Draw a conclusionYour attempt: C ✗Answer B
Brazilian police in Bahia recently ended their strike over low pay, which lasted 12 days and saw the region's homicide rate double. During that time, politicians accused some police officers of causing panic by encouraging looting while on strike. Now the strike has ended they have offered an amnesty against the punishment of any striking officers as long as they did not commit any crimes during the strike. For their part, the officers have received a 6.5 per cent pay rise and have complained that they were played off against the public and ended their strike so they could properly police the Salvador carnival.Evidence / example The Government contest this and say their decision to stop salary payments to any officers still on strike is what made the police return to work.Opposing view

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. AThe police will halve the homicide rate in Bahia now they are back at work.✗ The rate doubling during the strike doesn’t mean it will halve now.
  2. BThe Salvador carnival will be policed by a full-strength police force on improved pay.✓ The facts both sides agree on: the strike is over (everyone back) and they got a pay rise, so the carnival is policed by a full force on better pay.
  3. CThe Government had to threaten to cut off police pay to get them to go back to work.✗ This is only the Government’s side of a disputed claim, so it can’t be drawn as fact.
  4. DThe police were happy to accept a 6.5 per cent pay rise.✗ They complained; nothing says they were happy.
  5. EThe Government knows it was underpaying officers, which is why it chose not to punish them for going on strike.✗ Pure guesswork about the Government’s motives.
Tip Only conclude what is agreed fact. When two sides disagree (“The Government contest this”), neither side’s story is a safe conclusion.

16(Q29 in the original paper) AssumptionYour attempt: D ✗Answer B
When preparing for examinations, it is important that students do not suppress any emotional or other problems they might be having during their course of study. A good way of revealing problems is the creation of a partnership and a discussion between students, parents and teachers. This is far preferable to the problems only becoming apparent with the arrival of the results causing upset and disappointment for all concerned.Reason Therefore, if schools and colleges create as many opportunities as possible for three-way meetings between students, parents and teachers, they will make a valuable contribution to their students' achievements in examinations.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the above argument?

  1. AOther factors besides emotional problems are not important in determining examination success.✗ Far too strong, and not needed.

  3. CStudents often do not communicate as well with their parents as they do with teachers.✗ The argument doesn’t compare who students talk to better.
  4. DThe problems revealed by the three-way discussion can always be solved.✗ “Always” is too strong. Meetings can help even if some problems can’t be solved.
  5. EEmotional factors are just as important for students' success as their academic ability.✗ “Just as important” isn’t needed.
Tip Assumptions are the minimum the argument needs. Words like always, all and never are usually too strong.

17(Q33 in the original paper) WeakenYour attempt: E ✗Answer A
It is a strange fact that many people find violence entertaining as long as it's fictional. Encountering a chainsaw-wielding maniac on television in one's own home is obviously less traumatising than meeting one at the bus stop. But is it actually fun? The same is often true of reading crime novels. Take, for example, the popularity of the violent Swedish 'Millennium Trilogy', also now made into films. Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the above argument?

  1. AMost murders take place within the family unit and are premeditated.✓ If most killings are planned and within families, they aren’t thrill-seekers attacking passers-by. That breaks the link from “more crime” to “people need real violence for fun”.

18(Q34 in the original paper) FlawYour attempt: C ✓Answer C
A recent peaceful demonstration against government cuts ended in violence caused by relatively small numbers of 'anarchists' attacking police and property.Evidence / example Anarchism is a political philosophy, and as a group, anarchists tend to be unable to agree among themselves on exactly what it means. But what does it tell us about these events or about those involved if we call everyone with a brick in their hand an anarchist?Context Given that the central idea all anarchists have in common is the abolition of the state (not necessarily by violence),Reason 'anarchist' is a daft way to characterise the violent hangers-on of a demonstration protesting not for but against the shrinking of the state.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in the argument above?

  1. AThe fact that some anarchists are peaceful does not mean that violent demonstrators cannot be anarchists.✗ The argument never says violent people can’t be anarchists because some anarchists are peaceful.
  2. BThe protesters against government cuts are not protesting against a shrinking of the state.✗ This contradicts the facts of the passage rather than naming a flaw.
  3. CThe presence of anarchists at a demonstration does not mean that they agree with its official aims.✓ It assumes the violent people shared the march’s aim. Anarchists could join a protest they disagree with just to cause trouble.

  5. EThe violent demonstrators want the Government to increase, not cut, spending.✗ This repeats the argument’s own assumption instead of exposing it.

24(Q46 in the original paper) Parallel reasoningYour attempt: B ✓Answer B
The level of light pollution must be low in order to be able to observe the moon, planets and stars. My house is in the darkness of the countrysideReason so stargazing will always be possible from my back garden.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AMy train must be on time if I am going to catch my plane. The train is now half an hour late so I will not get to the airport on time.✗ “Need A; A has failed → no B”: valid reasoning, different shape.
  2. BMy doctor has told me that contact with a sufferer is necessary if someone is to catch flu. Ten members of my class now have flu so I am bound to catch it.✓ Same skeleton and the same mistake: a necessary condition is met, so the result is treated as guaranteed.
  3. CWe need to have an exceptionally high tide on this part of the coast for flooding to occur. There has not been a flood so therefore there cannot have been exceptionally high tides.✗ “Need A for B; no B → so no A”: a different (and different-wrong) shape.
  4. DBoiling water is essential to make an excellent cup of tea. The water used to make my aunt's tea was not hot enough so she did not enjoy it.✗ “Need A; A missing → no B” (plus a jump from “excellent” to “enjoy”): different shape.
  5. EThe finest wines in the world come from France. I buy French wines to ensure that I always have the best.✗ Close, but its first premise is a fact about where the best wines come from, not a stated requirement (“must”, “necessary”). B keeps the exact pattern.

Working: necessary vs sufficient

Original: you need A (low light pollution) to get B (stargazing) I have A so I will always get B.

The flaw: needing A doesn’t mean A is enough (clouds can still spoil it).

Bneed A (contact) for B (flu) → I have A (class full of flu) → “bound to” get B. ✓ Identical shape and flaw.
Aneed A → don’t have A → no B. ✗ (That one is actually sound.)
Cneed A for B → no B → so no A. ✗
Dneed A → no A → no B. ✗
E“the best are French” → buy French → have the best. Similar-ish, but there’s no “necessary” condition. ✗
Tip Write the original as “need A for B; have A; so B” and look for the option with the same three parts in the same order.
