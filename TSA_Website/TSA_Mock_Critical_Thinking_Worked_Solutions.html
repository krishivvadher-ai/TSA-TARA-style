1(TSA 2014, Q47) PrincipleAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Applying a principle question in the past papers. You must first distil the principle (the difference between harm as the aim and harm as an accident), then recognise it in an unexpected setting phrased as a recommendation. The option about spectators enjoying misfortune fits boxing's surface features and is the obvious lure. You have met this one before: it was in the 2014 paper you sat.

Although boxing is unacceptable, other risky sports are not.Main conclusion When people get injured or killed in horse sports, motor sports or while climbing, yachting or skydiving it is because things have gone horribly wrong. When people get killed in boxing it is because things have gone horribly right. The aim of any boxing match is to cause more permanent brain damage to your opponent than you yourself sustain.Reason

Which one of the following best illustrates the principle underlying the argument?

  1. ALaws are made to protect people from risks of which they are not fully aware.✗ About protecting people from risks they don’t know about.
  2. BMedical treatment should be limited to those who do not pursue lifestyles which cause medical conditions.✗ About who deserves medical treatment.
  3. CSpectator events where people take pleasure in others' misfortune should be banned.✗ About spectators enjoying misfortune, not what the activity is aiming at.
  4. DWe should not make bonfire parties illegal in the way that arson is.✓ Principle: harm as the aim = unacceptable; harm as an accident = acceptable. Bonfire (fire damage would be an accident) vs arson (damage is the aim).
  5. EDifferent attitudes to risk in life can be expressed in sport.✗ About attitudes to risk; no difference between harm by accident and harm on purpose.

Working: write the principle first

Risky sports: harm = things going wrong OK.
Boxing: harm = things going right (the aim) not OK.

D: bonfire (harm only if it goes wrong) is fine, arson (harm is the point) is not. ✓

2(TSA 2018, Q29) AssumptionAnswer A

Why it's herethe fourth-hardest Assumption question in the past papers. The gap is an abstract value judgement about what a library is for, not a factual claim, so it is easy to miss. Several options sound like the librarian's thinking but fail the negation test, one because of a single over-strong word.

In a survey done by a school librarian during one year, borrowing of books in the library declined by 20%. Amongst books borrowed, rates of late return or loss of books also increased by 10%. On the other hand, student use of computers in the library increased by 25%. This finding was reinforced by frequent complaints from students that there were no computers available when they needed to use them.Evidence / example The librarian concluded that the best response to these findings was to cut the number of books and purchase more computers.Main conclusion 20% of books were sold off or given away, the space given over to additional computer tables, and the library’s entire available budget given over to purchasing new computers.Context

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the librarian’s reasoning?

  1. AThe library should cater for the students’ preferences rather than trying to change them.✓ Students use books less and computers more, so the librarian gives them what they want. That only follows if the library should follow students’ preferences, not try to win them back to books.
  2. BA decline in the borrowing of books indicates a decline in reading.✗ Not needed. The librarian responds to borrowing and computer use, not to how much students read.
  3. CStudents were unjustified in complaining about the lack of computers.✗ The opposite: the librarian acts on the complaints, treating them as fair.
  4. DStudents’ educational achievement is improved by using computers.✗ Not needed. The reasoning is about demand, not about results.
  5. ELibrary use will necessarily increase if more computers are provided.✗ “Necessarily increase” is too strong. The aim is to meet the demand already there.
Tip Negation test: “the library should try to CHANGE students’ preferences”. Then cutting books makes no sense.

3(TSA 2011, Q34) FlawAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Flaw question in the past papers. The flaw only appears if you hold the argument to the goal set in its first sentence and check whether the recommended spending meets it. The options are unusual, and two sound like sensible objections but would actually support the conclusion.

The museum has decided to spend money in the hope of staying profitable and, therefore, remaining open.Context It has opened an expensive new front hall, which is very big and well planned, and will undoubtedly impress new visitors.Evidence / example For serious students, however, it has nothing to offer that was not there before. All the scholars who want to do so already use the museum, they will continue to come however artistic the displays may or may not be.Reason So, it was a waste to spend so much money on appearances,Intermediate conclusion the money should have been spent on improving conditions for those who come to study.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AThe museum cannot be sure that new visitors will be attracted by its new front hall.✗ A doubt about the hall would, if anything, support calling it a waste.
  2. BThere is no evidence that visitors to museums are interested in the conditions in which the contents are displayed.✗ Display conditions for visitors are not the issue. The conclusion is about study conditions.
  3. CIf scholars will continue to come anyway, the museum will not make more money by improving their conditions.✓ The aim is to stay profitable. Scholars come anyway, so spending on them earns nothing. The argument’s own reason undermines its conclusion.
  4. DThere is no contact or sympathy between serious scholars and members of the general public in museums.✗ Never assumed or relevant.
  5. EIt is regular users, and not potential new visitors, to the museum who deserve to be rewarded.✗ A value judgement that backs the conclusion. It is not a flaw.
Tip Hold the argument to its own goal (profit). Does the recommended spending meet it?

4(TSA 2018, Q41) WeakenAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Weaken question in the past papers. The weakener looks like a dull neutral fact until you home in on 'for an extended period' in the first reason. Several other options feel like weakeners on first reading but actually make poorer young people's position worse, or help them find placements without helping them afford them.

The financial circumstances of many young people make it difficult for them to forego paid employment for an extended period in order to undertake unpaid internships or work experience placements. In many industries, it is unlikely that a young person will gain entry-level paid employment unless he or she has held one of these temporary unpaid positions.Reason Therefore, the current system of unpaid internships creates an unfair advantage for individuals from more affluent backgrounds.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the above argument?

  1. AUnpaid internships and work experience placements often provide young people with an important opportunity for gaining references.✗ Makes the placements more valuable, so missing out matters more: this strengthens.
  2. BThe highest proportion of unpaid internships and work experience placements are based in a few central locations, such as London.✗ Living in London costs more, which hurts poorer people: this strengthens.
  3. CUnpaid internships and work experience placements come in various lengths of duration from one to two days up to a number of months.✓ The problem is giving up pay “for an extended period”. If many placements last only a day or two, poorer young people can do them too, so the advantage shrinks.
  4. DSome young people find themselves moving from one unpaid position to another without being able to land a paid job.✗ Shows unpaid positions can drag on, which hurts poorer people: this strengthens.
  5. ESome schools and other organisations have programmes in place to help match young people with internships or work experience placements.✗ Helps people find placements, but not afford them. The barrier is money.
Tip Attack the key phrase in the reason: “for an extended period”.

5(TSA 2021, Q23) Draw a conclusionAnswer A

Why it's herethe second-hardest Draw a conclusion question in the past papers. The supported conclusion is never stated and at first looks like a leap, while the near-misses about public awareness and actual spending priorities sound closer to the text. You must follow the passage's own link between what we can imagine needing, what we care about and what we will back, and notice that each near-miss quietly changes one of those terms.

Conditions in some of the UK’s prisons are scandalous.Intermediate conclusion Inspectors found some prisons to be dirty, vermin-infested places, with frightening levels of violence and prisoners having easy access to illegal drugs.Evidence / example Some critics attribute the poor management of these prisons to the fact that the government has allowed private companies to run many prisons, although inspectors also criticised some state-run prisons. Others say the real problem is one of drastic funding cuts, affecting both state-run and privately-run prisons.Context Either way, we have allowed the situation to become very serious – and why? Because for the most part, we do not care.Intermediate conclusion The truth is that, personally, we can much better imagine that at some point we’ll need a clean, well-run hospital than a decent criminal justice system.Reason

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. APeople are more likely to support spending on hospitals than on prisons.✓ We don’t care about prisons because we can picture needing a hospital but not a prison. So people will back hospital spending more readily.
  2. BThe general public are not sufficiently aware of the scandalous prison conditions.✗ The passage says we don’t care, not that we don’t know.
  3. CThe government should not allow private companies to run prisons.✗ The passage reports this view, then sets it aside (“Either way”).
  4. DFunding cuts are the cause of the poor conditions in prisons, both state-run and private.✗ Also just a view it reports, not one it accepts.
  5. EHospitals have been made a greater priority for spending than prisons.✗ Nothing is said about what has actually been spent on hospitals.
Tip Two views followed by “Either way”: the conclusion won’t pick one of them.

6(TSA 2022, Q45) Parallel reasoningAnswer B

Why it's herethe second-hardest Parallel reasoning question in the past papers. One option copies the original's wording almost word for word but reasons the opposite way, with the expected thing happening rather than failing to happen. The real match uses different wording and puts its conclusion first, so you must ignore the surface and compare skeletons.

If he was telling the truth about the incident, then we would not expect him to look so nervous talking to the police.Reason The fact that he looks so nervousEvidence / example suggests he is probably lying.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AIf everyone who found themselves in the same position as Zach behaved the same way that he did, then we would be in an absolutely terrible situation. Fortunately for us, not everyone does behave in that way.✗ No observed fact leads to a conclusion. It just says not everyone behaves that way.
  2. BThe volume of waste they generate shows that most coffee companies do not care about the environmental impact of their businesses. If they did, they wouldn’t serve coffee in disposable cups.✓ Same shape: if A then not B; but B; so not A. It just puts the conclusion first.
  3. CIf Bella really wanted to come to the party, then she would have told Anne. Unfortunately, since we are unable to contact Anne, we can’t know if Bella really wanted to come.✗ Ends with “we can’t know”: no conclusion is drawn.
  4. DIf Einstein’s theory of relativity is correct, then we would expect light rays from distant stars to bend as they pass our own star. The fact that we are able to observe this suggests Einstein’s theory is correct.✗ The trap: same wording, but if A then B; B is seen; so A. The expected thing happens instead of failing to happen.
  5. ENo one who saw the game on Saturday would have thought it was between two sides at the very bottom of the league. If you’d seen the game, you would have agreed that the standard of football was impressively high.✗ Two claims about what viewers would think. No fact used to rule anything out.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: if A (telling the truth) then not B (not nervous) but B (he is nervous) so probably not A (lying).

BIf A (they cared) then not B (no disposable cups) → B (masses of waste from disposable cups) → so not A (they don’t care). ✓ Same shape, conclusion first.
AIf everyone did X then terrible → not everyone does X. No inference from a fact. ✗
CIf A then B → can’t check B → can’t know A. No conclusion. ✗
DIf A (Einstein right) then B (light bends) → B seen → so A. The expected result happens. ✗
ESaw the game → would think it was good football. Just restates a claim. ✗
Trap! D copies the wording (“we would expect… the fact that… suggests”). Ignore the wording: does the expected thing fail to happen (original) or happen (D)?

7(TSA 2021, Q34) FlawAnswer E

Why it's herethe hardest Flaw question in the past papers. The obvious weak step (from 'hard to change' to 'genetic') is a red herring that no option describes accurately. The real flaw is a quiet change of wording between the last two sentences, and two options misdescribe the argument in plausible ways.

The dark tendencies humans may have, such as psychopathy (lack of empathy), narcissism (excessive self-absorption), egoism and sadism are based on the same single underlying source: a drive to maximize one’s individual interests, and to disregard or malevolently cause harm to others’ interests. These tendencies often underlie the most serious violent crime in any society, usually dealt with by imprisonment.Context Psychiatrists say that people with these characteristics are notoriously difficult to change.Evidence / example This suggests that these personality traits are likely to be inherited genetic dispositions over which people have no control. It is wrong, therefore, to punish people for the actions that have resulted from such tendencies.Intermediate conclusion The justice system should not imprison someone for actions over which they had no control.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt fails to show the research on which the psychiatrists’ conclusions were based.✗ Not quoting the research is not where the reasoning goes wrong.
  2. BIt ignores differences in punishments between different countries.✗ Other countries’ punishments are irrelevant.
  3. CIt implies that imprisonment is the only option for sentencing.✗ It argues against prison. It never says prison is the only option.
  4. DIt suggests that ways of altering serious antisocial behaviour can never be found.✗ It says “difficult to change”, not “can never be changed”.
  5. EIt assumes that the only objective of imprisonment is punishment.✓ “Wrong to punish” only leads to “should not imprison” if prison is just punishment. It also protects the public from dangerous people.
Tip Watch a word swap between steps: “punish” in one, “imprison” in the next. Do they mean the same?

8(TSA 2022, Q15) Main conclusionAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Main conclusion question in the past papers. One option is a near-paraphrase of a premise about what environments include and reads like the main point, and another restates the author's concession. You must see what the 'Whilst' clause concedes and which claim the light-and-noise evidence is there to prove.

There is no denying the unwanted consequences of car emissions, burning of fossil fuels, chemical effluent, and other pollutants. Their negative effects include the increasing incidence of respiratory, skin, and heart conditions.Context But ‘environments’ consist of more than just the elements of air, earth, and water.Reason Whilst it is quite right to headline the damage we are doing to our lungs and other bodily organs,Opposing view it must not be thought that this is the only way in which we are polluting our world.Main conclusion The effects of artificial light and constant noise are also detrimental to mental and physical wellbeing.Reason Many people, to their cost, rarely see the stars in the night sky, or experience the quiet of the natural environment in which our species evolved.Evidence / example

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AThe word ‘environment’ does not apply only to the elements of air, earth, and water.✗ A reason: what ‘environments’ include.
  2. BIt is right to give prominent coverage to the damage human activity is doing to our bodies.✗ A point the author concedes (“quite right to headline”), not what is argued for.
  3. CIt is wrong to think that pollution of the air, earth and water is the only form of environmental damage.✓ “It must not be thought that this is the only way we are polluting our world.” C says the same in plain words.
  4. DThe effects of artificial light and noise are physically and mentally harmful.✗ A reason: light and noise do harm too.
  5. EMany people are deprived of the sight of the stars or the experience of natural silence.✗ An example of the harm from light and noise.
Tip After “Whilst X,” the main clause is the author’s own point.

9(TSA 2015, Q28) Draw a conclusionAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Draw a conclusion question in the past papers. The passage only condemns a practice, so you must work out what it implies should happen instead. Two descriptive options about why sound bites are used and what politicians prefer sound reasonable, but they claim motives the passage never establishes.

An intensely irritating fashion among politicians of all parties is to use the sound bite 'doing the right thing' to justify whatever action they have taken or propose to take.Context The phrase cleverly implies that if you do not agree with the action you are an immoral person – and no-one wants to be that. Taken to its logical conclusion, all we need are politicians who have some sixth sense that tells them what 'the right thing' is, and, hey presto, we can trust them to act on our behalf without any need for accountability.Reason This is nothing short of dangerous brainwashing.Intermediate conclusion

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion of the passage above?

  1. ASound bites are used to avoid proper explanations for decisions.✗ Too general: the passage attacks one sound bite, not sound bites in general.
  2. BPoliticians always think they know what is the right thing to do.✗ “Always think they know” is too strong. The passage is about what they say, not what they believe.
  3. CPoliticians should give proper rationales for their decisions, not glib sound bites.✓ Using “doing the right thing” in place of a real justification removes accountability and is “dangerous brainwashing”. So politicians should give proper reasons instead.
  4. DIrritating sound bites have the effect of alienating the general public.✗ The public’s reaction is never discussed. “Brainwashing” suggests it works on them, if anything.
  5. EPoliticians prefer to avoid giving a proper rationale for their decisions.✗ A guess about what politicians prefer. The passage criticises the practice, so the conclusion is what they should do.
Tip When a passage ends by condemning a practice, the conclusion is usually what should be done instead.

10(TSA 2023, Q21) StrengthenAnswer C

Why it's herethe hardest Strengthen question in the past papers. A dense, abstract passage, and the strengthener's relevance is indirect: you have to see why a fact about how the original photo was made becomes something that colourisation erases. One option reads as relevant but actually cuts the other way, and another attacks the people doing the colourising rather than the practice.

In recent years, the production and visibility of colourised and ‘restored’ historical photographs has grown exponentially, owing to both social media and the introduction of user-friendly colourisation software. But the expansion of the technical capacity and popular appetite to alter historical photography has not been matched by sufficient debate about the assumptions and consequences of this practice.Context Colourisers do not address photographs as physical artefacts with unique histories of creation and reception: they treat original photos as ‘windows’ onto the past that can be ‘improved’ for viewers. But a photograph is never just a neutral representation of the past. Every photograph carries with it a complicated history of who took it, who looked at it, how it was shaped by the technology behind it, and what all this means for questions of representation, experience, and history.Reason Colourisation blocks out this rich repository of historical meaning, substituting a fleeting jolt of novelty for deeper understanding of the image.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the above argument?

  1. AArtificial intelligence is now being used to colourise photographs automatically.✗ Only says how colouring is done, not that it hides meaning.
  2. BColourisation techniques are almost as old as photography itself.✗ Makes colouring part of photography’s own history. If anything this weakens.
  3. CSome photographers of the past chose to use black and white film instead of colour.✓ If black and white was a deliberate choice, it is part of the photo’s history of creation. Colouring it in wipes that choice out, just as the argument says.
  4. DSome universities no longer teach courses on the history of photography.✗ About university courses, not about what colourisation does to a photo.
  5. EColourisers rarely have academic experience of studying history.✗ An attack on who does it. The argument is about what colourisation does, whoever does it.
Tip Strengthen the last sentence: find the option showing that the lack of colour itself carries meaning.

11(TSA 2017, Q47) PrincipleAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest Identifying a principle question in the past papers. A short, abstract passage where every option borrows its words. You must separate the rule for choosing what to do from the details of how it is carried out (cost and speed), and recognise the passage's 'greatest good' idea in different wording.

In a situation where every course of action is immoral, there still remains the ability to act for the greatest good for the greatest number.Reason Therefore, the outsiders ought to be eliminated at the least possible cost and with the least possible delay. I am sorry to arrive at that conclusion, but it is the right step.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best illustrates the principle contained in the above argument?

  1. AThe swiftest and cheapest option is often the least immoral action.✗ Takes the “least cost, least delay” detail, which is how to do it, not the rule for choosing.
  2. BThe action that harms the least number of people is the best possible action.✓ “The greatest good for the greatest number” means picking the option that harms the fewest people. B states that as a general rule.
  3. CWhere all options are immoral, it is necessary to consider cost and speed.✗ Cost and speed again. The deciding rule is the greatest good, not cost or speed.
  4. DAn action cannot be considered totally immoral if it is the right action.✗ The argument says every option is immoral, yet one is still the right step. It never says the right step is less immoral.
  5. EAn action cannot be considered totally immoral if it is done for the greater good.✗ Same mistake as D. The argument accepts the action is immoral even though it serves the greater good.

Working: write the principle first

Every option is immoral still choose the greatest good for the greatest number so eliminate the outsiders (quickly and cheaply).
So the principle: choose the action that does the most good, and least harm, for the most people.

BFewest people harmed = best action. ✓ The same rule.
AFastest and cheapest = least immoral. Cost and speed are only how it is carried out. ✗
CAll immoral → think about cost and speed. Right situation, wrong deciding rule. ✗
DRight action → not totally immoral. The argument still calls every option immoral. ✗
EGreater good → not totally immoral. Mixes up “best choice” with “not immoral”. ✗
Tip Separate the rule for choosing (greatest good) from the details of doing it (cost, delay).

12(TSA 2023, Q47) AssumptionAnswer E

Why it's herethe second-hardest Assumption question in the past papers. The correct assumption looks almost like a restatement of the conclusion, so it is easy to dismiss. Two close-sounding principles about rich and poor are either too strong (one would rule out the author's own proposal) or simply not needed.

In the USA, interest paid on a home loan is tax-deductible (meaning it can be deducted from your taxable income, reducing the tax you pay to the government). This tax break is known as mortgage interest deduction,Context and it should be abolished.Main conclusion After all, the poorest Americans generally rent their homes rather than owning them, and rent cannot be deducted from taxes. And certain technicalities related to how taxes are filed often prevent poorer homeowners from receiving the deduction.Reason This means that the poorest Americans benefit the least from mortgage interest deduction. The people who benefit the most are the richest,Intermediate conclusion since they own the most expensive houses and have the largest tax bills, and often employ accountants to file their taxes for maximum deductions. The American government could more than double rental assistance payments to poorer Americans with the money lost as a result of this tax break.Reason

Which one of the following is an underlying assumption of the above argument?

  1. AGovernment money should not be spent subsidising private household consumption choices.✗ Too broad. The argument wants more rental assistance, which is itself a subsidy for housing.
  2. BThe financial benefits you receive from the government should not depend on whether you are rich or poor.✗ Too strong. The argument wants extra help just for poorer people, so it accepts benefits that depend on wealth.
  3. CMoney raised by taxing the rich should be used to support the poor.✗ Not needed. Abolishing the break is justified by its unfairness, whatever the money is then used for.
  4. DThere are no low-cost accounting services that poorer Americans can access.✗ Not needed. The technicalities and the renters’ point stand without it.
  5. EThe government should not offer tax breaks that benefit the richest most and the poorest least.✓ The reasons only show the break helps the rich most and the poor least. To get to “abolish it”, you need the rule that such tax breaks should not exist.
Tip The missing rule bridges “this is unequal” to “so get rid of it”.

13(TSA 2014, Q34) FlawAnswer C

Why it's herethe second-hardest Flaw question in the past papers. A dense, opinionated passage with a rhetorical question and a 'not for but against' twist that must be untangled first. The flaw is never stated, and three options echo or paraphrase the passage, one of them picking up its 'not necessarily by violence' aside. You have met this one before: it was in the 2014 paper you sat.

A recent peaceful demonstration against government cuts ended in violence caused by relatively small numbers of 'anarchists' attacking police and property.Evidence / example Anarchism is a political philosophy, and as a group, anarchists tend to be unable to agree among themselves on exactly what it means. But what does it tell us about these events or about those involved if we call everyone with a brick in their hand an anarchist?Context Given that the central idea all anarchists have in common is the abolition of the state (not necessarily by violence),Reason 'anarchist' is a daft way to characterise the violent hangers-on of a demonstration protesting not for but against the shrinking of the state.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is the best statement of the flaw in the argument above?

  1. AThe fact that some anarchists are peaceful does not mean that violent demonstrators cannot be anarchists.✗ The argument never says violent people can’t be anarchists because some anarchists are peaceful.
  2. BThe protesters against government cuts are not protesting against a shrinking of the state.✗ This contradicts the facts of the passage rather than naming a flaw.
  3. CThe presence of anarchists at a demonstration does not mean that they agree with its official aims.✓ It assumes the violent people shared the march’s aim. Anarchists could join a protest they disagree with just to cause trouble.
  4. DThe diverse nature of anarchism makes it possible to call anyone an anarchist, as even they do not know exactly what the word means.✗ This agrees with the argument’s point that the label is unclear.
  5. EThe violent demonstrators want the Government to increase, not cut, spending.✗ This repeats the argument’s own assumption instead of exposing it.
Tip The leap: “the demonstration opposed cuts” → “everyone at it opposed cuts”.

14(TSA 2021, Q40) Parallel reasoningAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Parallel reasoning question in the past papers. Every option is a long temperature argument, and the most natural-looking match uses a temperature that is enough to cause the result rather than needed for it. You must test needed-versus-enough in every option, and the true match hides behind an aside about unknown ancient furnaces.

In order for a gritted road surface to freeze over, temperatures must fall to –10 °C or below. Above this temperature, grit will continue to melt ice on the road.Reason Since ice has formed even on treated surfaces in recent weeks,Evidence / example the temperature must have fallen below –10 °C at times.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AIn order to make bread successfully it is essential that the oven reaches a temperature of 200 °C or above. If this is not reached or maintained, the bread will not rise properly. Since this bread has not risen, the oven must not yet be up to temperature.✗ Reverses the rule: “not hot enough, so it won’t rise” does not give “not risen, so not hot enough”. Other things stop bread rising.
  2. BTemperatures of 37 °C and above cause chocolate to melt and release some of the oils it contains. When it cools again a whitish residue is left behind. Since this chocolate has a white residue, at some point it must have been kept at a temperature exceeding 37 °C.✗ 37 °C is shown to be enough to cause the residue, not needed for it. The residue could have another cause.
  3. CA fever is medically defined as an oral temperature exceeding 37.7 °C, but a diagnosis of fever may be given below this point. Since the patient’s notes state that he had a fever on admission to hospital, we can be confident that his temperature was 37.7 °C or above at that time.✗ It admits a fever can be diagnosed below 37.7 °C, so the temperature is not needed for the outcome. The conclusion doesn’t follow.
  4. DIron melts at temperatures in excess of 1538 °C. Without reaching this temperature it is impossible to cast iron. Although we do not know exactly how some of the earliest furnaces worked, since we have found evidence of cast iron in China from around 550 BCE, we can be sure that these ancient people found ways to reach temperatures exceeding 1538 °C.✓ Same shape: a temperature is needed for something; it happened; so that temperature must have been reached. Cast iron needs over 1538 °C, cast iron was found, so they got over 1538 °C.
  5. EIdeally coffee should be stored below 5 °C once ground in order to help preserve its flavour. Stale coffee tastes bitter and can have a musty aroma. Since the coffee has been kept at room temperature, we should expect complaints about its flavour.✗ Predicts a result from a cause. It doesn’t work back from an outcome to the temperature it needs.
Tip Ask whether the temperature is needed for the result (safe to work back) or just enough to cause it (not safe).

Working: strip it to a skeleton
