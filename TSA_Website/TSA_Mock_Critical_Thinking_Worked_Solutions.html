1(TSA 2016, Q45) Main conclusionAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Main conclusion question in the past papers. The conclusion sits in the middle of the passage, and the final sentence begins with 'so', which pulls you towards an intermediate step. You must test which claim all the others are reasons for.

Many people criticise what they call ‘the younger generation’ for what their seniors regard as immoral behaviour.Opposing view These critics need to realise that because the world has changed, moral standards have changed with it.Intermediate conclusion In fact, technology is advancing so rapidly that it is only the young who understand the world in which they live.Reason It is they, not their parents or grandparents, who should be setting moral standards.Main conclusion Anyone over the age of 25 cannot help but be blinded by what they learned in the past. Their education has prevented them from perceiving and responding to the demands of the modern day,Reason and so their moral judgments have no validity.Intermediate conclusion

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AMoral judgments made by people over the age of 25 are not valid.✗ An intermediate conclusion: it supports why the old should not set standards.
  2. BMoral standards have changed.✗ An early step (“moral standards have changed with it”).
  3. CMoral standards should be set by people under the age of 25.✓ “It is they … who should be setting moral standards”. Over-25s are blinded, so the young (under 25) should set them.
  4. DThe younger generation is guilty of what older people regard as immoral behaviour.✗ The critics’ view, which the author is arguing against.
  5. EYoung people are the only ones who understand the contemporary world.✗ A reason (“only the young … understand the world”).
Tip The conclusion is in the middle here. Ask which sentence the others are all reasons for.

2(TSA 2015, Q41) WeakenAnswer D

Why it's herethe hardest Weaken question in the past papers. The argument is a chain of steps, and you need to spot the statistical leap within it; options about wealth or other exams feel relevant but do not break the chain.

The main reason private schools thrive in Britain is that they are extremely good at getting their students into elite universities.Context In the UK only 7% of students attend private schools but they account for 40% of students at Oxford and Cambridge.Evidence / example As entry to the top universities is dependent on examination results (particularly A-level results),Reason private school students must be achieving higher grades than those in the state sector.Intermediate conclusion Higher grades are the result of good teaching,Reason so the teaching in private schools must be better than the teaching in state schools.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, would most weaken the above argument?

  1. AA-levels are not the only exams that can be used for admission to Oxford or Cambridge.✗ Entry still depends on exam results, whichever exams are used.
  2. BOnly people from wealthy families can go to private schools.✗ Says who goes, not why they get in. Wealth would need a further link to grades.
  3. CMore than 40% of students at Oxford and Cambridge attended state schools.✗ Fits the figures: if 40% are from private schools, the rest can come from state schools.
  4. DA higher proportion of private school candidates take A-levels than those from the state sector.✓ Private pupils are 7% of all students but a much bigger share of A-level candidates. So 40% of places doesn’t prove higher grades.
  5. EMany private schools now accept scholarship students from poorer backgrounds.✗ Scholarship pupils still get the private-school teaching, so the reasoning is untouched.
Tip Check the comparison group. 7% of all pupils is not the same as 7% of the pupils who apply.

3(TSA 2011, Q39) Draw a conclusionAnswer E

Why it's herethe hardest Draw a conclusion question in the past papers. A comparison option looks like a straightforward reading of the emission figures, but you must check whether the passage really lets you compare risks for everyone, given that exposure depends on how much someone uses a phone. Any conclusion you choose must also survive the admission that no one knows the true level of risk.

Many people who live near to mobile phone masts are worried that radiation from these masts will damage their health. Mobile phones themselves have also been suspected of emitting dangerous levels of radiation, and a report recommended that children should be discouraged from making non-essential calls.Context Emission of radiation from the masts is much lower than that from a mobile phone. A few seconds making a phone call is comparable to 24 hours of exposure from a mast. Radiation from both these sources is below the international guidelines as to what is a dangerous level,Evidence / example but no one can yet be certain if these guidelines accurately reflect the level of risk.Reason

Which one of the following can be drawn as a conclusion from the above passage?

  1. APeople who use mobile phones are putting their health at risk.✗ Not shown. Phones are below the guidelines; we just can’t be sure the guidelines are right.
  2. BThe international guidelines underestimate the risk to health from low levels of radiation.✗ Too strong. The passage only says no one is certain, not that they are wrong.
  3. CThe health risk from living near a phone mast is lower than that from using a mobile phone.✗ Not safe: someone near a mast is exposed 24 hours a day, every day, which may outweigh a few calls.
  4. DPeople who live near mobile phone masts overestimate the risks to their health from these masts.✗ Can’t tell. Nobody knows the true risk, so we can’t say they overestimate it.
  5. EIf extensive use of mobile phones is not harmful to health, radiation from a mast is unlikely to be a risk.✓ A mast gives off far less than a phone. So if a lot of phone use is safe, the much smaller dose from a mast is unlikely to be risky.
Tip The uncertainty rules out firm claims. A conditional (“if… then…”) is often the safe conclusion.

4(TSA 2022, Q49) WeakenAnswer B

Why it's herethe third-hardest Weaken question in the past papers. The argument is a 'they do it too' comparison, so the key is exactly what the complaint about BBC presenters says; an option that sounds damning about the presenters does nothing to the argument, while the real weakener is easy to dismiss as background.

The government has recently announced that the method by which the British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC) is funded will be changed, leading to speculation that public funding for the organisation could soon be withdrawn.Context One of the complaints is that BBC TV and radio presenters “make their profile at public expense”, but then earn income on top of their salaries by charging to speak at events to which they are invited purely due to their association with the BBC.Opposing view However, figures show that, over the last five years, Members of Parliament (MPs) received £8.4m on top of their salaries through similar activities.Evidence / example Until MPs can behave in a way that is consistent with their criticisms of others, this policy to change the funding of the BBC should be scrapped.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the above argument?

  1. ABBC presenters make up a small proportion of all people invited to speak at events.✗ How many speakers are BBC presenters doesn’t affect the comparison with MPs.
  2. BMany MPs were established public figures before they entered Parliament.✓ The argument says MPs do “similar” things, so they are hypocrites. If many MPs were famous before Parliament, their speaking fees don’t come from a profile made at public expense, so the comparison fails.
  3. CMany MPs have second or third jobs outside Parliament.✗ Second jobs show MPs earning extra too, which if anything helps the hypocrisy point.
  4. DInvitations to BBC presenters to speak at public events do not continue once the presenter has left the BBC.✗ This backs the complaint about presenters, but the argument never denied it. It rests on MPs’ hypocrisy.
  5. EThe BBC issues clear guidelines to its employees about the types of events that they are allowed to speak at.✗ BBC guidelines don’t touch the comparison with MPs.
Tip The argument is “you do it too”. Weaken it by showing the two cases are not really alike.

5(TSA 2011, Q29) AssumptionAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Assumption question in the past papers. Two options about regional differences look almost interchangeable, and telling them apart depends on reading exactly how the spending figures are expressed. A quick reader picks the wrong twin.

A recent study by a drugs company found that the amount of money spent on a specific pill used for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease in the UK showed wide regional variation.Evidence / example Alzheimer's is a condition associated with the ageing process.Context In some areas the expenditure was as low as £1 per head of the population over 65 whereas in others it was as high as £10 per head.Evidence / example These pills are the most effective treatment for this condition that is currently available.Reason Therefore, this is another example of 'post-code lottery' where the effectiveness of treatment for a health condition will depend upon where you live.Main conclusion

Which one of the following is an assumption underlying the above argument?

  1. AThe pills are very successful in halting the progress of Alzheimer's disease.✗ Not needed. They only have to be the best treatment available.
  2. BThe drugs company that did the research doesn't have a vested interest in selling the pills.✗ Not needed. The argument uses the spending figures, not the company’s opinion.
  3. CThere are no regional variations in the incidence of Alzheimer's in the 65+ age group.✓ Spending is per person over 65, not per patient. If some areas have more over-65s with Alzheimer’s, spending should differ, and there is no lottery.
  4. DThere are no regional variations in the number of people in the 65+ age group.✗ Already dealt with: the figures are per head of the over-65s.
  5. EIt is not possible to develop Alzheimer's disease below the age of 65.✗ Not needed. A few younger patients would not explain a £1 to £10 gap.
Tip Ask “per head of what?” A fair comparison needs the right group: people who have the disease.

6(TSA 2011, Q17) WeakenAnswer C

Why it's herethe fourth-hardest Weaken question in the past papers. A statistical option looks impressive but is largely consistent with the argument; you must check which assumption about how alarms affect burglars could be undermined.

When you install a burglar alarm in your home, you do so presumably because the cost of the alarm is less than the expected benefits.Context The cost of a burglary, if one occurs, is likely to be much higher than the cost of fitting an alarm. A clearly visible box on an outside wall of an alarmed house alerts and discourages potential thieves.Reason Because your house is less likely to be burgled, your neighbour's house may be more vulnerable.Intermediate conclusion Therefore, while there may be little impact on overall crime levels, expensive security devices reduce crimes against those with the devices but increase crimes against those without.Main conclusion

Which one of the following, if true, would most weaken the above argument?

  1. AFor similar houses, a higher percentage of those without intruder alarms are burgled than those with alarms.✗ Alarmed houses burgled less: this strengthens.
  2. BAs the percentage of houses with intruder alarms increased from 40% to 90%, house burglary rates fell by 60% and there was a rise in street muggings of 100%.✗ Crime moved elsewhere (muggings) rather than vanishing. That fits “little impact on overall crime”.
  3. CBurglars perceive an alarm as a signal that there are items well worth stealing in a house, while no alarm indicates less valuable contents.✓ If an alarm box says “valuables inside”, it may attract burglars, not put them off. Then alarms need not reduce crime against their owners.
  4. DOnly 60% of houses with alarms set the alarm every day.✗ The argument relies on the visible box putting thieves off, set or not.
  5. EBurglars know that 25% of all visible alarm boxes are fakes, but cannot tell the fake from the genuine.✗ Thieves can’t spot fakes, so every box still deters: this strengthens.
Tip The argument hangs on “a visible box discourages thieves”. Attack that link.

7(TSA 2011, Q46) Fill the gapAnswer E

Why it's herethe hardest Fill the gap question in the past papers. Several linking phrases read smoothly, so you have to look closely at the grammar and logic of the sentence just before the gap to see which phrase genuinely connects it to the recommendation that follows.

Land reform is urgently needed in the UK.Intermediate conclusion It is well documented that around two-thirds of Britain is owned by just 0.3% of the population. Many of the largest estates have been in the hands of the same families for centuries, and were acquired under outdated feudal laws, often by means of aggression.Evidence / example If these families had any sense of social justice in the 21st century they would by now have voluntarily surrendered all but the acreage they needed for personal privacy.Reason __________________________________ the state should confiscate all excessive land holdings without compensation and redistribute them into the families of workers and taxpayers at affordable prices.Main conclusion

Which of the following phrases, inserted in the blank space, most logically completes the above argument?

  1. AIn the current economic climate, ...✗ The economy is never mentioned.
  2. BOn the other hand, ...✗ Signals a contrast, but confiscation follows on from the reasons rather than opposing them.
  3. CThis being the case, ...✗ The sentence before is an “if” about something that has not happened. There is no “case” to build on.
  4. DTherefore, on legal grounds, ...✗ The argument is about social justice, not law. The land was in fact acquired under (old) laws.
  5. ESince they have not, ...✓ “If they had any sense of justice, they would have given it up.” They haven’t, so the state should take it. E supplies that missing fact.
Tip Read the sentence before the gap carefully. An “if they had… they would have” sentence invites “since they have not”.

8(TSA 2011, Q33) StrengthenAnswer C

Why it's herethe fourth-hardest Strengthen question in the past papers. No option strengthens the left-turn reasoning in an obvious way: one offers evidence that is not real, and the one that works supports a part of the plan that is easy to overlook.

In a high speed police chase, police drivers sometimes lose sight of the car they are chasing.Context But that’s no reason to give up. All they have to do is take the first left, and then the first left again.Intermediate conclusion Pursued drivers are likely to keep taking turns as they come up, as this seems instinctively like the best way to lose someone who is following them. They are not going to turn right as this involves waiting and checking for oncoming traffic.Reason Therefore take a couple of left turns and the police car will soon have their criminal in sight once again!Main conclusion

Which of the following, if true, would strengthen this argument?

  1. ACar chases tend to end with the pursued driver losing control of their vehicle.✗ How chases end says nothing about the left-turn trick.
  2. BDrivers in TV car chases tend to turn left rather than right when pursued.✗ TV chases are fiction, not evidence of real drivers.
  3. CPolice drivers are usually able to sustain faster average speeds over a length of time than other drivers.✓ Taking the same turns puts police on the right road. Being faster means they close the gap and “soon” see the car again.
  4. DPolice drivers usually request overhead assistance from a helicopter when chasing criminals in cars.✗ A different way to track them. It does not support the left-turn method.
  5. ETwo left turns might take the criminal where they want to be going.✗ Only “might”, and reaching their destination could let them stop and vanish.
Tip The plan has two parts: same route, then catch up. An option that helps either part strengthens it.

9(TSA 2014, Q23) PrincipleAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Applying a principle question in the past papers. One option echoes the last-minute situation in the passage and another matches its general feel, but the principle is about something different. You must work out exactly what the student leader thinks is unjust. You have met this one before: it was in the 2014 paper you sat.

The Universities Minister faced a torrent of criticism when he suggested that students could find themselves "cut-price degrees" at the last minute as universities charging the new maximum fee of £9,000 would slash prices to fill any vacant places.Context The chair of a group of student unions attacked the proposal, saying: "If a student qualifies for a place they will pay one amount, while someone who fails but then gets an unfilled place at the end will somehow pay less."Evidence / example Clearly this turns justice on its head.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best matches the principle underlying the above argument?

  1. AIf you wait until the last minute to book a holiday you can often pay less than if you booked well in advance.✗ This just describes last-minute discounts as normal; it doesn’t condemn them.
  2. BSomeone who applies for a job after the closing date should not be appointed over someone who applied in time.✗ About being late, not about doing worse. A late applicant hasn’t failed anything.
  3. CIt would be wrong in any competitive event to reward those who are second-best more than those who win outright.✓ Principle: the one who does worse (fails) shouldn’t get a better deal (pay less) than the one who does better.
  4. DEducation should not be treated like a commodity which can be bought and sold.✗ A different principle (education as a product).
  5. EA convicted criminal should not be released early just because the prisons are overcrowded.✗ About leniency because of lack of space. It matches the situation (spare places), not the principle (worse shouldn’t beat better).

Working: write the principle first

Student who qualifies pays more. Student who fails pays less. “Injustice”.
So the principle: never reward the weaker performer more than the stronger one.

C: second-best rewarded more than the winner → wrong. ✓ Same principle in a new setting.

10(TSA 2017, Q47) PrincipleAnswer B

Why it's herethe hardest Identifying a principle question in the past papers. A short, dark passage in which every option borrows a phrase from it. You must decide whether the principle lies in the rule for choosing what to do or in the details of how it is carried out.

In a situation where every course of action is immoral, there still remains the ability to act for the greatest good for the greatest number.Reason Therefore, the outsiders ought to be eliminated at the least possible cost and with the least possible delay. I am sorry to arrive at that conclusion, but it is the right step.Main conclusion

Which one of the following best illustrates the principle contained in the above argument?

  1. AThe swiftest and cheapest option is often the least immoral action.✗ Takes the “least cost, least delay” detail, which is how to do it, not the rule for choosing.
  2. BThe action that harms the least number of people is the best possible action.✓ “The greatest good for the greatest number” means picking the option that harms the fewest people. B states that as a general rule.
  3. CWhere all options are immoral, it is necessary to consider cost and speed.✗ Cost and speed again. The deciding rule is the greatest good, not cost or speed.
  4. DAn action cannot be considered totally immoral if it is the right action.✗ The argument says every option is immoral, yet one is still the right step. It never says the right step is less immoral.
  5. EAn action cannot be considered totally immoral if it is done for the greater good.✗ Same mistake as D. The argument accepts the action is immoral even though it serves the greater good.

Working: write the principle first

Every option is immoral still choose the greatest good for the greatest number so eliminate the outsiders (quickly and cheaply).
So the principle: choose the action that does the most good, and least harm, for the most people.

BFewest people harmed = best action. ✓ The same rule.
AFastest and cheapest = least immoral. Cost and speed are only how it is carried out. ✗
CAll immoral → think about cost and speed. Right situation, wrong deciding rule. ✗
DRight action → not totally immoral. The argument still calls every option immoral. ✗
EGreater good → not totally immoral. Mixes up “best choice” with “not immoral”. ✗
Tip Separate the rule for choosing (greatest good) from the details of doing it (cost, delay).

11(TSA 2013, Q34) FlawAnswer B

Why it's herethe second-hardest Flaw question in the past papers. Two options are mirror images ('only one reason why...'), one about the Swiss and one about the British. You must work out which step of the argument actually goes wrong, not just which assumption sounds suspicious.

There is widespread dissatisfaction and disillusionment in Britain with politics and with politicians, who are seen as untrustworthy. Many of the electorate do not even bother to vote in a general election. In Switzerland democracy is more direct than in Britain, in the sense that many referendums take place, and many important political decisions are thus based directly on the votes of citizens rather than on those of their elected representatives.Context Surveys show that in those parts of Switzerland where more of these important decisions are made by referendum, the citizens are happier than in those areas where there is less opportunity to influence decisions.Evidence / example So if we were to adopt the Swiss system in Britain, the dissatisfied and disillusioned British electorate would be much happier.Main conclusion

Which one of the following identifies the flaw in the above argument?

  1. AIt assumes that most Swiss citizens vote in general elections.✗ Swiss general election turnout is never part of the reasoning.
  2. BIt assumes that there is only one reason why some Swiss citizens are happier.✓ More referendums and more happiness go together, but other things could make those Swiss areas happier. It treats the link as the cause.
  3. CIt assumes that British citizens would trust politicians if there were more referendums.✗ The conclusion is about being happier, not about trusting politicians.
  4. DIt assumes that there is only one reason why the British are disillusioned with politics.✗ Tempting, but the argument never names a cause of British disillusion. The broken step is reading cause into the Swiss survey.
  5. EIt assumes that better policies emerge when important decisions are made by referendum.✗ Better policies are never mentioned; the claim is only about happiness.
Tip Two things found together (referendums, happiness) does not mean one causes the other.

12(TSA 2017, Q11) Main conclusionAnswer D

Why it's herethe fourth-hardest Main conclusion question in the past papers. The author rejects someone else's claim, so the conclusion is a denial that you must restate precisely. One option overshoots by asserting the opposite of the original claim, and others are just the supporting evidence.

Health clubs are booming.Context It is claimed that this must be a positive sign about modern society, in that people now appreciate that a healthy body is needed in order to sustain a healthy mind.Opposing view But that claim is mistakenMain conclusion because what those optimistic conclusions fail to appreciate is people’s ability to deceive themselves. Surely if someone is really keen to stay fit they go jogging rather than join a health club?Reason The health club phenomenon is just an example of how easily people can be swayed by marketing.Intermediate conclusion They pay to jog on the spot when they could jog around the park for free. The vast majority of members barely use their health clubs and feel they are achieving something just by paying the fees.Evidence / example

Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the above argument?

  1. AHealth clubs deceive people into joining them.✗ Close to a reason (marketing), but the passage says people deceive themselves.
  2. BMost people who join health clubs are not getting healthy.✗ This is the evidence: members barely use their clubs.
  3. CPeople do not really appreciate that they must keep their bodies healthy.✗ Never claimed. The point is that people fool themselves, not that they don’t care.
  4. DThe success of health clubs is not a reliable sign of an improvement in people’s health.✓ “That claim is mistaken” means booming health clubs are not a good sign that people are healthier. D spells that out.
  5. EWe should judge the nation’s health by looking at the sales of jogging shoes not health club membership.✗ Jogging shoes are never mentioned.
Tip “It is claimed… But that claim is mistaken”: the conclusion is the rejection, so rewrite it in full.

13(TSA 2019, Q23) PrincipleAnswer B

Why it's herethe second-hardest Applying a principle question in the past papers. The closing slogan 'Practise what you preach!' makes several options look right, because they are all about consistency. You must pin down the more specific principle the passage applies and find the option that matches it exactly.

It’s common for people to agree in principle to socially useful projects such as the opening of centres for people with serious mental health problems.Context However, if the project is planned for the area where they live, they tend to object and suggest that alternative locations would be preferable.Evidence / example While they approve of the general idea of such projects, they won’t tolerate any negative effects on their own lives. We really don’t want to go back to outdated practices of housing people with mental health problems in remote areas with no contact with the general population.Reason So we have to accept that these projects can only work if we live near, and interact with, the people they are designed to help. Practise what you preach!Main conclusion

Which one of the following illustrates the principle used in the above argument?

  1. APeople who think sport has been tainted by bribery and performance-enhancing drugs should not buy tickets to sporting events.✗ About not supporting something you disapprove of, not accepting the downside of something you approve of.
  2. BPeople who think more money should be spent on the health service should not complain when their income tax is increased to fund it.✓ Principle: if you back something in principle, you must accept the cost it brings to you. Backing more health spending means accepting the tax that pays for it.
  3. CParents who think too much ‘screen time’ is bad for young children should not buy them so many electronic gadgets.✗ Also “practise what you preach”, but about avoiding something you think is bad, not accepting a cost of something you support.
  4. DPeople who choose to be vegetarians should seek to persuade others not to eat meat or fish.✗ About persuading others, not accepting a cost yourself.
  5. EPeople who have benefitted from grants or bursaries for university education should lobby for others to have the same advantages.✗ About passing on a benefit you had, not bearing a cost.

Working: write the principle first

People back mental health centres in general but object when one is planned near them (the cost to them) the projects only work if we live near them so accept it: practise what you preach.
So the principle: if you support something in principle, you must accept the cost it brings to you.

BWant more health spending → accept the higher income tax that pays for it. ✓ Same principle, new setting.
AThink sport is tainted → don’t buy tickets. Acting on disapproval; no cost of something you back. ✗
CThink screen time is bad → don’t buy gadgets. Acting on disapproval again. ✗
DVegetarian → persuade others. No personal cost accepted. ✗
EHad a grant → lobby for others. Passing on a benefit, not putting up with a cost. ✗
Trap! “Practise what you preach” loosely fits A and C too. Be precise: you support a project, so you must put up with its downside for you.

14(TSA 2023, Q49) Parallel reasoningAnswer B

Why it's herethe second-hardest Parallel reasoning question in the past papers. The option that sounds most like the original, right down to 'it's unlikely', hides a different logical connective. You need to set out precisely what the original requires, what is present and what is missing, and match that rather than the wording.

To win business investment, you need a good product and a well-developed business plan.Reason Your product is brilliant, but you haven’t spent enough time on the plan.Evidence / example So it’s unlikely you’ll get funding.Main conclusion

Which one of the following most closely parallels the reasoning used in the above argument?

  1. AFor the school to approve their proposal for meat-free lunches, the students must develop a menu and win support from their peers. The student body has approved their new menu in a poll, so it’s unlikely the school will continue to serve meat.✗ Both conditions are met, so it concludes X will happen. Treats needed as enough.
  2. BThe only way their team will be disqualified is if they miss a match and fail to notify the tournament director at least three hours before the start of play. They couldn’t attend the first match but told the director in plenty of time, meaning they won’t be disqualified.✓ Same shape: X needs both A and B. A is there (missed a match), B isn’t (they told the director), so no X.
  3. CThe new hotel manager must be fluent in Mandarin and Korean. This candidate speaks both, meaning the hotel won’t need to interview anyone else.✗ Both conditions are met, so the job is settled. Needed treated as enough.
  4. DTo earn a distinction in music, you must perform in public concerts or write your own music composition. You don’t have time for the concerts or the composition, so it’s unlikely you’ll earn a distinction.✗ Needs A or B, and neither is met. A different shape.
  5. EIf they want to take a holiday that is affordable and unique, the friends can volunteer on a farm or at a nature reserve. The farm only hosts volunteers for the weekend, so they probably won’t go there.✗ Two options, one looks unsuitable, so they won’t pick it. No “need both” rule.

Working: strip it to a skeleton

Original: X (funding) needs A and B (product and plan) A yes, B no so (probably) not X.

BX (disqualified) needs A (miss a match) and B (fail to notify) → A yes, B no (told in time) → so not X. ✓ Same shape.
AX (approval) needs A (menu) and B (support) → both met → so X. Needed treated as enough. ✗
CX (job) needs A (Mandarin) and B (Korean) → both met → so X. Needed treated as enough. ✗
DX needs A or B → neither → so not X. “Or”, not “and”. ✗
EFarm or reserve → farm is weekends only → not the farm. No two-part condition. ✗
Tip B is about a bad outcome (disqualification), not a good one. Ignore the topic and mood; match the logic.

15(TSA 2023, Q21) StrengthenAnswer C

Why it's herethe third-hardest Strengthen question in the past papers. A long, abstract passage; one option seems to back the attack on colourisers but targets the people rather than the claim about what colourisation destroys. You need to pin down exactly what the argument says is lost.

In recent years, the production and visibility of colourised and ‘restored’ historical photographs has grown exponentially, owing to both social media and the introduction of user-friendly colourisation software. But the expansion of the technical capacity and popular appetite to alter historical photography has not been matched by sufficient debate about the assumptions and consequences of this practice.Context Colourisers do not address photographs as physical artefacts with unique histories of creation and reception: they treat original photos as ‘windows’ onto the past that can be ‘improved’ for viewers. But a photograph is never just a neutral representation of the past. Original: if A (telling the truth) then not B (not nervous) but B (he is nervous) so probably not A (lying).

BIf A (they cared) then not B (no disposable cups) → B (masses of waste from disposable cups) → so not A (they don’t care). ✓ Same shape, conclusion first.
AIf everyone did X then terrible → not everyone does X. No inference from a fact. ✗
CIf A then B → can’t check B → can’t know A. No conclusion. ✗
DIf A (Einstein right) then B (light bends) → B seen → so A. The expected result happens. ✗
