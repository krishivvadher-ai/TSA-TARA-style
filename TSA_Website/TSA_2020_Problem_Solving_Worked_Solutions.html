1(Q2 in the original paper) Scores & combinations

In the game of tigball, two teams compete against each other to try to score points.

Points are scored as follows:

A ‘penalty’ scores 5 points.
A ‘tigdown’ scores 8 points.
A ‘transformation’ scores 3 points1, but teams only have the opportunity to achieve a transformation after scoring a tigdown2.

There are no other ways of scoring points.3

Which of the following is the only one that is a possible scoreline4 in the game of tigball?

  1. A9–23
  2. B11–21
  3. C12–20
  4. D13–14
  5. E15–17

Reading it properly

  • 1Three ways to score: 5, 8 and 3.
  • 2A 3 only comes straight after an 8. So it is never on its own: one 3 at most per tigdown.
  • 3So every score is built from blocks of 5, 8 and 11 (8 + 3).
  • 4A scoreline is two scores. Both sides must be possible.

Working it out

  1. Turn the rule into building blocks. A transformation is a bonus stuck onto a tigdown:

  2. Mark which totals can be made from 5s, 8s and 11s:

    5s only: 5, 10, 15, 20
    one 8: 8, 13, 18, 23
    one 11: 11, 16, 21
    two of 8/11: 16, 19, 22 … and 5 + 8 + 8 = 21

  3. Now check each scoreline. One impossible side kills it:

    A: 9 ✗ (only 3 + 3 + 3)
    B: 11 = 8 + 3 ✓, 21 = 5 + 8 + 8 ✓ 11–21
    C: 12 ✗
    D: 14 ✗ (8 + 3 + 3 needs two tigdowns)
    E: 17 ✗ (11 + 3 + 3 again)
    Tip Quick test for a number: take away 5s and see if you land on 0, 8, 11, 16, 19 or 22. For 12: 12, 7, 2. None work, so 12 is impossible.
B11–21: 11 = tigdown + transformation, 21 = penalty + two tigdowns.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 23 = 5 + 5 + 5 + 8 is fine, but 9 would need 3 + 3 + 3: three transformations with no tigdown.
  • C✗ 20 is four penalties, but 12 can’t be made: 12 − 5 = 7 and 12 − 8 = 4 don’t work, and 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 needs four tigdowns first.
  • D✗ 13 = 5 + 8 is fine, but 14 = 8 + 3 + 3 uses two transformations after only one tigdown.
  • E✗ 15 is three penalties, but 17 = 11 + 3 + 3 (or 5 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3) needs transformations without tigdowns.
Tip Blocks of 5, 8 and 11 only. Hunt the impossible side: 9, 12, 14, 17 all fail. 11 = 8 + 3 and 21 = 5 + 8 + 8 both work.
2(Q6 in the original paper) Prices & groups

A teacher is organising a trip to the local zoo for 20 students. Of the 20 students, 12 are aged 11–151 and 8 are 16–18 years of age2. Tickets cost £12.95 per adult (16 years of age and over)3 and £9.95 per child (2–15 years of age). For every 4 students under 16 they must have one teacher supervising them4. They will take the minimum number of teachers5.

What was the total admissions price for the group6?

  1. A£199.00
  2. B£223.00
  3. C£259.00
  4. D£261.85
  5. E£287.75

Reading it properly

  • 112 pay the child price.
  • 216 and over = adult. These 8 students pay the adult price.
  • 3Teachers are adults too: £12.95 each.
  • 4Only the under-16s need supervising: 12 ÷ 4 = 3 teachers. The 16–18s don’t count.
  • 5So exactly 3 teachers, no spares.
  • 6The group = students and teachers. Everyone needs a ticket.

Working it out

  1. Picture who is going and which ticket each person needs:

  2. Add up the two kinds of ticket:

    children: 12 × £9.95 = 12 × £10 − 12 × 5p = £120 − 60p = £119.40
    adults: 11 × £12.95 = 11 × £13 − 11 × 5p = £143 − 55p = £142.45
    total = £119.40 + £142.45 = £261.85
    Tip Prices ending in 95p: round up to the whole pound, then take off 5p for each ticket.
  3. Check Pence check: 12 × 95p ends in 40p, 11 × 95p ends in 45p, so the total ends in 85p. Only D does!
D£261.85: 12 child tickets (£119.40) + 11 adult tickets for 8 students and 3 teachers (£142.45).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 20 × £9.95: every student at the child price and no teachers.
  • B✗ £119.40 + 8 × £12.95 = £223.00: the students are right, but the 3 teachers are forgotten.
  • C✗ 20 × £12.95: every student at the adult price and no teachers.
  • E✗ Takes 5 teachers (one per 4 of all 20 students). Only the 12 under-16s need supervising, so 3 teachers.
Tip Only the 12 under-16s need teachers: 3. So 12 × 9.95 + (8 + 3) × 12.95 = 119.40 + 142.45 = £261.85 (only answer ending in 85p).
3(Q7 in the original paper) Tables & scoring

The Pentarathon Challenge is a competition in which five athletes compete against each other in five marathon races, run on consecutive days.

Points are awarded in each race1, as follows:

first place 7 points
second place 5 points
third place 3 points
fourth place 2 points
fifth place 1 point

This table shows the total points2 of the competitors in this year’s Pentarathon Challenge after each race.

nametotal points after race 1total points after race 2total points after race 3total points after race 4total points after race 5
Colin79101217
Jason1461314
Kyle310151825
Noel27141518
Ray5691416

Who finished in a different position in each of the five races3?

  1. AColin
  2. BJason
  3. CKyle
  4. DNoel
  5. ERay

Reading it properly

  • 1Points tell you the place: 7 = 1st, 5 = 2nd, 3 = 3rd, 2 = 4th, 1 = 5th. Each race hands out one of each.
  • 2Running totals! Take each column from the next to get the points for one race.
  • 3We want someone whose five race scores are 7, 5, 3, 2, 1 in some order.

Working it out

  1. Undo the running totals. For each person, subtract column from column:

    Colin: 7, 9 − 7 = 2, 10 − 9 = 1, 12 − 10 = 2, 17 − 12 = 5
    Noel: 2, 7 − 2 = 5, 14 − 7 = 7, 15 − 14 = 1, 18 − 15 = 3

  2. Do the same for everyone and turn points into places:

  3. Only Noel came 4th, 2nd, 1st, 5th and 3rd: a different place every time Noel

    Check Each race gives out 7 + 5 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 18 points. Check one race: race 3 gives 1 + 2 + 5 + 7 + 3 = 18. ✓
DNoel: 2, 5, 7, 1, 3 points, so 4th, 2nd, 1st, 5th and 3rd.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Colin scored 7, 2, 1, 2, 5: 4th place twice (races 2 and 4).
  • B✗ Jason scored 1, 3, 2, 7, 1: last in both race 1 and race 5.
  • C✗ Kyle scored 3, 7, 5, 3, 7: two wins and two 3rd places. (He has the most points, but that isn’t what’s asked.)
  • E✗ Ray scored 5, 1, 3, 5, 2: 2nd in both race 1 and race 4.
Tip One of each place = 7 + 5 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 18 points in total. Only Noel finished on 18. Quick check: 2, 5, 7, 1, 3. ✓
4(Q8 in the original paper) Reading graphs

Between 1987 and 1992 there were four formats of singles recordings: 7", 12", cassette and CD. The accompanying graph shows how total sales of singles were split between the four different formats1 in the years 1987 to 1992.

Which one of the following statements is false2?

  1. AThe revenue from sales of CD singles grew every year3.
  2. BThe revenue from sales of 7" singles fell every year.
  3. CThe revenue from sales of 12" singles fell every year.
  4. DSales of 7", 12" and cassette singles accounted for approximately 60% of the revenue from singles in 1992.
  5. E1989 is the first year of those shown in which 7" singles accounted for less than half the revenue from singles.

Reading it properly

  • 1Stacked bars: each format is a slice. Its size = top of the slice − bottom of the slice.
  • 2Four statements are true. Hunt for the one that breaks.
  • 3“Every year” means 5 changes in a row, all the same way. One exception makes it false.

Working it out

  1. Read each slice as top minus bottom (£m, roughly):

    198719881989199019911992
    7″ (bottom)434036292414
    12″ (black)77 − 43 = 3471 − 40 = 3171 − 36 = 3563 − 29 = 3449 − 24 = 2532 − 14 = 18
    CD (top)35691933

  2. Zoom in on 1988 and 1989. The black slice ends at 71 both years, but in 1989 it starts lower, so it is taller:

    12″ sales went up from 1988 to 1989, so “fell every year” is false C

    Trap! Don’t read the top of the black slice (77, 71, 71, 63…). That makes 12″ look like it never rose. Measure the slice itself.

  3. Quick check that the others are true:

    A: CD 3 → 5 → 6 → 9 → 19 → 33 ✓
    B: 7″ 43 → 40 → 36 → 29 → 24 → 14 ✓
    D: 1992 first three slices reach 49 out of 82: 49 ÷ 82 ≈ 0.6 = 60% ✓
    E: 7″ share: 43/80 and 40/76 are over half, 36/80 is under half ✓
CC is false: the 12″ slice grew from about £31m in 1988 to about £35m in 1989.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ True: the white CD slice gets bigger every year (about 3, 5, 6, 9, 19, 33).
  • B✗ True: the bottom 7″ slice shrinks every year (43, 40, 36, 29, 24, 14).
  • D✗ True: in 1992 the 7″, 12″ and cassette slices stack up to about 49 of the 82 total, about 60%.
  • E✗ True: 7″ is over half in 1987 (43 of 80) and 1988 (40 of 76) but under half in 1989 (36 of 80).
Tip Measure the black slice, not its top: 1988 is 40 to 71 (31), 1989 is 36 to 71 (35). It rose, so C is false.
5(Q12 in the original paper) Ratio & mixtures

A builder has 32 kg of a mix of sand and cement, of which 25% is sand1. For a new job, he needs 120 kg2 of a mix of sand and cement in which the ratio of sand to cement is 2 : 13. To obtain this, he starts with his initial 32 kg mix and adds more sand and cement4.

What is the ratio of the masses of sand and cement that he needs to add5?

  1. A4 : 7
  2. B3 : 4
  3. C5 : 3
  4. D9 : 2
  5. E12 : 1

Reading it properly

  • 125% of 32 = 8 kg sand, so 24 kg is cement.
  • 2The finished mix is 120 kg (not 120 kg extra).
  • 32 : 1 = 3 parts. Sand is 2 parts, cement 1 part.
  • 4What’s already in the 32 kg counts. Only add the difference.
  • 5Ratio of the extra sand to the extra cement.

Working it out

  1. What he has now:

    sand = 25% of 32 = 32 ÷ 4 = 8 kg
    cement = 32 − 8 = 24 kg

  2. What he needs: 120 kg split 2 : 1.

    3 parts = 120 kg, so 1 part = 40 kg
    sand = 2 × 40 = 80 kg, cement = 40 kg

  3. Top up the difference:

    sand: 80 − 8 = 72 kg
    cement: 40 − 24 = 16 kg
    72 : 16 ÷ 8 9 : 2
    Check 32 + 72 + 16 = 120 kg ✓
D9 : 2 (add 72 kg of sand and 16 kg of cement).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Turns the target round to sand : cement = 1 : 2 (40 sand, 80 cement). Then he adds 32 sand and 56 cement = 4 : 7.
  • B✗ Adding 88 kg as 3 : 4 gives about 38 sand + 50 cement, so 46 sand and 74 cement in total: more cement than sand.
  • C✗ Adding 88 kg as 5 : 3 gives 55 sand + 33 cement, so 63 sand and 57 cement: nowhere near 2 : 1.
  • E✗ Far too little cement: 88 kg as 12 : 1 is about 81 sand + 7 cement, giving 31 kg cement instead of 40.
Tip Have 8 sand + 24 cement. Need 80 + 40. Add 72 + 16 = 9 : 2.

7(Q14 in the original paper) Logic & letters

A combination lock has three dials, each dial has eight letters1. It is known that the following combinations have been used2 - one, who, two, bob, add, owl, fab, den, mia and tat.

Which one of the following words is not possible3 to use on this combination lock?

  1. Awin
  2. Bdeb
  3. Chat
  4. Dmen
  5. Ebad

Reading it properly

  • 1Letter 1 comes from dial 1, letter 2 from dial 2, letter 3 from dial 3. Only 8 letters on each.
  • 2Each used word proves which letters are on each dial.
  • 3Find a word needing a letter its dial can’t have.

Working it out

  1. Collect the letters each dial must have, position by position:

    1st letters: o, w, t, b, a, o, f, d, m, t → o w t b a f d m (8!)
    2nd letters: n, h, w, o, d, w, a, e, i, a → n h w o d a e i (8!)
    3rd letters: e, o, o, b, d, l, b, n, a, t → e o b d l n a t (8!)

    Every dial already has 8 known letters, so there is no room for anything else.

  2. Now test each word on the dials:

    Dial 1 has no h, so hat can’t be set hat

Chat: dial 1’s eight letters are o, w, t, b, a, f, d, m, with no h.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ win: w is on dial 1 (who), i on dial 2 (mia), n on dial 3 (den).
  • B✗ deb: d on dial 1 (den), e on dial 2 (den), b on dial 3 (bob, fab).
  • D✗ men: m on dial 1 (mia), e on dial 2 (den), n on dial 3 (den).
  • E✗ bad: b on dial 1 (bob), a on dial 2 (fab), d on dial 3 (add).
Tip First letters of the 10 words: o, w, t, b, a, f, d, m. That’s 8, so dial 1 is full and has no h. hat is impossible.
8(Q18 in the original paper) Best deal

Every week I buy 12 cans of lemonade. The shop that I always buy them from sells individual cans for 80p each and packs of 4 cans for £2.60, so I buy three packs1 of cans.

Last week the shop had a special offer, as follows:

Individual cans – buy one, get another half price2

Pack of 4 cans – buy one pack, get a 5th can free3

I didn’t want more than 12 cans4, and I managed to get 12 cans for the lowest possible total price.

How much less than usual5 did I pay for my 12 cans last week?

  1. A20p
  2. B60p
  3. C80p
  4. D£1.00
  5. E£1.40

Reading it properly

  • 1Usual cost: 3 × £2.60 = £7.80.
  • 2Two single cans: 80p + 40p = £1.20 (60p a can).
  • 3A pack now gives 5 cans for £2.60 (52p a can): best value.
  • 4Exactly 12 cans. Three packs would give 15, too many.
  • 5Answer = usual price − offer price.

Working it out

  1. Price per can on offer:

    pack: £2.60 for 5 cans = 52p each (best)
    pair of singles: 80p + 40p = £1.20 for 2 = 60p each
    a lone single: 80p

  2. Use as many packs as fit in 12 cans: 2 packs = 10 cans. That leaves 2 cans, which is exactly one half-price pair.

  3. Check the other ways of making 12:

    1 pack (5) + 7 singles: 2.60 + 3 × 1.20 + 0.80 = £7.00
    12 singles: 6 × 1.20 = £7.20
    2 packs + a pair: 5.20 + 1.20 = £6.40 (cheapest) ✓
    £7.80 − £6.40 = £1.40
E£1.40 less: £6.40 (two packs with free cans, plus one half-price pair) instead of £7.80.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Forgets the free 5th can: 2 packs (8 cans) + 4 singles as two pairs = 5.20 + 2.40 = £7.60, only 20p less.
  • B✗ Buys all 12 as single-can pairs: 6 × £1.20 = £7.20, 60p less.
  • C✗ Uses only one pack: 2.60 + 7 singles (3 pairs + 1) = £7.00, 80p less.
  • D✗ Right cans (2 packs + 2 singles) but pays full price for both singles: 5.20 + 1.60 = £6.80. The second is half price.
Tip 2 packs = 10 cans for £5.20, plus a pair at 80p + 40p = £1.20. £6.40 against £7.80: £1.40.
9(Q19 in the original paper) Calendars & best price

I have booked myself, my wife and our three children1 into The Wavecrest Hotel, Southbourne, for seven nights from June 19th2. The hotel has sent us a ‘What’s on’ booklet, and we are particularly keen to see Oliver!, the advertisement for which is shown below:

The Southbourne Songsters present
OLIVER!
at the Pavilion Theatre
from Monday 3rd June to Saturday 22nd June3
Nightly at 7.30pm
Matinees Wednesdays & Saturdays at 3pm
Adults: £12 (Matinees £10)
Children: £6 (Matinees £5)
Family Ticket: £28 (all performances)4
(2 adults and 2 children)
Monday nights: £5 (all tickets)5

What is the least6 it could cost for us all to see Oliver! during our holiday in Southbourne?

  1. A£25
  2. B£28
  3. C£33
  4. D£34
  5. E£35

Reading it properly

  • 12 adults + 3 children = 5 tickets.
  • 2They are there from the 19th to the 26th.
  • 3Monday 3rd, so the 10th, 17th and 24th are Mondays and the 19th is a Wednesday. The show ends Saturday 22nd.
  • 4Covers 2 adults + 2 children only. The 3rd child needs their own ticket.
  • 5£5 each looks the cheapest, but is there a Monday they can actually go?
  • 6Cheapest possible way for all 5 to see it.

Working it out

  1. Which days can they actually go? Draw June:

    No Monday falls in the green days, so the £5 Monday deal is out.

  2. Price the best options for 5 people (2 adults, 3 children):

    5 separate matinee tickets: 2 × £10 + 3 × £5 = £35
    family ticket + 3rd child (evening): £28 + £6 = £34
    family ticket + 3rd child (matinee, Wed 19 or Sat 22): £28 + £5 = £33
    Tip The family ticket is £28 at any performance, so use it at a matinee, where the extra child ticket is only £5.
C£33: the £28 family ticket plus a £5 child matinee ticket (Wednesday 19th or Saturday 22nd).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 5 × £5 on a Monday night. But Monday 17th is before they arrive and Monday 24th is after the show has ended.
  • B✗ The family ticket covers only 2 adults and 2 children. The third child still needs a ticket.
  • D✗ Family ticket + a child’s evening ticket (£6). A matinee child ticket is £1 cheaper.
  • E✗ Five separate matinee tickets (2 × 10 + 3 × 5). The family ticket saves £2.
Tip They can go Wed 19 to Sat 22: no Monday. Family £28 + child matinee £5 = £33.
10(Q20 in the original paper) Patterns & parity

In computer technology, a bit can take two values: 0 or 1. A byte is composed of 8 bits.

I start with the following byte: 010001111

I choose two adjacent bits and change each of them to the opposite value2 (that is, a 0 becomes a 1, and a 1 becomes a 0). I repeat this procedure a number of times.3

Which one of the following bytes cannot4 be the result?

  1. A01001011
  2. B11111111
  3. C10101011
  4. D10111101
  5. E00001111

Reading it properly

  • 1Count its 1s: there are 4.
  • 2Every move flips two neighbouring bits at the same time.
  • 3Any number of moves, so don’t try to trace paths. Look for something that never changes.
  • 4Find the one that is impossible.

Working it out

  1. Look at what one move does to the number of 1s:

  2. The start has four 1s (even), so every result must have an even number of 1s. Count them:

    A 01001011 4 ✓
    B 11111111 8 ✓
    C 10101011 5 ✗ odd!
    D 10111101 6 ✓
    E 00001111 4 ✓

    Only C has an odd number of 1s C

  3. Check The even ones really can be reached. E: 01000111 → 00100111 → 00010111 → 00001111 (slide the 1 along). A: flip bits 5 and 6 once.
C10101011: it has five 1s, but every move keeps the number of 1s even.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Four 1s. Flipping bits 5 and 6 of 01000111 gives 01001011 in one move.
  • B✗ Eight 1s. Flip bits 1–2 (10000111), then 2–3 (11100111), then 4–5 (11111111).
  • D✗ Six 1s. Flip 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, then 6–7: 10000111 → 10110111 → 10111011 → 10111101.
  • E✗ Four 1s. Flip 2–3, 3–4, 4–5 to slide the first 1 along: 00001111.
Tip Each move changes the number of 1s by 2 or 0. Start has four (even). Only C has an odd number (five).
11(Q24 in the original paper) Timetables & scheduling

I had planned my day trip to the stately home very carefully, but then the bus was late. I won’t arrive until 11:30 am1 and will have to leave by 4:15 pm2. There were five things that I had wanted to do and I had written these down in the table below:

activitylength of timestart times
visit the museum55 minutes11 am or 2 pm3
visit the maze55 minutesevery half hour (at quarter past and quarter to the hour)
take the guided tour80 minutes1 pm or 3 pm4

Which activity should I miss out so that I can still do the other four6?

  1. Avisit the museum
  2. Bvisit the maze
  3. Ctake the guided tour
  4. Dwalk in the grounds
  5. Ehave lunch in the café

Reading it properly

  • 211:30 to 4:15 is 4 h 45 min = 285 minutes.
  • 3Only the 2 pm museum is left: 2:00 to 2:55.

    tour: 3 pm ends 4:20 (too late), so 1:00 → 2:20
    they overlap from 2:00 to 2:20 you must drop the museum or the tour
    Check Also, all five add up to 55 + 55 + 80 + 75 + 25 = 290 minutes, but there are only 285. Something has to go.

  2. Try dropping the museum. The tour fills 1:00–2:20. Lunch must start by 2:00, so it has to be before the tour (about 12:30–12:55).

  • B✗ Dropping the maze leaves the museum (2:00) and the tour (1:00–2:20) still clashing.
  • D✗ Dropping the walk still leaves the museum and tour clash.
  • E✗ Dropping lunch still leaves the museum and tour clash.
Tip Museum must be 2:00 (11:00 is gone). Tour must be 1:00 (3:00 ends 4:20). They overlap, so the answer is A or C. Without the tour it all fits.

Tip 32 a week. 9 weeks = 288 = 63 days. 12 left = three 4-days. 66 − 50 = 16.

George lives a 14-minute walk away from Beetown station2. He is going to the cinema in Exbay to watch a film that starts at 19:203. It will take him five minutes to walk from the station at Exbay to the cinema4. George wants to make sure that he will miss no more than the first five minutes5 of the film.

What is the latest time George should leave home6?

  1. A17:36
  2. B17:51
  3. C18:05
  4. D18:19
  5. E18:31

Reading it properly

  • 1Every 40 minutes. Beetown to Exbay always takes 8:02 − 7:25 = 37 minutes.
  • 2He gets on at Beetown, not Ayton.
  • 3Film at 19:20.
  • 4Exbay station → cinema: 5 minutes.
  • 5He can be in his seat by 19:25 at the latest. So off the train at Exbay by 19:20.
  • 6Latest train that works, then take off the walk.

Working it out

  1. Deadline at Exbay station:

    19:20 + 5 min late allowed = 19:25 at the cinema
    19:25 − 5 min walk = 19:20 at Exbay

  2. Trains reach Exbay at 8:02 and then every 40 minutes. The minutes go :02, :42, :22, :02 … (every 2 hours = 3 trains):

    … 18:02, 18:42, 19:22 …
    19:22 is 2 minutes too late take the 18:42 arrival

  3. That train leaves Beetown 37 minutes earlier, then take off the walk:

    18:42 − 37 min = 18:05 from Beetown
    18:05 − 14 min walk = 17:51
B17:51: walk 14 minutes to catch the 18:05 from Beetown, which reaches Exbay at 18:42.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Uses the Ayton time of the same train (17:50) instead of Beetown: 17:50 − 14 = 17:36.
  • C✗ 18:05 is when the train leaves Beetown. He still has a 14-minute walk to get there.
  • D✗ Adds the 14-minute walk (18:05 + 14) instead of taking it away.
  • E✗ Catches the 18:45, which reaches Exbay at 19:22. Walking 5 minutes, he reaches the cinema at 19:27 and misses 7 minutes.
Tip Must be at Exbay by 19:20. Arrivals …18:02, 18:42, 19:22. Take 18:42 → Beetown 18:05 → leave 17:51.

The table below shows the medal count by the top five teams in a recent Mini Olympics.

Each team calculated the proportion of their medals that were gold1.

Which one of the following charts, with appropriate labelling2, shows this proportion3 for each of the five teams?

  1. Achart A
  2. Bchart B
  3. Cchart C
  4. Dchart D
  5. Echart E

Reading it properly

  • 1Proportion = gold ÷ (gold + silver + bronze) for that team.
  • 2No numbers on the axes, so only the shape matters: the bar heights must be in the right proportion (from 0).
  • 3Work out the five fractions, then compare heights.

Working it out

  1. Total medals and the gold fraction for each team:

    Amber 8 + 7 + 6 = 21: 8/21 ≈ 0.38
    Emerald 5 + 10 + 6 = 21: 5/21 ≈ 0.24
    Garnet 6 + 6 + 10 = 22: 6/22 ≈ 0.27
    Jade 6 + 5 + 5 = 16: 6/16 = 0.375
    Sapphire 11 + 8 + 9 = 28: 11/28 ≈ 0.39

  2. Draw what the right chart should look like:

    Look for: Sapphire, Amber and Jade almost level at the top; Garnet just above Emerald; Emerald about 60% of Sapphire’s height (0.24 ÷ 0.39).

  3. Run through the charts:

    A: Garnet shorter than Emerald ✗
    B: these are the gold counts 8, 5, 6, 6, 11 ✗
    C: these are the total medals 21, 21, 22, 16, 28 ✗
    E: Emerald under half of Sapphire ✗
    D: matches, with each gridline worth 10% ✓ D
DChart D: bars of about 38%, 24%, 27%, 38% and 39% (gridlines 10% apart).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Garnet is drawn shorter than Emerald, but 6/22 ≈ 27% beats 5/21 ≈ 24%.
  • B✗ Shows the number of golds (8, 5, 6, 6, 11), not the proportion. Sapphire towers over the rest.
  • C✗ Shows each team’s total number of medals (21, 21, 22, 16, 28).
  • E✗ Right order, but Emerald’s bar is less than half of Sapphire’s; it should be about 60% of it. The heights only fit if the axis started at about 11%, not 0.
Tip Fractions ≈ 38, 24, 27, 38, 39%. Top three nearly level, Garnet a bit above Emerald, Emerald about ⅗ of Sapphire. Only D.
