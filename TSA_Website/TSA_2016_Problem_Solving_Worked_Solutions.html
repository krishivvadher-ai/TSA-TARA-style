1(Q2 in the original paper) Number puzzles

An unusual dartboard is used in a playground game:

In order to win the big prize, contestants must score exactly 50 with three darts1, all of which must be in different sectors2.

Which number must3 they hit with one of the three darts in order to score 50?

  1. A8
  2. B11
  3. C18
  4. D21
  5. E24

Reading it properly

  • 1Three darts, total exactly 50. No more, no less.
  • 2No repeats: three different numbers.
  • 3Find every way to make 50. The answer is the number that is in all of them.

Working it out

  1. Odd and even trick. 50 is even. The only odd numbers on the board are 11 and 21.

    odd: 11, 21
    even: 8, 18, 24, 30
    even total use no odd numbers or both odd numbers
    Tip One odd number + two even numbers always makes an odd total, so it can never be 50.

  2. Case 1: both odd numbers.

    11 + 21 = 32 need 50 − 32 = 18 ✓ (18 is on the board)

    Case 2: three even numbers from 8, 18, 24, 30.

    any trio with 30: smallest is 30 + 8 + 18 = 56 ✗ too big
    without 30: 8 + 18 + 24 = 50 ✓

  3. So there are exactly two winning throws:

    18 is in both 18
C18: the only two ways to make 50 are 8 + 18 + 24 and 11 + 18 + 21.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 8 is only in one way (8 + 18 + 24). You can also win with 11 + 18 + 21, which has no 8.
  • B✗ 11 is only in 11 + 18 + 21. The throw 8 + 18 + 24 wins without it.
  • D✗ 21 is only in 11 + 18 + 21. The throw 8 + 18 + 24 wins without it.
  • E✗ 24 is only in 8 + 18 + 24. The throw 11 + 18 + 21 wins without it.
Tip Even total, so 0 or 2 odd numbers: 11 + 21 + 18, or 8 + 18 + 24. 18 is in both.
2(Q6 in the original paper) Time zones

I have booked a flight from London to Auckland, which is in a time zone 12 hours ahead of GMT1. I am due to leave London at 17:30 GMT on 19 August2 and will arrive in Auckland at 06:15 on 21 August (local time)3. My journey includes a 1-hour stop in Los Angeles and a 1.5-hour stop in Hawaii4.

How many hours am I due to spend in the air5?

  1. A12¾
  2. B22¼
  3. C23¼
  4. D24¾
  5. E34¼

Reading it properly

  • 1Auckland clocks are 12 hours ahead. To turn Auckland time into GMT, take 12 hours off.
  • 2Start time, already in GMT.
  • 3This is Auckland time! Put it into GMT before you subtract.
  • 4Time on the ground: 1 + 1.5 = 2.5 hours.
  • 5Flying time only, so the stops come off.

Working it out

  1. Put both times on the same clock (GMT):

    arrive 06:15 on 21 Aug (Auckland) − 12 h 18:15 on 20 Aug GMT

  2. Whole trip, from 17:30 on the 19th to 18:15 on the 20th:

    17:30 → 17:30 next day = 24 h
    17:30 → 18:15 = 45 min
    total = 24 h 45 min = 24¾ h

  3. Take off the two stops:

    24¾ − 1 − 1½ = 22¼ hours
B22¼ hours: a 24¾-hour trip minus 2½ hours of stops.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 12¾ is just 17:30 to 06:15 on the clock. It ignores the dates and the time zone. Far too short for a flight to the other side of the world.
  • C✗ 23¼ takes off only the 1½-hour Hawaii stop. The 1-hour stop in Los Angeles is on the ground too.
  • D✗ 24¾ is the whole trip. It forgets to take off the 2½ hours of stops.
  • E✗ 34¼ ignores the time zone: 17:30 on the 19th to 06:15 on the 21st is 36¾ hours on the clocks, minus 2½. But Auckland is 12 hours ahead.
Tip 06:15 Auckland = 18:15 GMT the day before. 17:30 → 18:15 next day = 24¾ h. Minus 2½ h of stops = 22¼.

4(Q8 in the original paper) Nets & cubes

A cube is labelled with six letters and, when viewed in two positions1, looks like this:

Which one of the following nets could be folded2 to make the above cube?

  1. Anet A
  2. Bnet B
  3. Cnet C
  4. Dnet D
  5. Enet E

Reading it properly

  • 1The same cube twice. A is in both views, so build everything around A.
  • 2Right faces and letters pointing the right way.

Working it out

  1. View 1: B sits on top of A, and F is on A’s right. Everything is the right way up.

    View 2: A is upside down. Spin this view half a turn so that A stands up again:

    • D, on top, drops below A and turns upside down.
    • E, on the right, swings to A’s left and becomes the right way up, with its arms pointing at A.

  2. Now draw A with its four neighbours:

    So opposite pairs are A–C, B–D, E–F. Every net gets these pairs right, so it all comes down to which way the letters point. Four checks:

    • ① A’s head touches B.
    • ② F is on A’s right; E is on A’s left with its arms pointing at A.
    • ③ D is under A’s feet, upside down.
    • ④ E and F stand up on the sides, so their tops touch B.

  3. Test the nets:

    A: E and F stand upright beside B, so their tops point up the page, not at B ✗ (④)
    B: turn it so A is upright: E is now on A’s right, F on its left. Swapped ✗ (②)
    C: E’s arms point at C, away from A ✗ (②)
    E: A is above B with its feet on B ✗ (①)
    D: B on A’s head, D upside down under A, tops of E and F point at B ✓ D
    Tip Moving a square round a corner of a net turns it a quarter turn. That is why E and F lie on their sides in net D but stand upright in the map.
DNet D: every letter touches the right neighbours and points the right way.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ E and F sit beside B standing upright, so their tops point up the page, not at B. On the cube their tops touch B, so here they would end up lying on their sides.
  • B✗ With A turned upright, E is on A’s right and F on A’s left. View 1 shows F on A’s right, so this is the mirror image.
  • C✗ E’s arms point at C, away from A. View 2 shows E’s arms pointing at A.
  • E✗ A is above B with A’s feet touching B. On the cube B sits on A’s head.
Tip Spin view 2 so A is upright: B on A’s head, D upside down under A’s feet, E left and F right with their tops touching B. Only net D does all of that.
5(Q12 in the original paper) Logic & rotas

Three night security guards, Robert, Sheila and Tom, are assigned to guard a factory for five consecutive nights, Monday night to Friday night inclusive.

Two of the guards are to be on duty each night1. Tom must be on duty for four nights2 and Robert and Sheila are on duty for three nights each. Regulations do not allow any guard to work for more than two consecutive nights3.

If Robert is on duty on Friday night4, then the nights when Sheila is on duty are

  1. AMonday, Tuesday, Thursday.
  2. BMonday, Tuesday, Friday.
  3. CMonday, Wednesday, Thursday.
  4. DMonday, Wednesday, Friday.
  5. ETuesday, Wednesday, Friday.

Reading it properly

  • 13 guards, 2 on duty, so exactly one guard is off each night.
  • 2Tom has just one night off out of five.
  • 3Two in a row is fine. Three in a row is banned.
  • 4The extra clue that pins everything down.

Working it out

  1. Start with Tom: 4 nights out of 5 but never 3 in a row. His night off must split the week in the middle:

    Mon Tue | off | Thu Fri ✓
    off any other night 3 or 4 nights in a row ✗

  2. Wednesday: Tom is off, so Robert and Sheila both work.
    Friday: Tom works, and we are told Robert does. Friday is full.

  3. Robert has Wed and Fri, and needs one more night. Thursday would give Wed-Thu-Fri, three in a row ✗. So Sheila takes Thursday, and Robert’s extra night is Monday or Tuesday.

  4. Sheila now has Wed and Thu, and needs one more: Monday or Tuesday. Tuesday would give Tue-Wed-Thu ✗. So Sheila takes Monday and Robert takes Tuesday.

    Sheila: Monday, Wednesday, Thursday
CMonday, Wednesday, Thursday.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Sheila is off on Wednesday. But Wednesday is Tom’s night off, so Robert would be on his own.
  • B✗ Friday is already full with Tom and Robert. Sheila can’t work Friday (and she must work Wednesday).
  • D✗ Friday is already full with Tom and Robert, so Sheila can’t be there.
  • E✗ Friday is already full with Tom and Robert, so Sheila can’t be there.
Tip Tom off Wednesday (no 3 in a row), so R and S both work Wednesday. Friday is Tom + Robert. Robert can’t do Thursday (W-T-F), so Sheila does; Sheila can’t do Tuesday (T-W-T), so Monday.

Reading it properly

  • 1Me: 6, a child.
  • 2Saturday is peak (off-peak is only Monday to Friday). Use the Peak column only.
  • 3Mum works, so she is a full-price adult. She can’t go on a concession ticket.
  • 4Dad is unemployed: he counts for a concession ticket.
  • 5Sister: 3, under 4, so free.
  • 6Brother: 8, a child.
  • 7Grandma just needs a spectator ticket.
  • 8Grandpa is over 65: a senior citizen, so he counts for a concession ticket too.
  • 9Dad + Grandpa are the two concession adults!

  1. Who is who? 4 swimmers who can share the cheap ticket, plus Mum, Grandma and a free sister:

  2. The concession family ticket (2 adults + 2 children) for Dad, Grandpa, me and my brother:

    concession ticket £4.80
    Mum, adult £3.80
    Grandma, spectator £0.70
    sister, free
    total = £9.30

  3. Check the other ways quickly (all peak prices):

    everyone separately: 3.80 + 2.10 + 1.90 + 1.90 + 1.90 + 0.70 = £12.30
    normal family ticket (Mum, Dad, 2 boys) 9.50 + Grandpa 1.90 + 0.70 = £12.10
    concession for Dad + 2 boys 3.60 + Mum 3.80 + Grandpa 1.90 + 0.70 = £10.00
    £9.30 is the lowest ✓

  • D✗ £12.10 uses the normal family ticket (9.50) for Mum, Dad and the boys. It misses the concession ticket with Grandpa.
  • E✗ £12.30 is everyone paying separately, with no family ticket at all.
Tip Saturday = peak. Dad (unemployed) + Grandpa (senior) + the 2 boys = concession £4.80. Mum £3.80, Grandma £0.70, sister free: £9.30.
7(Q14 in the original paper) Shapes & patterns

The floor of the reception area of the head office of Stott and Walsh is tiled as shown below:

Which one of these tiles could not1 be used to replace a damaged tile on this floor2?

  1. Atile A
  2. Btile B
  3. Ctile C
  4. Dtile D
  5. Etile E

Reading it properly

  • 1Four of these tiles are somewhere on the floor. Find the one that isn’t.
  • 2A replacement must match a tile that is already there. You may turn a tile round, but you can’t flip it over (the pattern is only on top).

Working it out

  1. Every option has a slant that runs into a corner of the tile. T, L, H and + have no slants at all, and the O and A only have little cut-off slants between the middles of two edges. So only look at the S and the bottom of the W. Draw them tile by tile:

  2. Match the options:

    C: the long slant through the middle of the S ✓
    D: the S’s bend (straight line in, slant out), turned a quarter turn ✓
    A and E: the two bottom corners of the W, turned ✓
    B: straight line in, slant out… but it bends the other way

  3. Check B properly: turn it all four ways and compare with the S-bend tile.

    B is the S tile’s mirror image, and it appears nowhere else B
BTile B: it is a mirror image of the tile used in the S, so no turn of it fits the floor.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A is the bottom right corner of the W (straight line down, slant up), turned a quarter turn.
  • C✗ C is the long corner-to-corner slant in the middle of the S.
  • D✗ D is the S’s bend: a straight line into the middle and a slant to a corner, turned a quarter turn.
  • E✗ E is the bottom left corner of the W, turned upside down.
Tip Only S and W have slants. C, D come from the S; A, E from the W. B bends the opposite way to the S’s tiles, so it’s the mirror image.
8(Q18 in the original paper) Routes & networks

This diagram shows the roads that connect four towns and the distances involved.

I live in Essover. This afternoon I have to visit clients in Arford, Teechester and Yewton before returning to Essover1. It does not matter in which order2 I visit the clients.

What is the minimum distance3 I could travel this afternoon?

  1. A78 km
  2. B83 km
  3. C86 km
  4. D89 km
  5. E93 km

Reading it properly

  • 1A round trip: start and finish at Essover, calling at all three towns.
  • 2Try every order. With three towns there are only a few.
  • 3Shortest round trip.

Working it out

  1. A loop from Essover through the other three towns. Going round a loop backwards gives the same distance, so there are only 3 different loops:

    E → A → Y → T → E = 31 + 18 + 17 + 23 = 89
    E → A → T → Y → E = 31 + 25 + 17 + 20 = 93
    E → Y → A → T → E = 20 + 18 + 25 + 23 = 86
  2. Tip Both slow loops use the 31 km road. Avoiding it is the key.

  3. Could doubling back help? No: any repeated road just adds distance (e.g. E → Y → A → Y → T → E = 96).

    minimum = 86 km
C86 km: Essover, Yewton, Arford, Teechester, Essover (or the reverse).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 78 = 17 + 18 + 20 + 23, the four shortest roads. They don’t make a loop: you’d be stuck at Arford and have to come back along the 18 km road.
  • B✗ 83 is E → T → A → Y (66 km) plus the 17 km road from Yewton to Teechester. That ends at Teechester, not at home.
  • D✗ 89 is the loop E → A → Y → T → E, which uses the long 31 km road.
  • E✗ 93 is the loop E → A → T → Y → E: the 31 km and 25 km roads, the worst loop.
Tip Only three loops. Avoid the 31 km road: E–Y–A–T–E = 20 + 18 + 25 + 23 = 86.
9(Q19 in the original paper) Reading data

The following is from a report on road accidents in Scotland in 1998:

A total of 13,828 car users were injured in road accidents, representing 62% of all casualties. Of these people, a total of 2,386 were either fatally or seriously injured, 223 of whom died. Roads in built-up areas1 accounted for a little over half of all car-user casualties (53%: 7,389 out of 13,828). Presumably because average speeds are higher in non-built-up areas, they accounted for much higher percentages of the total numbers of car users who were fatally injured (84%: 187 out of 223)2 or were fatally or seriously injured (72%: 1,724 out of 2,386)3.

How many car users suffered serious, but not fatal,4 injuries in accidents on roads in Scotland in non-built-up areas5 in 1998?

  1. A1,537
  2. B1,724
  3. C2,199
  4. D6,439
  5. E7,389

Reading it properly

  • 1The 7,389 is for built-up roads: the wrong group.
  • 2187 = deaths on non-built-up roads.
  • 31,724 = deaths plus serious injuries on non-built-up roads.
  • 4Serious ones only. The deaths must come out.
  • 5Only the non-built-up roads (the faster, country roads).

Working it out

  1. Picture the groups. Each figure in the bracket is “part out of whole”:

  2. “Fatally or seriously injured” includes the people who died. Take them out:

    non-built-up, fatal or serious = 1,724
    non-built-up, fatal = 187
    serious, not fatal = 1,724 − 187 = 1,537
    Tip Ignore the percentages. They just describe the numbers in the brackets.
A1,537 = 1,724 (fatal or serious, non-built-up) − 187 (fatal, non-built-up).

Why not the others?

  • B✗ 1,724 still includes the 187 who died. The question wants serious but not fatal.
  • C✗ 2,199 = 2,386 − 187: it takes the non-built-up deaths away from the fatal-or-serious total for all roads.
  • D✗ 6,439 = 13,828 − 7,389: every car-user casualty on non-built-up roads, slight injuries included.
  • E✗ 7,389 is all the casualties on built-up roads.
Tip Non-built-up: fatal or serious 1,724, fatal 187. 1,724 − 187 = 1,537.

One of the exhibits in the Interactive Art Exhibition at the Hanson Gallery consists of 16 identical free-standing cubes1. Visitors to the exhibition are invited to handle these cubes and rearrange them to create their own work of art.

This is the arrangement that I have made:

What is the side view2 of my arrangement from the direction indicated by the arrow3?

  1. Aview A
  2. Bview B
  3. Cview C
  4. Dview D
  5. Eview E

Reading it properly

  • 116 cubes. “Free-standing” means every cube sits on the floor or on another cube, so some cubes are hidden underneath.
  • 2A flat outline: in each column you only see the tallest stack.
  • 3Stand where the arrow starts (top right) and look along it. Which end is on your left?

Working it out

  1. Look down on the arrangement from above and write the height of each stack on a plan drawn the same way round as the picture:

    2 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 16 cubes ✓

  2. The arrow looks along the coloured rows. Each row shows up as one column, as tall as its tallest stack:

    front row (bottom right of picture): 1
    next row: 2
    middle row with the 3-high stack: 3
    back row (top left): 2

  3. Which way round? Standing at the arrow and looking down-left, your left hand points to the front of the picture (bottom right). So the front row is on the left:

    1, 2, 3, 2 E
EView E: columns 1, 2, 3, 2 from left to right.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ A ends with a column of 1, but the back row has a stack of 2 (the cube top at the upper left of the picture, partly hidden behind the tall stack).
  • B✗ B (2, 3, 2, 1) is E back to front: the view from the opposite side, or left and right swapped.
  • C✗ C has a second column of only 1, but that row includes the 2-high stack just below the tall stack in the picture.
  • D✗ D has no column of height 1, but the front row is just one cube on its own.
Tip Plan + heights. Rows along the arrow: tallest 1, 2, 3, 2 from the front. The front is on your left, so 1-2-3-2: E.
11(Q24 in the original paper) Number puzzles

Last night I took part in a quiz night, and my team won first prize.

The quiz consisted of three rounds:

Round 1 was 20 easy questions worth 1 point each1;
Round 2 was 20 medium questions worth 2 points each;
Round 3 was 20 hard questions worth 5 points each.

Our winning score was 138 points2, having answered just nine questions incorrectly3.

The runners-up were annoyed because they had fewer incorrect answers, but fortunately for us (although a little embarrassing) we had more incorrect answers in Round 1 (the easy questions) than in either of the other two rounds4.

How many questions did we answer incorrectly in Round 15?

  1. A4
  2. B5
  3. C6
  4. D7
  5. E8

Reading it properly

  • 1Full marks = 20 × 1 + 20 × 2 + 20 × 5 = 20 + 40 + 100 = 160.
  • 2So they lost 160 − 138 = 22 points.
  • 39 wrong answers in total.
  • 4Round 1 wrong is bigger than Round 2 wrong and bigger than Round 3 wrong. This picks out the answer.
  • 5Wrong answers in Round 1.

Working it out

  1. Work with the points lost. A wrong answer loses 1, 2 or 5 points depending on the round. Call the wrong answers in each round R1, R2, R3:

    R1 + R2 + R3 = 9 (wrong answers)
    R1 + 2 × R2 + 5 × R3 = 22 (points lost: 160 − 138)

  2. Every wrong answer loses at least 1 point: that’s 9. The other 22 − 9 = 13 points come from the extra 1 point for each Round 2 miss and the extra 4 points for each Round 3 miss:

    R2 + 4 × R3 = 13

  3. Try R3 = 0, 1, 2, 3:

    R3 = 0 R2 = 13, more than 9 in total ✗
    R3 = 1 R2 = 9, R1 = −1 ✗
    R3 = 2 R2 = 5, R1 = 2 ✗ Round 1 is not the biggest
    R3 = 3 R2 = 1, R1 = 5 ✓
    Round 1 wrong = 5
    Trap! R3 = 2 also fits both totals (2 + 5 + 2 = 9 and 2 + 10 + 10 = 22). Only the “more wrong in Round 1” clue rules it out.
B5 wrong in Round 1 (with 1 in Round 2 and 3 in Round 3).

Why not the others?

  • A✗ With 4 wrong in Round 1, the other 5 misses must lose 18 points. 5 misses at 2 or 5 points can make 10, 13, 16, 19… never 18.
  • C✗ With 6 in Round 1, the other 3 misses must lose 16 points. 3 misses can make 6, 9, 12 or 15, never 16.
  • D✗ With 7 in Round 1, the other 2 misses must lose 15 points. 2 misses make 4, 7 or 10, never 15.
  • E✗ With 8 in Round 1, the last miss must lose 14 points. One miss loses 2 or 5, never 14.
Tip 160 − 138 = 22 lost over 9 misses. Extra over 1 each: R2 + 4·R3 = 13. R3 = 3, R2 = 1 gives R1 = 5, the only split where Round 1 is biggest.

    Othello gains 120 − 145 = 9180 − 4180 = 136 of a lap a day catches Hamlet every 36 days
    Hamlet gains 145 − 1120 = 8360 − 3360 = 172 of a lap a day catches Romeo every 72 days
    first time both happen together = every 72 days
BEvery 72 days: Othello catches Hamlet every 36 days and Hamlet catches Romeo every 72 days.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ Every 36 days Othello and Hamlet line up, but Romeo is somewhere else (only 0.3 of a lap round).
  • C✗ At 132 days the moons have done 6.6, 2.93 and 1.1 laps: three different places.
  • D✗ 180 is when Othello and Hamlet are both back at the start (LCM of 20 and 45). Romeo has done 1½ laps and is on the far side: in a straight line, but not in the order shown.
  • E✗ They are lined up at 216 days, but that is the third time: it happens every 72 days.
Tip Laps = days ÷ period. 72 days gives 3.6, 1.6 and 0.6 laps: the same 0.6 for all three. 36 fails (0.8, 0.8, 0.3).
24(Q49 in the original paper) Graphs & best deal

One of the options available to purchasers of furniture from Home Help Superstore is to hire one of the store’s self-drive delivery vans. They have four models, with different capacities, which can be hired for either 4 hours or 1 day.

What is the minimum distance2 for which it is cheaper to hire a 12 m³ van3 for a whole day, even if it could be returned within 4 hours4?

  1. A64 miles
  2. B72 miles
  3. C89 miles
  4. D92 miles
  5. E94 miles

Reading it properly

  • 1The first 40 miles are included: only miles after 40 cost 30p.
  • 2The first whole number of miles where the day hire is cheaper.
  • 3Only the two 12 m³ rows matter: £47 for 4 hours, £63 for a day, 30p per extra mile.
  • 4Same trip, two ways to pay: compare the 4-hour price with the day price.

Working it out

  1. The day costs £16 more up front:

    £63 − £47 = £16 = 1600p

  2. How many extra miles at 30p does £16 pay for?

    1600 ÷ 30 = 53⅓ extra miles
    40 + 53⅓ = 93⅓ miles both deals cost £63 here

  3. Check the whole numbers either side (the day deal still covers them, as 94 < 120):

    93 miles: 47 + 53 × 0.30 = 47 + 15.90 = £62.90 4 hours still cheaper
    94 miles: 47 + 54 × 0.30 = 47 + 16.20 = £63.20 day is cheaper ✓
    minimum = 94 miles
    Trap! Don’t forget the 40 included miles! 53⅓ on its own is far too low.
E94 miles: the 4-hour hire would cost £63.20, more than the £63 day hire.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ At 64 miles the 4-hour hire costs 47 + 24 × 30p = £54.20, much cheaper than £63.
  • B✗ At 72 miles the 4-hour hire costs 47 + 32 × 30p = £56.60, still cheaper.
  • C✗ 89 miles is the change-over for the 8 m³ van (£12 gap ÷ 25p = 48 extra miles). Wrong row.
  • D✗ 92 miles is the change-over for the 20 m³ van (£18 ÷ 35p ≈ 51.4 extra miles). For the 12 m³ van, 92 miles costs £62.60 for 4 hours, still under £63.
Tip £16 gap ÷ 30p = 53⅓ extra miles. 40 + 53⅓ = 93⅓, so the first whole number of miles where the day wins is 94.
25(Q50 in the original paper) Measures & rounding

Rolls of a particular wallpaper have a repeating pattern every 60 cm1 of their length. Each roll is 10 m long and 50 cm wide2. A wall 2.5 m high and 6.2 m wide3 is to be papered from ceiling to floor with single lengths of paper hung vertically (drops).

How many rolls of wallpaper will be needed so that the pattern on each drop matches the next4? (No joins can be made in one vertical drop.5)

  1. A3
  2. B4
  3. C5
  4. D6
  5. E7

Reading it properly

  • 1The pattern repeats every 60 cm down the roll.
  • 2Each roll: 10 m of paper, 0.5 m wide.
  • 3Each drop is 2.5 m long, and 6.2 m of wall must be covered.
  • 4Every drop must start at the same point in the pattern, so drops can only start every 60 cm along the roll.
  • 5Each drop is one piece. Offcuts too short for a drop are wasted.

Working it out

  1. How many drops?

    6.2 ÷ 0.5 = 12.4 12 is not enough, so 13 drops

  2. How much roll does each drop use? It is 2.5 m long, but the next drop must start at the same point in the pattern, so you skip to the next whole number of 60 cm steps:

    60, 120, 180, 240, 300 cm
    2.5 m drop uses 3 m of roll (0.5 m wasted)

  3. Cut up one roll:

    drops start at 0, 3 and 6 m; a 4th would need 9 m to 11.5 m, but the roll is only 10 m
    13 ÷ 3 = 4⅓ round up 5 rolls
C5 rolls: 13 drops, and each roll gives only 3 pattern-matched drops.

Why not the others?

  • A✗ 3 rolls only covers 12 drops if you get 4 drops a roll and round 12.4 down. Both are wrong: 13 drops, 3 per roll.
  • B✗ 4 rolls ignores the pattern: 4 plain drops of 2.5 m fit on a roll (13 ÷ 4 → 4). The same comes from wall area 15.5 m² ÷ 5 m² = 3.1 → 4. Both forget the waste.
  • D✗ 6 rolls would mean only about 2 drops per roll (e.g. allowing 3.6 m per drop) for 12 drops. But 3 m per drop is enough, so 3 drops fit.
  • E✗ 7 rolls is 13 drops at 2 per roll, from leaving an extra 60 cm repeat on every drop (3.6 m each). 3 m is already a whole number of repeats.
Tip 13 drops. Each uses 3 m (the first multiple of 60 cm that is at least 2.5 m), so 3 drops per 10 m roll. 13 ÷ 3 → 5 rolls.
